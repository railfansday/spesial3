Using BNPL for holiday shopping isn’t inherently bad, but there are risks, including sneaky fees and the ease of loan stacking across many providers.

Here’s how to minimize those risks and set yourself up for success this holiday season.

Brenton Harrison, a certified financial planner in Nashville, says many of his clients are turning to buy now, pay later in the “messy middle of life,” when everyday expenses are high and budgets are tight. This is especially true during the holidays.

“They have kids they're providing for, or they’re trying to go travel and see family,” Harrison says. “When those expenses pile up, it can be very attractive to be able to spread out payments.”

Since BNPL divides your total purchase into smaller installments, typically due every two weeks, it feels easier on your cash flow than having to pay all at once.

While Harrison is open to his clients using BNPL, he emphasizes how important it is to understand the terms before opting in. BNPL apps only require a soft credit pull and a short application. When you can apply for a loan in just a few clicks, it’s easy to miss when payments are due or whether they’ll include interest.

But if clients understand the terms and are certain they can make the payments, Harrison says BNPL's set repayment schedule can be a better option than more open-ended financing.

“With a credit card, [a holiday purchase] might be something that they buy over and over and over again, because that interest just continues to accrue over time, and they never pay it off,” he says.

Not all BNPL apps are created equal. I measure the top apps across multiple categories, but I weigh affordability the highest. My thinking is if you’re already strapped for cash, it’s a bad idea to pay extra just to break up the payments.

There are providers, like Affirm and PayPal, that don’t charge interest with a common pay-in-four term (your total purchase, divided into four installments). They also charge no fees, including no late fees for missed payments.

Compare that to apps, like Sezzle and Zip, that may charge an automatic service or origination fee with your first installment, as well as late fees.

Long-term BNPL financing, which can stretch months or even years, usually includes interest, expressed as an annual percentage rate or APR. This is a more expensive payment option than a zero-interest pay-in-four.

Pay close attention to your checkout screen to see what you’re offered. Are the installment amounts equal or is the first installment higher than the others? That may mean there’s a fee. Do you see an APR listed? That means you’re paying interest.

Harrison points out that just because a store partners with a BNPL company doesn’t mean that’s your only option. Download a different BNPL app, search for the store and see if you get access to better terms, like a 0% offer.

Taking out multiple loans with different BNPL providers is known as “loan stacking,” and it’s risky.

Because you’re juggling different payment amounts due on different days, it’s hard to keep track of what’s coming out of your bank account. This is especially true if you set up automatic payments, which most BNPL apps require. If you overdraw your checking account, your bank will likely charge a fee.

According to research published in August by the Federal Reserve, 11% percent of BNPL users had a BNPL payment trigger an overdraft or nonsufficient funds fee from their bank in the prior year. People with less savings were even more likely to pay fees.

Before applying for multiple BNPL loans, look at a typical month of expenses and ask if there’s extra room in your budget for those payments, Harrison says.

“If you’re just assuming that because you’re spreading it out that you’ll be able to pay it, that's where I think people can get in trouble,” he says. “They set it and forget it, and they don't realize, ‘Oh, I am in no better position to pay that next month than I am this month, or I have three of these going at the same time, and collectively, it's more than I can manage.’”

Most apps send payment reminders, so you can potentially reschedule the payment or change your payment method.

Still, it’s best to avoid the headache to begin with. If you’re feeling pressured to spend more this holiday season, communicate your limits clearly with loved ones, Harrison says, and avoid any loans you can’t comfortably repay.

“Think about the purchase itself, especially around the holidays,” he says. “There's a big difference between something that I can pay off over three months and something that I should have never been buying in the first place.”

#2939. Junk bonds are 'flashing yellow.' Watch these warning signs

Cracks are forming in the junk bond market as investors demand higher payouts for owning the market's riskiest debt. It isn't time to ditch high-yield bonds, but investors should pay attention to the warning signs.

High-yield bonds now yield 8.1%, up from 7.22% a month ago. The increase reflects a jump in yields across the curve as investors bake in more inflation from high energy prices and other pressures, including concern about the deficit — hitting nearly $2 trillion in the fiscal year that ended Sept. 30.

The high-yield market is also showing stress on the credit side with spreads recently widening to levels not seen since April, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Credit spreads are the difference in yield between the bonds and Treasurys of similar maturities. Wider spreads mean investors are demanding higher yields for holding corporate debt, viewing it as riskier.

Spreads are at 315 basis points in the overall high-yield market, higher than a year ago but still below levels in March when they reached 346 bps. One basis point equals one one-hundredth of a percent, or 0.01%.

The high-yield market consists of bonds rated BB+ by S&P and Fitch and Ba1 and under by Moody's. The lowest-rated cohort, CCC and below, has seen the most movement with spreads climbing dramatically over the past year to roughly 1,250 bps.

Right now, the high-yield market is "flashing yellow" but is "far from red," said Michael Arone, chief investment strategist at State Street Investment Management.

It makes sense that investors are demanding more compensation for taking on additional credit risk as borrowing costs rise, he said.

Yields are elevated across the board with the 10-year Treasury reaching its highest level since 2002 earlier in the week.

"The bigger question is whether this is simply a repricing of interest rate risk, or the beginning of a more fundamental reassessment of credit quality," Arone said.

He's in a wait-and-see mode since earnings are still growing, interest-coverage ratios remain good, and while default rates have ticked up some, he believes it is not concerning.

That said, the starting point in spreads is likely weighing on investors' psyche, since they are still low by historical standards.

"There's a small margin of error here, which I also think raises the anxiety level," Arone explained. "The compensation that investors are receiving for taking on this credit risk isn't overwhelming relative to history, and therefore subtle changes in credit spreads can be concerning."

While there may be concerns about part of the lower-rated market, the overall high-yield market is in good fundamental shape.

In fact, credit quality is at a record high, with BB bonds making up over 60% of the market compared with 38% prior to the global financial crisis, said Kelley Gerrity, a fixed income strategist at Morgan Stanley Investment Management.

"We've had higher-quality companies coming in, and with higher rates now, you also have more discipline from companies that are more indebted ... just because of the higher cost of capital, so that actually is creating a bit of a healthier picture as well," she said.

The lowest tier of high yield has always been more speculative, since the bonds have a higher default risk. Therefore, it's no surprise that they are the ones seeing the most widening in spreads, said Collin Martin, head of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research.

The overall move in high-yield spreads have been "orderly," he said. "Are there cracks forming? I think the cracks are logical cracks with the lowest-rated bonds and it's too early to say that it's spreading to the broad credit market."

In addition, fluctuations within the CCC-rated and lower spreads have been idiosyncratic, Gerrity added. Morgan Stanley recently broke down the spreads in the lowest cohort into two buckets: performing assets and non-performing assets, which are defined as spreads above 1,000 bps.

The non-performing spread to worst is 2,818 bps, while the performing bucket — the biggest piece of the CCC market — is 461 bps, she said.

"It is isolated," she said. "I'm not necessarily sure that that will go away anytime soon, but it doesn't tell the story of overall concern and flashing caution for credit markets right now."

For now, investors should remain selective within higher yield, Gerrity advised.

"We're looking for the best relative value opportunities, and we think that exists within that single-B cohort, and so we're leaning into that," she said.

Investors should be concerned if there is a steep widening in the broader high-yield market.

But there's scant evidence of stress in the BB cohort. Spreads in that group are at 194 bps, up from 179 bps a year ago — although the move hasn't been in a straight line.

"We're going to be focusing our attention on what are considered some stronger businesses," Martin said. "If we start to see the markets demand higher spreads there also, that's what we'll be looking at to see if risks are really rising."

It's also important to understand that the Federal Reserve is raising rates in a strong economic environment, said R.J. Gallo, chief investment officer of global fixed income at Federated Hermes. The central bank is concerned about an inflation problem amid high fossil fuel prices due to the Iran war, while growth has been stronger than expected, he noted.

"With growth being good as the reason that the Fed is hiking, then you wouldn't expect high yield to blow out because growth means revenues stay up, means cash flow and profitability stays up," he explained.

High yield becomes a disaster when the economy is heading into a sharp economic downturn, Gallo said.

"That's when spreads really widen out," he said. "But a recession is not the odds-on bet. Now, talk to me in six months if the Fed keeps hiking over and over, if oil prices stay high for longer, well, then maybe we will start to wonder."

Service and repair. Any interior or exterior mechanical, functional or cosmetic issues that were found are repaired, including paint, dents, oil changes and major engine work.

Inspection. Once repairs are completed, the car is inspected again to make sure everything looks and functions as it should.

Detailing. This includes deep interior, engine and exterior cleaning as well as any necessary leather refurbishing, bumper repair or wheel restoration.

Repositioning cruises move ships between seasonal destinations, often with longer sailings, fewer port stops and lower fares.

A repositioning cruise is a one-way sailing that moves a ship from one region to another. For example, many ships sail in the Mediterranean Sea during the summer and move to the Caribbean Sea for the winter, and that trip between the two is a repositioning cruise.

Repositioning cruises are most common in spring (roughly March to May) and fall (roughly September to November), though they can occur at other times of year.

Because ships are crossing oceans, these trips often include many days at sea. For some travelers, that’s a chance to relax and enjoy the ship’s onboard amenities. Sometimes, these sailings can also be cheaper than traditional cruises.

One key consideration for a repositioning cruise is that the embarkation and disembarkation ports will be different. This means you generally can’t buy a standard round-trip flight to and from the same city. Instead, you’ll need transportation to where the cruise starts and another way home from where it ends, which usually means two one-way flights (or one open-jaw ticket) unless you live near one of the ports. While these one-way sailings may be more affordable than traditional cruises, airfare costs may be higher.

This could be a great opportunity to redeem miles for your flight.

Another feature that sets these cruises apart is that stops along the way tend to include ports that aren’t traditionally part of regular itineraries. These might include islands like Bermuda, the Azores or the Canary Islands, and on some northern routes, Greenland.

Just keep in mind that the main purpose of these sailings is to get to a new region for the next season’s itineraries rather than make daily port stops. So if you’re someone who wants to visit as many places as possible on a cruise, a repositioning sailing may not be the ideal choice.

Repositioning cruises are often longer than standard cruises, commonly running two to three weeks compared with about a week for a typical sailing, which may appeal to travelers looking for an extended vacation. The experience is often more relaxed, with many days at sea and less of a rush to book shore excursions or visit important sites when off the ship.

If there are particular ships with certain amenities — like tattoo parlors or massive waterparks — that you really want to experience, keep in mind that not all ships reposition between regions. Some stay in certain regions year-round.

For ships that do change locations for the season, there are plenty of onboard activities to fill the time. You can enjoy shows, food and wine tastings, organized activities and time at the spa, pool or casino.

Many repositioning crossings are relatively smooth, and modern ships use stabilizers to reduce movement. But since these itineraries cross open ocean rather than staying closer to land, there can be more motion, especially on North Atlantic crossings in fall and winter.

If you tend to get seasick, this could be a dealbreaker. Even if a repositioning sailing is smooth, temperatures can be chilly on the open ocean, especially on northerly or spring and fall transatlantic routes, with strong winds that may make suntanning by the pool less appealing.

You can book one of these sailings in the same way you would book a traditional cruise. But if you’re searching for dates and itineraries on a cruise line’s website, they may be listed as transatlantic, transpacific or Panama Canal sailings rather than repositioning cruises, though some lines do use the term "repositioning." There’s even a website that specializes in rounding up these cruises: RepoCruises.com.

Keep an eye out for themed sailings that focus on topics like wine, theater or music, where fellow guests on the ship may share similar interests. Cruise lines often schedule them on repositioning cruises to spur demand.

If Palantir's share count stays near where it is now and it keeps the 49% net margin it had in the third quarter, earnings like that would take about $26 billion in 2030 sales -- about three times the $8.53 billion it expects for 2026.

For a company I'd expect to still grow profits more than 20% a year in 2030, that looks like a fair price to me.

A gain of around 10% a year isn't spectacular for a growth stock. But my forecast doesn't need Palantir to keep growing anywhere near its 2026 rate, and it already assumes investors pay under half of the current price-to-earnings ratio. If earnings rise more than 30% a year, $240 could turn out to be low.

The road there likely won't be smooth. Palantir shares dropped about 7% on Nov. 20, then jumped about 11% on Nov. 23.

Yet about 10% a year is enough for me to start. At roughly $161, I'd consider a small first buy and spread the rest over time.

The number I'd track most is U.S. commercial remaining deal value. If it keeps climbing just a few percent a quarter, I'd expect a 2030 price-to-earnings ratio closer to 30 than 50.

If there's one thing I'm qualified to assess as a longtime professional blogger, it's this: That was a lot of words to not really make any point or address the issue at hand. Which, come to think of it, might constitute a pretty loud statement about the state of affairs in Cleveland right about now.

Mitchell wasn't the only Cavalier searching for an answer to the issues that have characterized the club's fits-and-starts opening to the season. Peyton Watson, the swingman who came over from Denver in an offseason sign-and-trade intended to plug Cleveland's long-yawning hole on the wing, offered a somewhat more direct answer than the All-NBA scoring guard.

"I think if we knew the answer, we would just do it," Watson said in the locker room. "I think we're still searching for that. You know, I'm always trying to communicate with my teammates and try to help make their job easier and just be a guy that connects the team in a lot of ways. I think I try to just go out there and lead by example by playing as hard as I can every night. I just think that everybody has got to look inward and look themselves in the mirror and ask themselves, 'Am I giving it 100% every single night?' I think that if that's not the case, then we need to go to the drawing board and really, like I said, look in the mirror and evaluate ourselves."

That doesn't exactly seem like the most stirring vote of confidence for the competitive makeup of the Cavaliers, especially considering they've already gone through a few moments that would seem to invite such sober self-reflection.

Blowing a 29-point lead against the 76ers on opening night. Dropping both ends of a home-and-home to the post-injury-apocalypse Thunder. Getting boat-raced by the Knicks in the second half at home, an extension of the pain of the conference finals. Getting blown out by a Hornets team playing without Brandon Miller or Kon Knueppel. Getting out-executed down the stretch by a Grizzlies squad playing without Ty Jerome. And now, Monday's scattershot effort in Chicago.

It just feels like something's missing in Cleveland, some spark, some urgency — and it starts on the defensive end.

The last half-decade of success for the Cavs — a climb out of the post-LeBron basement that began with the Mobley/Allen/Darius Garland unit under J.B. Bickerstaff, and that continued after the trade for Mitchell made it a "core four" — had been predicated on consistently stout defenses, with Cleveland ranking in the top eight in points allowed per possession each season from 2021 through 2025. That began to shift when Atkinson's arrival supercharged the offense, and more firmly tilted after dealing for Harden; Cleveland posted a top-five offense after the trade deadline, but a below-average defense, and got routinely torched by the Knicks in the four-game conference finals sweep.

The Cavs now sit at 21st in defensive efficiency — on pace for their first bottom-10 finish on that end since 2020-21. They're conceding shots at the rim and from beyond the 3-point line more regularly, and they give up wide-open 3s at a bottom-10 rate. (Nearly half of Chicago's 3-point attempts on Monday came without a Cavalier defender within 6 feet of the shooter.) They don't force turnovers, and they're average-at-best at clearing the defensive glass and guarding without fouling. That's a recipe for underwhelming results, unless you can balance out that bottom-10 defense with a No. 1-with-a-bullet offense … which the Cavs, currently a good-but-not-overwhelming seventh in points scored per possession, don't have at the moment.

Maybe they will. Get Mobley healthy, get Harden — who followed up his 51-point Friday masterclass by shooting 5-for-14 from the floor with seven turnovers in Chicago — more consistently off the schneid, and get Watson (a career-low 48% on 2-pointers and 27% on 3s) thawed out, and maybe these Cavs have the ingredients they need to build on last season's trip to the final four and put themselves in position to make a bid to represent the East in the NBA Finals. They believe they have the talent to do that.

Right now, though, they feel like a team that's less than the sum of its parts — a group that's playing like it's just kind of going through the motions until the real season starts, rather than one that needs to earn its postseason spot in an East that's gotten more crowded at the top. And if the Cavs want to have a shot at being the last team standing come springtime, they need to start finding answers as fall turns to winter.

"You can't just turn it on in April or May," Cavs guard Sam Merrill told reporters. "I think building the right habits only makes it easier to play that way once you get to later in the season and in the playoffs. We continue to talk about it. That's got to continue to be our mindset."

Talking about it is one thing. Their next chance to do something about it comes Wednesday in New York. One bit of good news for Mitchell and Co., at least? The game won't air on ESPN.

#2807. What's ahead for AI financing after Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang departs

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's departure hasn't produced a broad shock to the credit market, but it is putting the company's role as a financier of artificial intelligence infrastructure under scrutiny.

Huang, who stepped down Thursday amid an espionage and adultery scandal, built Nvidia into an AI powerhouse in the 33 years since he co-founded the company. Chief financial officer Colette Kress has been named interim CEO as the company searches for a permanent chief executive.

Nvidia not only supplies its graphics processing units to hyperscalers and tech giants, it also provides financing to other companies to help the industry expand. That could all change.

"Although we expect the company to fulfill all of its previously announced commitments, we believe that going forward, it may be more difficult for the company to negotiate new multi-party investments among ecosystem partners at the same scale it has done up to this point," Goldman Sachs analyst James Schneider said in a note Thursday.

The news of Huang's resignation comes after investors were already concerned about the massive amount of debt being taken on to fund AI infrastructure.

Tech bond yields have risen and spreads have widened on credit default swaps, most notably in lower-rated names. Credit default swaps are contracts in which investors pay for protection in case the borrower can't repay its debt. Wider spreads indicate investors view the bonds as riskier.

On Friday, the yield on Nvidia's 4.5% bond due in 2031 sat at 5.717%, nearly 5% higher week to date. Bond yields move inversely to prices.

Tech stocks slumped Friday with the Nasdaq 100 closing down 1.2% as markets continued to digest the Nvidia news. Yields on hyperscaler bonds ticked slightly higher on Friday, signaling the broader concern about AI debt lingers.

It's the smaller AI companies that could ultimately see an impact if Nvidia makes any changes to its AI financing. The company had $36 billion in off-balance-sheet AI cloud agreements, or backstops, for neoclouds, according to its last quarterly financial filing in July.

Neoclouds CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN are all in Nvidia's portfolio. Their shares were down sharply Thursday.

CoreWeave, which has become the poster child for AI debt fears, saw its bond yields retreat after its quarterly earnings on Tuesday eased investor concerns. The yield on its 9.75% bond due in 2031 was 14.79% on Friday after climbing to 16.7% last week. The spread on its five-year CDS also dropped more than 17% week to date, although it remained above 1,000 — which is generally considered a sign of distress.

It remains unclear how Nvidia's support for those companies may change, said CreditSights analyst Andy Li.

"We don't know what the ultimate strategy for Nvidia is going to be coming out the other side," he said.

"However, I think directionally, just given the fact that Jensen was so crucial in selling the Nvidia story and being a very aggressive deal maker, it's likely that a lot of this creative deal-making and circular financing and equity and credit support is going to moderate," he added.

CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator told CNBC Wednesday, before Huang's departure, that Nvidia "is not one person."

"There are thousands of people over there that interface with thousands of people at my company every day. They integrate on the technology. They integrate on the physical infrastructure. They integrate on the networking," he said in an interview with "Squawk on the Street." "They have been a great partner for us for years. They will continue to be a great partner for us."

Bob Lang, founder and chief strategist at Explosive Options, doesn't see any big changes in terms of Nvidia's relevance or its ability to finance for now. As CFO, Kress was involved in the financing deals, and the search for a permanent CEO is going to take some time, he noted.

The real issue is just how much money the company will want to lend for AI financing, rather than, say using its hundreds of billions in free cash flow for other purposes, like share buybacks, capex, or acquisitions.

"Big companies like Nvidia and Meta, they're holding all the cards," he said. "When they decide that the game is over, that's it — it's going to be curtains for some of these smaller companies."

#2753. A trading strategy to ride out the Nvidia uncertainty and generate income in the process

Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation has sent shockwaves through the tech sector, dragging Nvidia's (NVDA) stock down roughly 30% since Oct. 6. The market despises uncertainty, and losing a visionary founder, particularly one without a well-telegraphed succession plan, makes it worse.

However, for opportunistic investors, this panic has made the stock cheaper (obviously) while options have become significantly more expensive. Trading in the $160s, Nvidia's valuation has taken a big haircut, yet its underlying business fundamentals remain largely intact. While Huang was the architect of Nvidia's AI dominance, the company's moat — superior GPUs, the CUDA software ecosystem, and networking expertise — does not walk out the door with him. The company's structural advantage in the global artificial intelligence rollout persists.

Buying good companies at a discount is investing 101, but the current market dynamics may do something else for those who use options.  Huang's departure guarantees that Nvidia will be operating under a cloud of transition for the foreseeable future. Executing a permanent CEO search while navigating global regulatory scrutiny takes time. Consequently, Nvidia's implied volatility (IV)—the market's pricing of expected future price swings — has spiked to highs not seen since the tariff tantrum in the first half of 2025, and this elevated IV is highly likely to persist for several months as the leadership drama slowly unfolds.

This prolonged period of high implied volatility can be a goldmine for option sellers, specifically those executing options overlays such as covered call strategies. When you buy NVDA shares at these depressed levels and simultaneously sell out-of-the-money call options against your position, the premiums you collect are directly tied to IV. Because fear and transition-related uncertainty have dramatically inflated these option prices, the income generated from selling covered calls is now significantly higher than usual.

This lowers the risk profile of holding the stock considerably over time. Consistently collecting these inflated premiums systematically lowers your overall cost basis in the underlying shares. If the stock trades sideways or recovers slowly during the CEO search, you are generating an outsized cash yield on a stagnant asset. If the stock experiences further downside turbulence, the heavy premium income acts as a substantial downside buffer, meaningfully reducing the strategy's overall risk over time.

By deploying a persistent covered call strategy, investors can leverage the exact fear driving the stock down to generate robust, risk-reducing income while maintaining exposure to the undeniable future of AI.
