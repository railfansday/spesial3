#7422. ChatGPT is about to show brands their mention count

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Marketers could soon find out how often ChatGPT users talk about them.

OpenAI is testing an analytics dashboard that counts how often a business shows up in ChatGPT’s answers and how many clicks those appearances send to its site. The tool sits inside a wider Business Hub that hasn’t launched yet. Juozas Kaziukėnas, founder of e-commerce research firm Marketplace Pulse, posted a screenshot on LinkedIn last week (November 6) after spotting a banner for the unreleased feature.

The banner asks businesses to “Claim your business in ChatGPT”, according to two separate sources with knowledge of its workings. Once claimed, a business can track its mentions and keep its products and business information current. Claiming also unlocks what OpenAI calls a business agent, which answers customer questions using contact the business supplies. Getting in requires proof of authority to represent the business. Kaziukėnas said in the post that connecting a Shopify store is one way to do that.

According to those sources, the dashboard logs impressions and clicks each day and plots them on a chart. It stops well short of what search marketers get from Google. Prompts stay hidden. So does user data and there’s no ranking against competitors. Kaziukėnas said he first saw traces of the feature in August.

Whatever OpenAI ships will land in a crowded market. Generative engine optimization vendors such as Profound and Adthena sell brands a read on their ChatGPT visibility, mostly by running prompts through the chatbot at scale and tracking what comes back. None of them has OpenAI’s own counts, though the beta dashboard also omits the prompts and competitor rankings those vendors sell. Kaziukėnas expects the two to work in tandem, with outside tools tracking prompts and OpenAI’s first-party data showing whether GEO strategies are working.

On paper the dashboard sits apart from ChatGPT ads as it deals in organic answers. Even so, OpenAI’s ads business has arguably the most to gain from its arrival. Once ChatGPT visibility shows up as a daily number, marketers will want that number to climb and the lever the company charges for it is a sponsored placement sitting next to the answer. Google ran the same play years ago. Business Profile listings put local businesses on its map. Search Console showed site owners how they performed in organic search and both gave Google’s sales teams an easier route to selling those businesses ads. OpenAI already has a self-serve ads manager and a measurement pixel. Business Hub fills in the organic side.

Ads in ChatGPT went into testing in the U.S. in February. By September OpenAI was claiming a $1 billion annualized run rate, reached in under 200 days, and in late October it said more than 100,000 advertisers had run campaigns across 89 countries. Sustaining that growth means signing up businesses that have never bought ChatGPT ads and a claimed listing gives OpenAI a line to them.

#7420. Campaign Trail: Wet Ones puts ‘hand butts’ on the streets

The Edgewell brand worked with BBH USA on an out-of-home campaign it hoped would cause some consumers to do a double take.

It’s cold and flu season, which means it's prime time for consumers to reach for antibacterial hand wipes. And while Wet Ones has been keeping hands clean consistently over the last half century, the Edgewell Personal Care brand had not released a major campaign in years.

Wet Ones decided to change that, seeking to create something fresh, bold and different that could capture the attention of a younger audience. The brand teamed with BBH USA and kept the brief loose: get the brand back out there and in conversations. The result, “Just For Hands,” launched last month and grew from a simple insight: More than 50% of consumers have used hand wipes on an intimate area, according to research commissioned by the brand.

“It was a bit surprising to us because it wasn't something that we had necessarily seen before,” said Nicole Harris, head of marketing for sun and skin at Edgewell. “We can do something with this. This is funny. How can we play on this?”

The insight about how consumers were using hand wipes inspired early rounds of work that played off inanimate objects like peaches. But BBH kept pushing and landed on an idea that the team calls “hand butts:” extreme close-ups of fingers that look like posteriors.

“We wanted to create a double take – that split second when you think you're seeing one thing, and then realize you're looking at something completely different,” said Yohan Daver, executive creative director at BBH USA.

“The craft had to make the illusion feel completely real, and by taking the idea across New York City at scale, we're turning one clever visual device into a much bigger cultural moment,” Daver added.

Created with photographer Ale Burset, a winner of more than 60 Cannes Lions awards, the campaign had a comprehensive out-of-home media plan including LinkNYC kiosks, transit shelters, digital newsstands, wild postings, bus wrapping and a Time Square takeover. Plus, interactive walls dispensed travel packs and individual wipes to pedestrians.

Originally, Wet Ones’ media plan included ads throughout the New York City subway system, where the variety of handheld surfaces like railings and straps provide a “torture test” for the product, Harris explained. That changed when the Metropolitan Transportation Authority vetoed the “Just For Hands” creative.

“When they refused us for being a little too risque, it was perfect,” Harris said. “It added juiciness to the story, but it also forced us to think quickly in terms of the other touch points. How do we want to evolve the media plan to still have the impact, but also take advantage of this from a storytelling perspective?”

Wet Ones’ PR team used the rejection as an angle to drive earned media. The brand also partnered with Kyle Cooke, of reality TV series “Summer House,” for a social stunt that brought a hand butt to celebrity gossip account DeuxMoi. Then, the “Just For Hands” campaign became the target of some friendly banter from Goodwipes, a brand of flushable wipes — not hand wipes — that isn’t a Wet Ones competitor.

“We [wanted] to get a response up in Times Square, so that was probably a record that I've seen, in my tenure for the company, for us to turn around a piece of creative and get it up within hours,” Harris said. That type of speed and agility was central to the “Just For Hands” campaign.

“We kicked it off at the end of August, so really fast turn out of the gate, but [it] led to amazing work,” Harris said. “We're getting great consumer response, but our retailers were really excited about it, too.”

#7414. How Lands’ End’s holiday pop-up feeds an always-on marketing strategy

Sarah Sylvester, who joined the retailer as CMO in March, digs into how a Holiday Tree Farm experience helps meet other imperatives.

Lands’ End is coming home for the holidays this year with an immersive pop-up in Chicago, the city where the Wisconsin-based lifestyle retailer was founded in 1963. The Holiday Tree Farm by Lands’ End is inspired by a line of archival copy that said that the retailer of apparel, outerwear and home furnishings provided “everything but the tree” at Christmastime.

“I just loved what that evoked, as far as being able to sum up how Lands’ End can help you for your holidays, because we literally have something for everybody… I feel like that really embodied what we were trying to accomplish,” CMO Sarah Sylvester said. “Lands’ End has done events in the past for holiday — how can you not? — but we really wanted to make sure that we were challenging everyone to think outside of the box.”

The holiday effort arrives as Lands’ End continues its turnaround efforts. The retailer and brand management firm WHP Global in April completed the creation of a joint venture that is intended to help those efforts. Lands’ End saw net revenue increase 2.7% year over year in Q2 2026 and made moves on marketing spend, customer acquisition and inventory that executives believe will better position it for the back half of the year.

The Holiday Tree Farm, open Nov. 6-8, will feature a variety of seasonally themed experiences, including a tote bag customization station, a vintage photo booth and a cozy lounge. The pop-up’s stations connect with the retailer’s key seasonal product categories — like outerwear and pajamas — so that the experience remains grounded in the brand.

The activation also includes a Holiday Drink Lodge in partnership with coffee chain La Colombe and a Sweater Swap in partnership with resale platform ThredUp that allows guests to exchange a used sweater for a new one.

Lands’ End’s prior collaborations with convenience store chain Wawa and lifestyle brand T&T Twin Talk were cited on a recent earnings call as marketing highlights that helped drive social media traffic and retail sales. But brand collaborations need more than just shared marketer interest, according to Sylvester.

“Even if you have really great brand names, if it doesn't make sense in some way, shape, or form with audience overlap or shared intent, it's hard to make it work,” the executive said. “We want to always ground it back to key product categories, either for us or them… how do you cut through the noise and do something that's fun and disruptive?”

Beyond other brands, Lands’ End is also partnering with Chicago native Lindi Presta and her Running the Runway run club for a “Miles & Monograms” activation and hosting a book reading and signing with local children’s author Lori Orlinsky. The goal of collaborating with such influencers is to help Lands’ End foster stronger local ties and extend the experience beyond Chicago.

“With these types of events, yes, you want to get broad awareness and great social content… even if you can't physically be at the pop-up in Chicago, you can still see and appreciate the content and enjoy it,” Sylvester said. “But specifically with Chicago, I love the localization aspect and how we're really trying to connect with the community.”

Lands’ End’s holiday pop-up arrives at a moment when marketers are reembracing experiential marketing after pandemic disruptions and amid a pendulum swing away from pay-to-play performance marketing toward IRL brand building. The retailer earlier this year teamed with Graduate by Hilton for a “Tote Tailgate Tour” that featured onsite experiences at three college towns.

“I started my career in experiential, so it holds a soft spot in my heart,” Sylvester said. “More than ever, people are craving things in real life and real experiences that they can participate in… There's just something nostalgic and comforting about when you can put down your phone and interact in real life. For Sylvester, who was appointed in March as Lands’ End’s first chief marketing officer in nearly a decade, experiential is a part of the brand’s always-on marketing — not just an opportunity for a few social posts and possibly PR-driven articles. That approach comes as some marketers are using experiential to help meet other needs around content creation and discoverability in the age of artificial intelligence.

“Experiences cost more than just paying for another paid social ad — how do we make that investment go as far as possible? Yes, it's about, hopefully, the thousands of people that will physically come through and interact with us, but also about creating content for holiday that we can use all season long,” the executive said.

In an increasingly fragmented consumer environment, uniting all aspects of the marketing mix — paid media, paid social, search, influencers, brand collaborations and experiences — around one core brand story is crucial, Sylvester explained.

“I feel like you need these three or four big moments a year of really being clear about who you are and what you stand for,” she said. “In marketing, you’ve got to do a little bit of all of it. It's always an ‘and.’”

#7412. WTF is the AI training opt-out policy token?

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This article is a WTF explainer, in which we break down media and marketing’s most confusing terms. More from the series →

Some of the largest AI tech companies — including Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI — have agreed to support a new mechanism that lets publishers opt out of having their content used to train AI models, in just a few lines of text.

The proposed standard, called a “policy token” for AI training, comes as publishers seek more control over how tech companies scrape and use their content. They’ve tried blocking AI bots, pursuing legal action and negotiating licensing deals.

At first glance, this AI training policy token appears to offer a straightforward fix. In reality, it highlights why the problem is so complicated.

The idea is that publishers can add a line to their robots.txt file — which websites use to tell web crawlers what content they are allowed to access — to express whether they want their content used for AI training.

Here’s what the policy token looks like, as written in robots.txt: User-Agent: __usage_ai-training

Below that, publishers can write “disallow” or “allow,” to show which crawlers can scrape and which can’t. In the “allow” line, publishers can also offer certain exceptions, such as letting crawlers scrape a press kit.

Typically, publishers need to list each crawler individually in robots.txt and give permission (or not) to crawl their sites. The new AI training policy token would allow publishers to do so in one step. Instead of managing separate instructions for every crawler, they can set broad preferences to participating AI and tech companies.

The proposal was written by Kevin Kelley, a technical lead for standards at Anthropic. Kelley submitted the proposal to the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), an internet standards development organization, on Oct. 30.

Technically, the standard is not official yet. The IETF launched an effort known as AI Preferences (AIPREF) to develop new standards for website owners to express preferences about how their content gets used for AI. Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI have all been involved in the group.

While the largest search and AI providers have agreed to support this policy token, it still needs to be deliberated on by the IETF. And the fact that these major companies have jumped to support this draft raises some concerns, said Alex Springer, technical lead at SPUR, a coalition of publishers working to develop AI standards.

“The IETF exists to allow for individuals to work in public to form consensus about the standards and protocols that make the internet work. The way that the platforms have approached this by announcing their unilateral support of a ‘standard’ that is an unreviewed draft is the antithesis of that,” he said. “They appear to have decided to unilaterally wield their collective weight in a concerted effort to force a new de facto standard in place, one that falls far short of what those content owners require.”

Anthropic says it is rolling out adoption of the signal now.

In a blog post, OpenAI said it would honor the policy token “in the coming months.” It noted that the proposed mechanism would not replace its existing crawler controls.

Google wrote in its own blog post that it aims to support this policy token in 2027. It noted that it builds on the options it already offers publishers, such as Google-Extended, a web crawler control implemented in 2023 that allows publishers to use robots.txt to restrict their content from being used to train Google’s Gemini AI models and ground responses in certain AI products.

Microsoft wrote in a blog post that not only would it comply with the AI training policy token but that it would also offer publishers the ability to opt out of AI grounding, or when crawlers scrape sites to find real-time information. These preferences will replace the existing Microsoft preferences “NoArchive” and “NoCache,” for AI training and grounding respectively, starting Feb. 1, 2027.

Meta didn’t go as far as to say it would honor the policy token, but Andy Stone, a Meta company spokesperson, wrote on X: “Meta, too, strongly support publishers’ ability to express preferences about how their content is used for foundational generative AI training. We believe creating a common signal that conveys such preferences to multiple providers at once is an important step. And we look forward to working with other AI companies on the standards and their implementation across the industry.”

The proposal may appeal to publishers seeking standardized controls. But, like robots.txt, it offers no way to ensure AI and tech companies comply with publishers’ preferences.

Robots.txt preferences are infamously more of a suggestion than an enforceable doctrine.

In fact, data suggests that web crawlers rarely even check publishers’ robots.txt files when scraping their sites. Crawlers operated by the four companies backing the shared opt-out token made roughly 1 billion requests over the past 30 days — but just 0.15% of those requests were for robots.txt files, according to data shared with Digiday by AI visibility measurement firm ScalePost, which tracked activity across more than 50 publisher properties.

Paul Bannister, chief strategy officer at Raptive, called the AI training policy token “window dressing.”

“I think this is useless and is marketing, and far, far, far too late… It does nothing and doesn’t solve the problem at all,” he said.

AI tech companies have a lot of reasons to want to appear to appease publishers. They’re fighting a number of copyright infringement lawsuits, pressure from government regulators, and proposed legislation targeting crawlers.

Four major AI tech companies have said they will honor this AI training policy token, making this one of the first proposed standards with that kind of support.

While it remains to be seen if this will be the case in practice, the policy token could be another layer publishers have at their disposal to protect their content from web crawlers, in addition to mechanisms like blocking crawlers at the CDN level or using tools like TollBit.

“Even though it doesn’t keep all the thieves out, you’d still put a lock on your front door because it keeps some of the thieves out… You do lots and lots of things to minimize the chances that your house gets broken into, but you cannot prevent it completely,” Bannister said. “Is it one other arrow to put in your quiver of about 1,000 things you need to do? Sure.”

Many publishers have been working hard to improve their AI visibility, and some are selling GEO products to brands to boost their visibility in LLMs. The last thing those publishers want is to jeopardize their mentions and citations in those tools.

Of the four companies backing the policy token, Microsoft was the only one to explicitly address its implications for AI visibility. If a publisher does disallow crawlers for AI grounding, “you will see reduced brand visibility in AI experiences,” reads Microsoft’s blog post.

Google’s official documentation states that using Google-Extended to opt out of certain AI training and grounding uses does not affect a publisher’s visibility in Google Search. But publishers argue that other opt-out mechanisms, such as Google’s Search generative AI control, present a tougher choice. Without the AI-specific click data they’re waiting on, publishers can’t yet accurately assess how much traffic and visibility they risk losing by opting out.

The AI training policy token doesn’t have anything to do with AI inference (or AI usage), which is when an AI model generates responses or performs tasks by retrieving or processing content from publishers’ websites in real time. This has become core to most of the recent AI licensing deals between publishers and tech companies.

And even if an AI crawler abides by publishers’ preferences expressed in robots.txt, it doesn’t mean the AI provider won’t be able to get that data elsewhere, such as from other crawlers, Bannister said.

“None of them have said, ‘We won’t buy data from third parties that scrape the content that don’t obey this,’ or ‘We won’t take open source crawls like Common Crawl,’ Bannister said. “They’re going to get all the data anyway, just through a different mechanism, and so it’s completely useless.”

This “opt in unless opted out” concept risks legitimizing a system where crawlers can presume they have permission for AI training, rather than needing explicit consent, he argued.

“The platforms are taking the stance that unless a preference to the contrary is expressed then content is fair game for AI training. We should not be normalizing this view and need to continue to work to maintain the concept that the usage of content should be determined by its owners and the result of their direct permission,” Springer said.

#7398. Why publishers must act now to protect content from AI scrapers

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Each technology wave brings the same promise: reach, relevance and a seat at the table. When the web arrived, publishers posted content to gain exposure, then spent a decade trying to claw back the revenue they gave away. When social media arrived, publishers threw open their archives to platforms that captured attention, data and advertising dollars. But each wave has left publishers with lower revenues, smaller newsrooms and a greater dependence on big tech platforms.

AI is the latest and potentially most disruptive wave. AI companies have spent four years crawling the web at industrial scale, ingesting decades of journalism to train models that answer questions directly instead of sending readers back to the source. Content that publishers built and funded is being taken without permission, compensation or attribution. AI companies have little incentive to pay while they can access publisher content for free.

Media leaders understand the risk. Yet research from digital analytics and customer engagement solutions provider Piano shows that fewer than 20% of the company’s 500 publisher customers use aggressive AI-bot blocking, while 30% use none at all. Without blocking, there’s no leverage to negotiate.

Some publishers worry that blocking AI means losing the chance to be discovered through AI. But less than 0.1% of publisher traffic comes from AI tools, according to data from Piano. Large language models keep users inside their own environments, so most readers never click through to the source. Anthropic sends back one visit for every 8,692 pages it scrapes; Perplexity, one for every 369. Until those numbers change, appearing in AI results doesn’t bring meaningful value.

Google’s pivot to AI Overviews has added to the pressure. Since AI Overviews launched in May 2024, clicks to publisher sites are down 49%. At Google I/O 2026 in May, Google unveiled a more AI-centric search experience that puts its chatbot-style AI Mode at the center of the search box, potentially pushing the traditional 10 blue links further down the page.

If organic search — currently 25% of traffic to Piano customers, according to internal benchmark data — continues to erode, the economic case for producing original content could collapse. Likewise, as long as AI companies can scrape content for free, they have no incentive to pay.

The path to compensation in the AI era runs through scarcity — and scarcity requires taking action like blocking bots and locking down content to create leverage. Then, publishers can negotiate.Here are four steps publishers can take now to create a path to compensation in the AI era.

Publishers can limit AI companies’ access to their content by declaring exclusions in robots.txt, blocking at the content delivery network (CDN) level, and locking paywalls to recognize only human visitors.

Some AI companies respect publisher rules, but many crawlers don’t. Piano’s analysis of Cloudflare data shows 70% of bot traffic is unverified. Perplexity has been documented circumventing blocking by impersonating Chrome browsers. Tavily openly admits its crawler skips a distinctive user agent specifically to dodge rules that let only Google crawl a site.

Basic bot-blocking may not be enough. Content protection ultimately means locking content for everyone, human or not, because the industry can’t reliably distinguish AI agents from human readers. One of the most secure approaches is requiring all readers to register, using server-side or CDN edge protection to block content from any unknown user.

An agentic commerce future in which AI systems pay publishers per crawl, per answer or per piece of content is real, but it’s not here yet. Platforms like TollBit and Pro Rata and standards like x402 and IAB’s Content Monetization Protocols are developing the frameworks. But no one is making meaningful money from bot marketplaces yet.

A defensive strategy won’t drive growth on its own. The same AI companies threatening publishers also enable valuable tools, including personalization at scale and AI-powered subscription monetization. The next generation of predictive systems will auto-optimize in real time, cutting manual work.

At the same time, an industry that spent 20 years unable to coordinate is starting to unify around how its content is used. The Standards for Publisher Usage Rights (SPUR) initiative is the clearest signal: 30 new members joined at WAN-IFRA’s World News Media Congress in June. The Real Simple Licensing Collective and the News Media Alliance are building similar infrastructure.

Publishers are also taking a harder line on how AI companies access their content. The Atlantic and People Inc. have put aggressive bot-blocking into practice. At the WAN-IFRA June conference, The New York Times’ publisher called on the industry to stop giving content away and demand fair terms.

Publishers should use AI where it creates value while retaining control over the content that powers these systems. The right move is the same one the best publishers made when subscriptions became necessary: be decisive, be clear about the value offered and build a business that doesn’t depend on someone else’s goodwill.The Publisher’s Content Protection Playbook from Piano covers practical guidance on blocking scrapers, how to know what content is worth before licensing it to AI companies, how to build leverage in AI licensing conversations and, how Piano protects content, creates personalized experiences and builds stronger reader relationships.

Here are five that marketing teams should be paying attention to.

Audiences are tiring of hyper-polished, solo "it-girl" glamour and gravitating toward content that feels like friends in a room. A candid carousel recapping a New York Fashion Week party reached nearly 740,000 accounts and drew more than 21,000 engagements, outperforming standard step-and-repeat coverage.

The action: Retire the solo interview. Try conversations and group chats where co-hosts or friends interview each other. Lightly produced and unscripted. Shift production focus from rigid scripts to creating environments that foster authentic interpersonal dynamics and natural conversational flow.

For one consumer tech brand, the most powerful emotional anchor was not a feature. It was relief from small, familiar anxieties: a dying battery, a package that might arrive while you are offline. Humorous, relatable posts about those moments drove strong reach and shares on Instagram and Facebook.

The action: Build bite-sized, highly visual scenarios that show the product solving an everyday problem. Focusing messaging on specific, common friction points rather than broad feature lists quickly builds emotional resonance and everyday relevance with your audience.

Many brands treat Stories as a place to repost the grid. But more often, short form content, like Instagram Stories, acts as a high-intent conversion powerhouse, generating massive click-through rates that can surpass follower reach.

The action: Rather than treating Stories as a secondary syndication feed, start treating them as optimized, high-velocity checkout funnels. Consider story-only offers and unique promo codes that turn intent into sales you can attribute, and design ephemeral content tailored for rapid action with clear, direct calls-to-action.

Relatable humor and conversational copywriting significantly outperform traditional feature-benefit structures in driving post engagement and click-through actions. We’re seeing meme-style copy ("My package: out for delivery / Me: I'm gonna be offline...") consistently outperforming feature-and-benefit announcements.

The action: Write the first three seconds of a Reel the way people actually post, using script-style overlays, familiar phrasing and emoji cues. Adopt native platform vernacular and visual formats to capture scroll attention and increase viewer retention.

Communities are starting to exhibit strong skepticism around highly marketed aesthetic features. For one technology brand, this was showing up in cases where customers felt basics were unaddressed: camera quality, software availability, stability. Sentiment scores alone would have missed the credibility gap.

The action: Show features in real-world moments where they are useful, not in isolated product shots. Frame product capabilities within everyday consumer workflows or challenges to demonstrate tangible value and maintain brand credibility.

Manscaped, the men's grooming brand, built a following by being very good at one kind of humor. But as the brand looked to expand into face and beard care, it needed to adapt its comedic approach to resonate beyond its below-the-waist roots.

It also helped validate the creative concept behind the brand's fall 2025 "Send Face Pics Instead" campaign, which contributed to Manscaped’s broader efforts to expand its reach and become the #3 brand in the U.S. Total Electric Shave Market.

All because cultural AI delivered rapid insights in a form leadership could confidently act on. The brand’s social director described it as "a quick postcard brief to make your point on why we need to act on something and to get the social teams heard."

None of these signals required more data. They required context: what is changing, why people are responding and what to do next. That’s what happens when you stop tracking the signals and start reading the room.

Echo builds on a strategic partnership between Ogilvy parent WPP and Creative Artists Agency and operationalizes The CAA Vault, the talent agency’s system for capturing, storing and licensing digital likenesses of its clients.

Echo looks to solve for marketer needs around content creation while protecting talent rights and intellectual property. Traditional productions of advertising featuring celebrities and athletes — a $25 billion-dollar annual global market, per Ogilvy — increasingly do not meet the needs of always-on, digital-first campaigns. At the same time, brands and their ambassadors are working to meet consumer demands around trust in an ecosystem heavy with deepfakes and other unauthorized artificial intelligence-generated content.

“Clients want more content, and [talent agencies] have high quality assets. How do we bridge those things together?” said Carol Reed, global chief innovation officer at Ogilvy. “It's not about creating new software to sell to people. It is more about how you solve the question around human AI likeness and making it scalable and repeatable for wide-scale use.”

Echo, commercially available across the WPP network this month, is powered by WPP Open, the holding company’s AI-powered operating system that is at the heart of its modernized offering. The workflow begins with capturing voice and video as part of the CAA Vault process. Ogilvy then takes those models and creates storyboard elements and creative assets that are turned into ads, all with approval from client and talent.

“I don't want people to feel like we're cutting corners. It's about ideally unlocking impossible things,” she said. “Being able to have more ideas come out of it and not just variations is really exciting to me… We can land more creative ideas in impossible situations.”

Trust is built into Echo in several ways. Generated assets are automatically checked against machine-readable talent usage terms and brand safety guidelines, with assets unable to be released without final sign-off through an approval portal. “I actually didn't know what was going to come out of that research. “This technology creates this convergence of acknowledging what could be possible if we all aligned our ducks, and so that takes a little bit of time to find the right use case,” Reed said. “We never want to feel like we're using AI or technology for the sake of using it.”

Amazon recently reported quarterly advertising revenue approaching $20 billion, with  Amazon Ads Agent – an AI-powered platform that’s likely to attract accusations of “black box” from detractors – a key part of its push to automate campaign planning, buying and optimization.

And with Amazon, the third pillar of adland’s triopoly, the key question for advertisers is whether they should hand over more control to a platform when they cannot independently verify how its existing auctions determine prices.

The FTC and 22 state attorneys general accuse Amazon of misleading advertisers about its Sponsored Products auctions.

Amazon presented these as generalized second-price auctions, where advertisers generally pay prices determined by competing bids rather than their maximum bid.

However, regulators allege Amazon introduced undisclosed reserve-price mechanisms that increased charges without corresponding competition, effectively pushing prices closer to first-price auctions.

According to the government body’s allegations, during the 2021 holiday season, Amazon increased average cost per click by 20%, with some advertisers seeing increases exceeding 200%. The complaint also alleges executives considered using reserve prices to address a $40 million revenue shortfall.

The allegations extend to how Amazon explained rising prices, i.e., whether Amazon misrepresented how its auctions worked and what advertisers were charged.

However, Amazon maintains that reserve prices reflect the value of its advertising placements, disputes the FTC’s interpretation, and argues that reserve pricing is not inherently unlawful.

For advertisers, the concern is straightforward: they can see what they paid, but cannot necessarily establish how that price was calculated.

The allegations come shortly after Amazon introduced new products designed to automate more advertising decisions.

At October’s unBoxed conference, Amazon unveiled Ads Agent and Full-Funnel Campaigns, allowing advertisers to set objectives, products, creative and budgets while its technology handles targeting, allocation and optimization.

Amazon says beta testing delivered up to 67% higher return on long-term ad spend and 29% lower costs per new-to-brand customer compared with manually configured campaigns. Those figures are company-reported.

The proposition is that advertisers can reduce manual campaign management and improve performance. However, greater automation also means relying more heavily on Amazon to explain how budgets are spent and why its systems make particular decisions.

While the FTC’s allegations concern Sponsored Products auctions, not Amazon’s new AI products, they still add weight to concerns raised about advertisers’ ability to inspect automated decisions, verify results, and understand fees.

Google’s ad tech antitrust case provides a precedent, although the conduct and markets differ.

A federal court found Google illegally monopolized publisher ad-serving and ad exchange markets – in a separate case, its search practices were also found to be monopolistic. The subsequent remedies stopped short of divestiture but imposed measures intended to improve competition and interoperability.

Both cases concern platforms whose control over advertising infrastructure limits customers’ ability to scrutinize how transactions work.

The difference is that Amazon is now asking advertisers to give its technology greater control over buying decisions, even as regulators challenge the transparency of its existing auction practices. These developments involve different businesses and issues, but they point to a broader accountability problem in digital advertising.

#7376. Synthetic audiences fall short: What marketers need to know

The AI-generated audience simulations may make it more difficult for brands to reach new audiences, writes RYA co-founder Mark Himmelsbach.

The following is a guest piece written by Mark Himmelsbach, co-founder of creative AI marketing tool RYA and advertising agency Episode Four. Opinions are the author’s own.

Artificial intelligence has proposed a novel technique for testing that’s cheaper, faster and whose proponents say is as accurate — perhaps even more so — as “real” research: synthetic audiences.

CMOs have good reasons to want this. Research is expensive, deadlines are short, marketers need to do even more with less and they increasingly need to justify their decisions using data. Synthetic audiences are simulated panels of customers, built from a set of data like customer records, interviews, demographic descriptions or combinations of those inputs.

These simulations can evaluate campaigns and explain their preferences in minutes. Marketers can then revise their work, ask again and repeat at a fraction of the cost of recruiting a human panel. But do synthetic audiences constitute high enough quality evidence?

The market seems to think so. In July, synthetic data company Simile raised $200 million at a $2 billion valuation, five months after announcing its series A. Even Adobe now lets marketers test creative, messaging and more against AI-generated customer profiles inside its Brand Intelligence product.

Now, properly identifying a target audience and codifying their preferences is hard enough. A simulation whose predictions hold up against real customer responses can help improve familiar campaigns. Indeed, for those teams who’ve successfully modeled their customers, synthetic audiences will save them considerable amounts of time and money.

There may even be additional creative possibilities on the margin — think more precise demographics, more targeted campaigns — but it basically helps you do more of the same.

A brand trying to acquire new customers, however, needs to understand people who haven’t yet made a decision to spend money with them. This is where simulations get tricky.

Synthetic audiences are being sold with that kind of solution in mind as well. Evidenza, for example, markets itself as “AI market research for impossible audiences,” as in impossible to do without AI.

But synthetic audiences still need evidence about the people they’re supposed to represent. Giving the model good evidence upon which to train is called grounding in the AI industry. For example, Bain reports that pairing simulated customers with a company’s own customer data substantially improved their overall performance.

But whose data are we talking about? What separates a useful synthetic audience from a generic simulation is data quality over time: whose experiences are represented, how recently were they observed, how relevant are they to the task at hand, and how often does new evidence get added to the training data?

A detailed snapshot of your existing customers may not actually provide much insight into potential customers who considered your brand but walked away. I’m reminded of the classic financial services industry disclosure: “past performance is not indicative of future results.”

Synthetic audiences – and synthetic data writ large – propose to use statistics and AI to create the data required to model those missed customers using external sources and observations. But again, part of the art of marketing is identifying trends and finding creative ways to stand out by doing something different that appeals to your target audience.

Whether or not AI can help humans do so is the trillion-dollar question.

Activision CMO Tyler Bahl, for example, asks what his team could do that only “Call of Duty” could do. His “stature, swagger, and scale” criteria express an ambition for the brand to delight their customers with singular, spirited ideas. A synthetic audience can help test how an idea might land, but can it decide what’s worth trying and how far to push it?

What’s more, there’s a self-reinforcing dynamic here that must be considered. Even if the data is high quality, and even if the model itself isn’t overtly biased, how might it eventually condition its users to test ideas likely to get accepted?

Constant scoring risks making a team more conservative and risk-averse rather than more creative and productive.

If campaigns need good “scores” to get approved, marketers will have every reason to learn what the panel rewards. This is commonly known as survivorship bias.

Under pressure, marketers may — consciously or not — begin incorporating those preferences before proposing something. Eventually, the process might invert: whereas the simulation was intended to improve creative decision-making, it may begin to influence creative decision-making, often regressing to the mean.

In the end, CMOs have to decide how much authority to give these tools, and leave their teams room to both use these models and pursue ideas the models wouldn’t have picked. They can thus derive considerable value from synthetic audiences while preserving the judgment and imagination that make their teams valuable. A few practical recommendations:

Cheaper, faster research should undoubtedly give creative teams more room to explore. Use simulation to test more ideas, learn from the results and improve the work. But marketing leaders must refrain from delegating too much of the process to the machines and remember to seek ways to ground their simulations in reality.

#7371. The case for and against AI token capping

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

When one of the world’s most valuable companies starts to get serious about AI waste, it’s time to pay attention. Apple is placing a hard cap of $200 on its engineers’ daily AI token use, and may reduce non-engineers’ token allowance to $25, Bloomberg reported last week (Nov. 4).

A handful of ad agencies have already embraced token capping this year — using AI software, or a bespoke tool, to prevent users from consuming more than a set amount of AI compute as the costs of AI transformation become clearer. Apple’s policy change indicates that more companies could follow that path, but there are tradeoffs involved in this strict approach to costs.

Agencies considering capping token usage must weigh up the risk they could douse their employees’ creativity by limiting their access to the tech.

AI costs — from tokens to licensing and subscriptions — are likely as cheap as they’ll ever be. While manageable today, they’re kept low because of the enormous capital expenditures of companies like Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. Eventually, those firms will want a return on those investments, meaning today’s prices might not be stable for long.

With that in mind, marketing agencies are building the infrastructure of AI accountability today, rather than wait for a crisis. Performance agency Roast, for example, has capped the amount of AI tokens its staffers can use since March.

“It’s up to the individual to use those tokens responsibly, and I think the benefit of that is then it’s making people think about what use cases they use tokens for,” John Campbell, head of innovation and AI at Roast, told Digiday.

The company allots a set amount of tokens to each employee within its Claude subscription, Campbell said. Higher individual caps can be provided at particularly busy times. He noted that the limit had stopped some users from relying on the tech for repetitive tasks that might be better delegated to non-AI software tools.

Video and image production generally represents the most token-intensive activity for creative agencies; media agencies experimenting with agentic tools for planning and buying must also grapple with runaway operating costs.

U.S. media agency PMG places a $50 daily limit on staff AI usage through its Alli4U portal. Staff that hit the cap more than once can be assigned a larger token budget, or receive advice on using different AI models, Dillon Larberg, consulting and strategy director, told Digiday in September. “That provides us a human in the loop moment to meet with teams or meet with individuals,” he said.

While agencies might still want to allow staff the flexibility to experiment and play with AI tools they can use tiered capping systems to provide teams with enough leeway to pilot something new, without burning up the budget.

“If you say you have a limit of $30, $25, $10 a day, you’ll find people become very creative in how they get this stuff done based on that constraint,” said Jonathan Whiteside, global executive vp at Dept, which caps team AI usage rather than individual costs.

“Constraints are sometimes useful for us to find new ways of working,” Whiteside added. “People start to think much more carefully about, ‘Well, do I actually need to use AI to do this?’”

AI costs, including the price of tokens, won’t stay low forever, so agencies should make hay while the sun’s shining. Intentionally cutting back before compute costs rise would be like abstaining from MoviePass or WeWork in the glorious days of zero interest rates. Agencies can chew through tokens while they develop the best uses for AI tech now, while it’s cheap to do so.

Some agencies use soft AI caps, rather than a hard limit. “When someone maxes out their spend for that tier, they show their work and their output to their department head,” explained Daniel Gilbert, CEO of Brainlabs, in September. He described the company’s approach as one that embraced “checkpoints” rather than caps. “The department head goes through the work with them, and if it’s good work, then they push them into the next tier.”

Such an approach preserves staff autonomy while keeping a lid on AI spending for agencies that want to to let a hundred flowers, or AI agents, bloom.

There are also means of nudging staff into cost-conscious AI decisions, without placing concrete limits on their usage. Gateway systems, or “gating,” refers to a tool which reroutes a user from a token-intensive AI model to a less powerful (and cheaper) model, if it’s better suited to a user’s project. If they’re using ChatGPT to draft their LinkedIn posts, they probably don’t need Astra, its latest and most powerful model.

Whiteside said that model selection is “a big factor” in keeping costs down, but that gating systems or staff training can provide a more appropriate guardrail than an individual token allowance.

#7347. The AI-or-Not Debate Is Advertising’s Most Pointless Argument

The most expensive finger in marketing belongs to a cartoon character in “Night in Color,” Dior’s new animated holiday short.

Last week, viewers noticed that character had one joint too many, along with some warped lettering and a lazy eye. Sure enough, the internet outrage machine started getting frothy again.

Online commenters concluded the film was made with generative AI, usually something that CMOs boast about. But it matters here because Jonathan Anderson, Dior’s creative director, had made a big thing about it not being AI, telling Women’s Wear Daily it was “all hand-drawn.”

The film itself is terrific, which is half the trouble. Sylvain Chomet directed a talking-animal fairy tale about Christian Dior and Marlene Dietrich, and Dior released a making-of to show off the craft. Then Ben Wild, who runs a film studio with Netflix and Disney on its client list, said two people who claim to have worked on it told him the motion was largely AI generated, and the furniture and the bees entirely so.

Dior has confirmed none of that. It hasn’t denied it either, and as of Friday three outlets had asked and heard nothing. Director Chomet has denied the use of generative AI but acknowledged on Friday its use to smooth out the animation. But the allegation is possibly the least interesting part of the story, because the argument we are now all having is a fairly ridiculous one.

Almost nothing you watch or read is AI free any more.

Your phone blends several frames before you have finished pressing the button. Photoshop will cheerfully invent the other half of a sky. AutoTune has been quietly rescuing pop vocals for more than 25 years. Your video captions are automatic and the dialogue in the edit suite was cleaned by machine learning. Every sentence of the column you are reading has been read by Claude to check for grammar and factual errors.

The monochrome disparity of real and artificial ended around 2024, a gradient that runs from a bit of noise reduction at one end to a creature imagined and drawn entirely by machine at the other, and anyone who tells you there is a clean line between these points is either selling something or full of shit.

The only sensible questions are where on the gradient you sit and whether you are willing to say so.

Does it matter if a bee was generated? Probably not. Does it matter if you said it wasn’t? Very much.

And if your brand DNA is forged from human creativity and hand-made, even more so.

There is a reason Dior wanted to claim it was hand-drawn. In a feed drowning in machine video, visible/fallible human effort has become a luxury cue. Prada, Porsche, Levi’s and Apple all went manual for the same reason. And Dior wants to be at the same luxurious, hand-drawn party. But a cue only works if it is expensive, and expensive takes time. Wild’s version is that Dior rushed into the film after hearing Prada had an animation legend on board, but you can’t speed up things like this. At least not without AI.

If Dior had said early on that a team of artists drew the keyframes and a machine helped with the gaps, I would not be writing this column. A few purists would have grumbled and everyone would have admired the bees and appreciated that Dior was a modern brand. Instead, it made a claim of pure manual labor, and that became the point of the ad—and its endemic flaw.

Luxury, more than any other category, cannot afford to be caught out. A mass brand can get away with it because nobody expects a Doritos ad to be drawn by monks. But luxury sells provenance first and product second, so provenance is the product, and a story about hands that turns out to be about graphics cards doesn’t just damage an ad’s reception, it invites people to revisit how they think about the brand.

Which is a shame. Because this was a refreshing idea from Anderson. And a lovely bit of advertising. But the bigger lesson is that this debate about AI or not AI is possibly the most pointless in all of advertising right now. And brands do well to avoid it completely. The work is the work. Boasting that it is “AI generated” or claiming that it is “entirely hand-made” are both pointless and increasingly impossible positions to adopt.
