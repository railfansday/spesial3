#7419. Why Liquid Death’s media chief is determined to break away from LiveRamp

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Liquid Death’s chief media officer could do without another branded hoodie this holiday season. A credible alternative to LiveRamp would go down far better.

Benoit Vatere has been looking for one since early August, when the beverage brand stopped the identity graph, after about 18 months of usage. The decision came weeks after Publicis Groupe’s plan to acquire LiveRamp was made public. In an interview with Digiday, Vatere said it was a simple choice: “I don’t want to train or have them see that super valuable information [for] anybody that I’m trying to get market share from.”

That fear stems from what LiveRamp learned about Liquid Death’s customers. The company’s job was to recognize the same people as they moved between screens and sites, so someone who saw an ad on a smart TV could be found again on a phone or a retailer’s site.

Over time that showed the brand which ads got people buying. Names and email addresses that get swept up in that tracking don’t concern Vatere since Publicis won’t see personal information. He’s far more concerned about the buying patterns. Publicis, after all, also works for brands Liquid Death competes with, like Evian and PepsiCo.

Vatere worries his patterns could end up in the data the holdco uses to train its AI tools and subsequently help make strategic calls for those rivals. Granted, the deal’s terms say that can’t happen. But Vatere expects them to change — terms and conditions get rewritten all the time, after all — not to mention the FTC’s watch over the deal lasts only five years.

“Let’s be clear about why Publicis bought LiveRamp,” continued Vatere. “They did it because there is value right? And the value is not just the revenue.”

That’s an indictment of every holdco with designs on an identity graph as it is of Publicis specifically. Vatere won’t hand his data to any company that also advises his competitors, which rules out the likes of Havas (still a LiveRamp user), WPP or Omnicom (which is building its own alternative).

In fact, Omnicom’s Acxiom has already tried its luck. Vatere sent it packing on the same grounds. “I’m not mad at Publicis. I just don’t want any of the holdcos to own it.”

That leaves independent providers, and Vatere isn’t naive about them either. Most are built with an acquisition and an exit in mind, so whichever one he picks will have to sign a contract that ends the relationship and deletes Liquid Death’s data the moment control changes hands.

“There should be a clause [that] if there’s change of ownership, change of control, contract out, right? Data deleted, like the whole thing, “ added Vatere.

He has a deadline of sorts. The LiveRamp contract runs out at the end of the year, and Liquid Death isn’t big enough to build its own graph. “I cannot do without it right now,” he said.

Chances are he won’t have to. The industry has no shortage of would-be LiveRamp replacements and plenty of them have already found their way to Vatere’s DMs. Earlier this week, the marketer posted his stance on LinkedIn after seeing reports that the holdco wasn’t concerned about competitors leaving now the deal has closed.

The replies read like a who’s who of the opportunists circling the fallout. One of them was LiveRamp’s CEO, said Vatere. He sent him a connection request, which was duly accepted. Nothing has come of it so far. Not much could. The only thing that would win him back is LiveRamp pulling out of the deal — and that ship has sailed.

Vatere is far from the only industry player to have taken this stance. LiveRamp rivals have been fielding calls and pitching for business since the deal was announced in the Spring. Both WPP and Omnicom have stopped working with the solution.

Advertisers have spent years investing heavily in cloud infrastructure, customer data platforms and first-party data infrastructure with the aim of retaining ownership of customer information as they activate media strategies. CMOs worried about maintaining that control may be at the front of the queue to speak to alternative providers.

“Advertising is an inherently zero-sum game. You need to beat your competitors to grow,” said Ruben Schreurs, CEO at Ebiquity. “Taking a uniform commoditized approach to communications or media is not a good strategy.”

“If your identity graph and your customer intelligence is your most important intellectual property, then you can’t choose infrastructure whose business model creates the appearance of conflicting incentives,” said Lai.

Schreurs argued, however, that working with LiveRamp alongside rivals won’t be the act that jeopardises a brand’s edge. “I do think it’s a little overplayed,” he said. “You can absolutely manage things in a way that if [your] agency also works for competing brands, that doesn’t necessarily have to be a problem. The same is true with LiveRamp.”

Whether any of this has a material impact on the trajectory of the business under Publicis remains uncertain. The likely answer is that Publicis wants LiveRamp’s data and connections for its own AI ambitions, combining LiveRamp’s clean room and data connectivity tech with the identity capabilities of Epsilon and the tech services of Publicis Sapient.

In any case, many of Vatere’s peers may find themselves boxed into to signing up with a Publicis-backed LiveRamp, or one of the rival holdco solutions, due to the market’s contraction in recent years.

“Clients absolutely care about conflict, how conflict is managed, but they have less and less optionality,” noted Schreurs. “There is now a big global three to choose from, so you’re going to work with a company that also works for competing brands. Then it becomes all about how you retain your competitive edge.”

Competitors and non-Publicis businesses are also a sliver of the business, accounting for roughly 5% of LiveRamp’s $800 million annual revenue.

A few defections at the edges are a price the holdco can afford to pay. Whether marketers like Vatere peel away in big enough numbers to change that sum remains an open question.

#7416. How TF is the grocery store a media channel?

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This WTF guide, sponsored by Grocery TV, explores how marketers are reimagining in-store retail media to engage consumers inside the grocery store.

Even with the growth of digital channels such as e-commerce, social and CTV, the physical store remains one of the most influential moments in the path to purchase.

According to Grocery TV’s 2026 In-Store Shopper Perception Report, 95% of grocery shoppers make at least half of their purchase decisions in-store. Even younger, in-demand audiences frequent physical retail locations, with 55% of Gen Z consumers shopping in-store at least once a week.

Despite this, marketers have historically treated the store more like a retail backdrop than a media channel. Media buyers, especially digital media buyers, have long defaulted to other channels that were easier to tap into. For digital buyers accustomed to unified inventory, real-time optimization and closed-loop attribution, in-store was the opposite: a fragmented, retailer-by-retailer negotiation with no standardized metrics.

Now, in-store can be bought through a single platform, targeted with the precision of digital and measured across the funnel, making it possible to plan against the store with more sophistication.

Investment is following, with eMarketer projecting that U.S. in-store retail media spending will cross the billion-dollar milestone to reach $1.15 billion by 2029.

“We’re seeing advertisers lean in, testing and learning what works now that the infrastructure and measurement capabilities are in place,” said Marlow Nickell, co-founder and CEO of Grocery TV. “There’s a lot of opportunity ahead as more retailers launch their in-store networks.”

In this WTF explainer guide, Digiday and Grocery TV examine how savvy brands, retailers and agencies are leveraging the grocery store as a media channel. The majority of brand choice is made in a retail environment — not on TV or online — and now is the time for nonendemic and CPG marketers alike to rethink how they use the grocery store to engage audiences.

In-store grocery media uses digital screens placed throughout grocery stores to reach shoppers during their shopping trips.

The channel combines the strengths of three other established media channels:  the reach and context of DOOH, the storytelling capabilities of video and the audience and commerce connection of retail media. These qualities come together inside the physical grocery environment, reaching consumers during their routine grocery shopping trip.

Nickell sees proximity to the purchase decision as the key distinction. “With digital out-of-home, it’s often more on-the-go media, like a billboard you’re driving past. In-store retail media is much closer to the point of purchase, where shoppers are making decisions and considering different products,” said Nickell.

Chelsea Monaco, svp, commerce media, Digitas North America, noted that this blending of channels makes classification tricky. “In-store retail media today can be categorized multiple ways, and it varies by the organization,” she said.

“The intersection of digital and in-store blurs in the retail media space, and the investment sources are so unique,” Monaco said. “I’ve typically seen it most categorized as shopper marketing, but investment source and where it sits in the organization depict the true categorization.”

Grocery trips offer more opportunities for product discovery than checking items off a list might suggest. Shoppers can encounter new products, respond to promotions and make unplanned decisions as they browse the aisles.

According to Grocery TV’s In-Store Shopper Perception 2026 report, 32% of shoppers enter the store without a list, and 78% make at least one impulse purchase during their trip.

In-store media can influence those decisions. Grocery TV research found that 62% of shoppers have purchased something directly after seeing it on an in-store screen. Screens are also shoppers’ top way to learn about sales, promotions and new products, allowing brands to directly engage shoppers at the point of purchase.

“When you ask people what they like most about grocery shopping, usually the answer is the discovery. They like the fact that they can see and hold things, and really learn about new products,” Nickell said. “That’s why we find shoppers are open to advertising. They’re already in a buying mindset.”

That opportunity is only growing. In 2026, shopper receptivity increased across every major store zone compared to Grocery TV’s 2023 study. Digital display formats saw an average 10-percentage-point increase in positive customer experience ratings, climbing from 41% to 51%. This suggests that in-store media can be relevant to a broad range of grocery shoppers.

Grocery retailers with in-store media networks run digital screens throughout the store, concentrated in high-traffic and high-dwell spots like entrances and checkout where shoppers pass through or wait. Brands match their creative to the products and audiences they want to reach, such as a financial services brand connecting with shoppers as they wait at checkout.

“The screen is physically there in the environment,” Nickell said. “You don’t have to worry about an ad being blocked or scrolled out of view in the same way you do online.”

Where screens appear also matters. Research from Grocery TV shows that shoppers are more receptive to screens in high-dwell areas, where they naturally spend time waiting, such as the pharmacy, deli and checkout. Shoppers rated these zones as 84% favorable, according to Grocery TV’s 2026 In-Store Shopper Perception Report.

Entrances are another opportunity, as shoppers are accustomed to seeing signage there. By contrast, placements that interrupt the shopping experience, such as cooler screens, tend to be less popular.

Effective in-store creative reflects both the product being advertised and the context in which shoppers encounter it. Brands can tailor messaging to the shopping environment, seasonal needs or specific audiences.

Nickell encourages brands to advertise across the entire store, with creative that connects to the shopping environment and gives shoppers a reason to engage beyond their planned purchases.

For instance, a snack brand might build a campaign around a game-day occasion, using creative that inspires shoppers to add something to their basket whether they see it entering the store or waiting at checkout. Education-forward content, such as recipes or tips for choosing an insurance carrier, can also give shoppers something useful to engage with during their visit.

Shoppers are 2.5 times more likely to consider a brand when an ad is contextually relevant to its environment, according to research from Grocery TV. The surrounding context can also shape how shoppers perceive an ad: 84% viewed end-cap ads featuring contextually relevant creative favorably or neutrally, compared with 79% for ads that were unrelated to the environment.

“As an industry, we often focus on the capability or the creative, but we forget about the overall experience,” Monaco said. “The biggest miss that I’ve seen is not connecting the entire shopper journey or that experience in how that shopper is going to engage with your brand in-store. What experience do you want that shopper to have with your brand at that touchpoint, and how does that fit in with all of the other omnichannel touchpoints they might experience?”

Brands getting started can adapt existing creative from channels such as CTV or social, making adjustments for the in-store environment. Text should be easy to read from a distance, and branding should be prominent. Over time, brands can develop creative specifically for in-store placements, incorporating context such as location, retailer and time of day.

As in-store media has evolved, audience targeting has become more sophisticated, bringing it closer to the strategies marketers already use in TV and digital campaigns. By combining geographic, demographic and behavioral signals with the context of the physical store, advertisers can reach shoppers close to the point of purchase with greater precision.

Geographic targeting can narrow a campaign by state, designated market area (DMA), ZIP code or point of interest. A brand looking to reach students and other campus communities, for example, could target grocery stores near universities.

Behavioral signals, such as purchase patterns and responsiveness to promotions, can help brands refine their audiences further. Demographic signals like age, income and household composition offer another layer. Brands can also prioritize stores that over-index for specific shopper groups, such as Hispanic consumers or older adults.

For example, a telecom brand promoted its international access plan across Grocery TV’s retailer-owned network in stores that over-index for Hispanic shoppers. The campaign drove a 27% lift in consideration for the brand when shopping for a wireless plan, a 15% lift in purchase intent and a 12% lift in unaided brand awareness.

As in-store media becomes easier to plan and buy, marketers have more ways to evaluate its impact, from brand awareness and consideration to incremental sales and repeat purchase. Yet measurement hasn’t always kept pace with the range of outcomes the channel can influence. A 2026 Grocery TV and IAB study found that, across four of five key outcomes, more marketers believe in-store media can drive those outcomes than they can currently measure those outcomes.

Part of the challenge is operational. Brands can struggle to align internal KPIs and standardize measurement across campaigns, while ownership of in-store media still sits largely with shopper marketing teams. The marketers getting the most from in-store take a broader approach, measuring beyond immediate sales and involving national media and brand teams.

Together, these approaches help advertisers assess performance across the funnel, using methods suited to the outcome they want to understand.

For example, a Grocery TV campaign for a financial health app measured brand impact against a control group, finding an 82% lift in brand rating, a 72% lift in recommendation and a 34% lift in brand awareness. The results show how in-store campaigns can be evaluated against objectives beyond immediate product sales.

“For banking, it makes a lot of sense to run ads at checkout. That’s when you’re pulling cards out of your wallet and making what, for most people, is the largest purchase of the week. Personal finances are already top of mind,” Nickell said.

Sales can be measured, too. In a campaign for Frito-Lay, Hy-Vee’s RedMedia and Grocery TV used a matched-store study across 103 stores, comparing test and control groups over a month. The campaign generated a 15% sales lift and a $7.94 return on ad spend.

Transaction-based measurement can also reveal effects beyond the advertised product. One national personal care brand working with Grocery TV saw a 15% sales lift for the advertised product, a 12% lift across complementary products and an 8% lift across its full brand portfolio.

The opportunity is to measure outcomes aligned with the campaign’s goals, including what happens after the initial purchase. In the IAB study, 75% of marketers who said they fully leverage in-store media measured post-purchase outcomes such as loyalty and repeat purchase, compared with 42% of those who said they underutilize the channel. Measuring beyond immediate sales can give marketers a fuller picture of the role in-store media plays in driving customer retention.

The grocery store can play a complementary role in an integrated plan, connecting brand messaging across channels with the moment shoppers are making purchase decisions. It starts with a clear business objective: What does the campaign need to achieve, and where could in-store contribute?

That may mean reconsidering how budgets are allocated across channels. “The ultimate question is what business outcome will drive the most success,” Monaco said. “We’ve seen cases where it might make sense to take budget out of underperforming digital and move it to in-store.”

A study from Grocery TV and MFour found that in-store media delivers an average of 49% incremental reach beyond existing media plans. Complementing campaigns on other channels with in-store media allows marketers to reduce overexposure by diversifying audiences. They can also maximize media dollars by driving new engagement and closing gaps left by other channels, such as display and online video.

Marketers don’t need to rethink their entire media mix to explore the channel, however. They can start with a defined objective, test in-store alongside other media and evaluate its incremental contribution to outcomes like brand lift or sales. Those findings can help determine where future investment will have the greatest impact.

In-store retail media is still developing as a full-funnel media-buying channel, giving early adopters an edge before it becomes a standard part of media planning. As Nickell noted, brands that start testing now can learn which creative works, how to measure success and how to make the case for investment internally. Those learnings take time to build. “The earlier you get started, the better,” Nickell said.

The campaign follows the recent launch of a holiday campaign from sister brand Gap that is centered around music. Taken together, these see the brands sticking to their respective marketing strengths as the holiday kicks into high gear.

Old Navy’s campaign was created with the brand’s in-house team and agency BBH USA. It was directed by duo Adam & Dave, with Academy Award-winner Autumn Durald Arkapaw as director of photography. The ad spotlights fashions starting at $7.99 including sequined dresses, matching pajamas and performance fleece and ends with the value-oriented tagline “More Merry For Your Money.”

“At Old Navy, we believe the holidays should be filled with more fun, more style and more possibilities for everyone. From festive fashion to gifts for the whole family, we’re making it easier to celebrate big without compromising,” said Zac Posen, chief creative officer for the brand in press materials.

The campaign is the first effort launched during the tenure of Old Navy CEO Michael Francis, who stepped into the role on Nov. 2, and the latest example of how the retailer has changed its marketing strategy after a recent sales slump.

“Our marketing has been totally rewired … to create more sharper price points and more value orientation with, of course, the Old Navy narrative that keeps our brands really relevant,” Gap Inc. CEO Richard Dickson said during a Goldman Sachs conference in September.

The Old Navy holiday effort sees the brand re-team with Hilton, who featured in a summer campaign that drove engagement but not traffic, Dickson said at the conference. The brand’s last holiday campaign was in 2024 and starred Jennifer Hudson with a cast of lesser-known characters.

Gap on Nov. 3 rolled out the latest iteration of its “Give Your Gift” campaign, highlighting individuality, creativity and community in an ad that brings together three choirs to sing Jackson 5 classic "I'll Be There.”

“The spirit of this campaign celebrates unity and togetherness. The campaign spans digital, social, video and owned channels and will be extended through immersive shopping experiences, including a multiday pop-up opening in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, this month.

Gap launched the “Give Your Gift” campaign in 2024 with an ad that featured an a cappella version of Janet Jackson’s “Together Again” and returned in 2025 with an ad centered on a cover of Miley Cyrus song “The Climb.”

Gap Inc. comparable sales were down 1% year over year in the second quarter of 2026, with Old Navy down 4% and dragging down overall results at a time when the company's flagship brand Gap saw comp sales increase 10%. The company will release its third quarter earnings on Nov. 19.

Creators are everywhere in marketing these days — everywhere except Christmas ads.

The first batch of festive campaigns, from John Lewis, SharkNinja, Ulta Beauty and Burberry among others, makes little for the industry’s favorite obsession. Despite leaning on creators throughout the rest of the year, marketers aren’t doing it for the holiday crunch. That job still belongs to celebrities.

Sephora has cast Seth Rogen as a man hopelessly out of his depth, rescued by one of the retailer’s Beauty Advisors before he can do any lasting damage. Over at Kay, Saturday Night Live’s Colin Jost is put through that most well-worn of Christmas tropes: the frantic hunt for the perfect present for someone special. Sports Direct roped in Nike to push the cliche a step further. Its ad follows an uncle wandering the aisles in search of something for his niece, blissfully unaware that a squad of Nike athletes is quietly steering him to the right choice. And Gucci has Mariah Carey — the industry version of the relative who gets passed from house to house every December. This year it’s the luxury brand’s turn to host her.

Why these brands do this year after year is no secret. Celebrities, particularly the calibre fronting this year’s crop, are the advertising equivalent of tinsel: nobody calls it innovative but a tree looks bare without it. For CMOs wary of how quickly an ad can set off the internet these days, that familiarity is a safety blanket. John Lewis is the latest case. Within a day of release, its spot had Piers Morgan calling it “possibly the worst advert I’ve ever seen for anything” and Mumsnet testing into the “ungrateful” grandchildren. Dame Kristin Scott Thomas, the ad’s star, escaped without a scratch.

“Part of the reason brands use celebrities in ads is because they’re borrowing fame,” said Lynne Deason, head of creative excellence at Kantar. “Christmas is this mass market moment where brands are trying to reach everyone and so often brands are looking for a celebrity that everyone will recognize rather than maybe a particular audience.”

None of which is a knock on creators. Indeed, marketers have spent the year handing creators budget, stage time and, increasingly, the lead role in global campaigns. Christmas ads, however, play by older rules.  It is the most scrutinized piece of work a brand releases all year, with the biggest budget and the most senior sign-off, and its conventions have barely changed in a decade: a big film, a broad audience and, more often than not, a famous face. Striking to that formula keeps a brand in line with every rival in the ad break. Leaving it puts the brand out on its own and few CMOs want to be the one doing that in December.

“That’s perhaps harder with creators because people tend to tap into them for particular interests,” said Deason. “Obviously there are some who are becoming very famous in their own right with mass audiences but there are fewer of those. And Chrismtas is such a competitive time.”

Attitude toward it, however, is divided, with only 51% of conversations positive. The best ideas are rooted in a brand’s DNA, and that’s something I always encourage our team to push for.”

The most telling thing about AI-generated ads is how many of them exclude disclosures — suggesting campaigns either know they’re unpopular, are deliberately misleading voters, or both.

No lie: The 2026 political ad outlay may have been the dumbest money spent in the history of advertising. Even so, media and tech platforms will be glad to take political money from the same undiscriminating sources again in two years’ time. It’s kind of a travesty.
