#7401. OpenAI’s new business agents give brands a free way into the conversation it’s also selling as an ad

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

The sponsored link had a good run. Its successor might talk back.

OpenAI certainly thinks so. Businesses can now claim a profile in ChatGPT through a new Business Hub, get verified and feed in product and company details. In the U.S. (for now), e-commerce and online services businesses can go a step further and publish a business agent – a separately labelled chat that speaks for the brand once a user wants to dig into a product. OpenAI’s vp of monetization Benji Shomair declined to share a timeline for further rollout.

He did, however, clarify that the business agent is the free, organic way into a format Digiday reported on in September, when OpenAI started testing it as a paid ad.

“Think of the business agent as the organic experience,” Shomair said during a press briefing. “The sponsored agent is the paid experience, but the agent that sits behind it should be the same.”

So, OpenAI has built two routes into the same conversation for brands, and only one of them costs money. One through ChatGPT’s organic recommendations, and the other via paid placements.

Shomair said early testing of sponsored agents had shown “promising results,” particularly in the depth of conversations between consumers and businesses.

“When a consumer chooses to engage more deeply with our business, there’s a high level of intent there,” he said.

Shomair described typical consumer use cases as working through product choices, weighing up size, colour and price before exploring related purchases. For businesses, that could result in turning an initial product enquiry into a bigger basket, and ultimately a deeper customer relationship.

When the shopper is ready to pay, the agent waves them off to the brand’s own site, because “it’s up to the business on how they want to close the transaction,” said Shomair.

Whatever got said before that point stays with OpenAI. Brands hoping to read back their agents’ transcripts get themes instead.

“The only insight provided today are the ones we sort of shared there initially, which are the themes of the conversations where you’re appearing,” Shomair said.

OpenAI keeps hold of the agent itself too. It’s built from the instructions and product feed a business supplies with no room for an in-house version. “We aren’t allowing folks to bring their own agents,” said Shomair.

The rest of the Business Hub sits under Insights, the the analytics dashboard-type product that Digiday reported on this week — the beta shows mentions, views and website clicks, plus discovery topics.

“Those are common themes in conversations where ChatGPT cites the business’s site,” an OpenAI spokesperson confirmed to Digiday. “Businesses don’t see individual prompts or chats. Share of voice isn’t part of this beta.”

How mentions are tracked depends on the type of business. For local businesses, a mention counts when ChatGPT shows a result for that location. For an online business, it counts when ChatGPT references the business’s website.

OpenAI declined to say how long mentions are tracked. An ad exec with access to Insights within the Business Hub did share that the chart within the feature shows everything within the past seven or 28 days.

Accuracast’s group CEO Farhad Divecha said that his team has been reliant on tools like Peec,.AI, Otterly.AI or Semrush to guesstimate brand visibility on ChatGPT as well as other AI platforms. But he noted that they are all using approximations from Google search data, third-party traffic estimators and their own data modeling tools, and none have access to original data from the source.

“Now that OpenAI is making their own mentions data available, we’re very interested to see how far off these tools were from the actual data from ChatGPT, and whether these tools will be able to adapt and incorporate the OpenAI data,” Divecha said. “If their estimation data was wildly erroneous, that will make us rethink paying for those tools.”

The implications extend beyond the accuracy of individual tools. OpenAI’s decision to share its own data could also shift the balance of power in the growing AI visibility industry. As Sonata Insights’ founder and chief analyst Debra Aho Williamson pointed out, the entire AI visibility (AEO/GEO) industry is built on third-party research (and some guesswork) about what helps a brand show up in an answer.

“The fact that OpenAI will now share insights itself will take some of the power away from the third-party companies,” she said. “But OpenAI isn’t going to share everything it knows, and ChatGPT is only one of several AI platforms, so demand for what the AI visibility companies offer will remain strong.”

Business Hub and agents are free for now but Shomair didn’t rule out a paid model in the future.

“In terms of where they develop in the future, I think it’ll depend on the directions that they go,” he said.

What none of it buys is a leg-up in ChatGPT’s recommendations. The core product needs to“maintain objectivity,” said Shomair, claiming a business “should not have an impact on how frequently it appears.”

That doesn’t mean the information is irrelevant to visibility though. Shomair said it could help ChatGPT surface a business in more relevant conversations since the platform would have a clearer picture of what that business offers.

#7398. Future of TV Briefing: Inside creator Grace Wells’s 100-hour branded video production process

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This Future of TV Briefing covers the latest in streaming and TV for Digiday+ members and is distributed over email every Wednesday at 10 a.m. ET. More from the series →

This week’s Future of TV Briefing looks at creator Grace Wells’s full production process, from initial brief to final delivery.

Picture the stereotypical sponsored video produced by a creator. You may be imagining someone holding up a product to their camera, hand behind the product to prevent their camera’s autofocus from blurring out said product, reciting a script about the product’s role in their everyday life and thanking the brand for partnering with them on this video.

Grace Wells (@gracewellsphoto) disproves that stereotype.

A creator who has attracted millions of followers across TikTok and Instagram, Wells represents how creator-produced branded videos have leveled up in production value. She spends, on average, 100 hours producing videos for brands and knocks out two to three branded videos per month. During Digiday’s recent Creator Strategies virtual event, she broke down her full production process, from initial brief to final delivery.

“It’s a very time-intensive process. I’m very big on pre-production, and that’s a question that I get a lot: is how much planning actually goes into these. There’s a lot of hours before I even get into the studio to film,” Wells said.

The first step in that process is joining a briefing call with the brand. Other creators may be satisfied with receiving a one-sheet telling them what the client is looking for. But Wells wants to have a conversation to understand the brand’s objective, its target audience and whether the video she is producing is meant to be tied to a larger marketing effort in case there are pre-existing brand assets or visual elements to incorporate.

“Because ultimately, I’m trying to create an asset that feels equally like me and like the brand, and that can be kind of a delicate balance to strike sometimes. Depending on how fleshed out the brief is and how much other content is being sourced for this particular campaign, I decide how many of those pre-existing elements I want to pull into my storyboarding and my ideation process,” said Wells.

Post-briefing call, Wells moves into her pre-production process. This stage can take roughly 30 hours. A pillar of Wells’s pre-production process is storyboarding. Every. Single. Shot.

“The perspective of the shot. How long that shot is going to last. Is there going to be voiceover or sound effects or text callouts that are happening?” said Wells.

She has adopted this extensive storyboarding process for two main reasons. First, it allows the brand to literally see what she intends to film and how she plans to edit it all together. Second, it allows Wells to head into filming on solid ground with minimal risk of the brand being dissatisfied when she hands in the final video and asking for reshoots.

“That can really mess me up and kind of create a domino effect in my workflow,” said Wells.

That domino effect is important to avoid because here’s the most crucial part of Wells’s production process to be aware of: She does everything by herself. That includes making custom newspapers in Photoshop (as she did for this recent video for Bayer), building sets, setting up lighting, prepping and styling food products and, of course, filming. Actually she effectively films two videos because the videos she produces for brands include the product showcase video as well as the behind the scenes of Wells making the product showcase video.

“The bulk of my time is spent on set filming, just because I am one person with two hands,” said Wells. She added, “every time I’m setting up a shot, I’m actually setting up two shots because I have the shot that I’m filming and then I have the behind the scenes that’s happening.”

A perk of filming solo is that it affords Wells the flexibility to film for as long as she wants, to take her time to experiment with shots and even to edit the video as she films it.

“I can film a shot then I can throw it into Premiere and edit it and that might inform decisions that I make about the following shot that I’m going to film,” Wells said.

Unsurprisingly then, post-production is the shortest stage of her process, typically taking around 20 hours to complete. She has her storyboards that effectively serve as a paper edit, so editing the video is a bit like coloring by numbers.

“Music is the very first thing that I choose, even before I’m storyboarding. So even decisions like that have been made at this point a very long time ago. And so it really is just putting the finishing touches on things,” she said.

This pre-production-heavy process helps Wells to avoid what can often be the bane of any creative client work: revisions.

“If there are any revisions, it’ll be simple things like, ‘Oh, actually let’s change the wording of this text,’ which is a very simple change, or ‘Let’s punch in on this so we can see details a little bit more.’ So I would say generally it’s like maybe three to four rounds of very small changes.”

This week’s Media Briefing looks at how the IAB’s months-long effort to standardize AI attribution ran into a roadblock: the lack of visibility into what happens inside AI platforms.

The Interactive Advertising Bureau spent months trying to determine how the industry should attribute and credit conversions influenced by AI. But it ran into a problem: you can’t standardize what you can’t see.

Digiday first reported in August that the IAB was developing an AI attribution framework to standardize measuring and crediting AI’s role in conversions, and the signals to quantify AI’s influence on purchase decisions. The IAB’s blueprint offers a framework for understanding AI’s role in driving business outcomes, while acknowledging that data gaps make its influence difficult to reliably measure and attribute. That attribution gap can distort marketing investment decisions, with spend going to channels that capture conversions rather than those that influenced them. Similarweb data cited in IAB’s report found consumers who received a brand recommendation from an AI assistant are 2.5 times more likely to visit that brand’s website within seven days, but only 8.8% of those visits come directly from AI referrals. The IAB framework focuses on three areas. It classifies who or what created the intent, made the decision, and executed the transaction. The IAB’s recommendations span AI platforms, brands, agencies and publishers. Platforms should disclose whether recommendations are paid or organic, which sources inform them and label AI-originated traffic consistently.

AI content licensing has largely comprised of one-off deals between publishers and tech companies. RSL, which is being renamed The Internet Licensing Collective (ILC), is pursuing a collective model instead, with Yahoo as its first licensee.

The deal means publishers of all sizes can license their content for use in Yahoo’s AI-powered search engine Scout and get paid based on how that content is used.

Digiday spoke with ILC co-founder Doug Leeds about how this model works and how publishers can earn revenue.

A licensee like Yahoo pays a blanket licensing fee for access to content in the collective. Leeds declined to say how much Yahoo was paying, but the fee is tied to the “demand-side revenue.”  Yahoo declined to be interviewed for this story.

As a licensee’s business scales, the amount available for publisher compensation can grow as well.

“It allows businesses that are making a lot of money off of the content to keep that content in place and sustainable. But it also allows for entrants,” Leeds said. “As the revenue scales, the percentage scales. So more money comes out. But that also means that entrants can come in, and there can be new use cases and new players and new products, and all of that’s important to the evolution of what we’re doing to the market.”

A new report out today from publishing analytics firm Mather Economics estimated that collective licensing for AI could generate publisher compensation equal to 15% to 35% of AI company revenue, a potential market that in three years could surpass the U.S. display advertising market. The analysis was based on the current annual revenue from the two biggest frontier models, OpenAI and Anthropic, and an economic model created by Mather Economics incorporating AI training-data scaling laws.

Leeds likened the licensing collective to a Spotify-like model.

“It’s kind of like Spotify, but also a song is a song is a song, and content is very different. And so we had to come up with these rules that made sense specifically for digital content,” Leeds said.

Leeds said ILC worked closely with its publisher collective to figure out those rules. The first step was to define a “segmented royalty pool.” This separates licensing revenue by AI company, rather than combining payments into a single fund shared by all participating publishers. That means publishers only receive a share of the revenue generated by AI products that actually use their content.

More AI and tech companies are testing pay-per-value licensing models. Digiday has previously reported that Google and Microsoft are testing and developing these models, which compensate publishers when their content meaningfully contributes to AI-generated responses, rather than paying based on how frequently their content is used. It’s a shift from flat-fee licensing deals to more dynamic pricing, based on the value publishers’ content delivers to AI companies.

When an AI company signs a licensing agreement with ILC, that money goes into its own royalty pool. Rather than combining all these payments into one enormous fund and distributing them across participating publishers, ILC keeps the royalty pools separate.

This protects specialized publishers from having their revenue diluted, Leeds explained. A general-purpose AI search engine like Yahoo Scout might use large quantities of content from hundreds or thousands of publishers, but a specialized healthcare AI product might use a much smaller amount of content from a handful of medical-focused publishers, and that specialized content could be considerably more valuable to the healthcare company, he said.

ILC wanted to ensure that a publisher’s compensation reflects the value of the AI product using its content, not simply how much content it contributes to the overall licensing ecosystem.

“An AI vertical in health might be very low volume, but much higher specific value. So if your content gets used in that, you should split that high value, not get diluted by all the content that got used by Yahoo,” Leeds said.

Over 3,000 publishers are now part of the ILC, he noted.

The payments are determined by what Leeds called “allocation rules.” These take into account how much content is used, like grounding, retrieval or displayed in an AI response, and whether the publisher is credited or cited as a source.

For example, an AI product that uses a publisher’s content to check a fact might receive a lower allocation than one that displays detailed election results and explicitly credits the Associated Press, Leeds said. He did not disclose the exact formula used to calculate each publisher’s share.

The allocation rules are intended to discourage AI companies from choosing content simply because it’s cheaper, and instead incentivize them to use what they consider the highest-quality sources, he said.

Publishers can also decide who they want to license to. Right now, their only option is Yahoo. But as more licensees sign up, they can go into their ILC portal and select which AI companies they do and don’t want to license their content to. For example, if a publisher signs a separate content licensing deal with an AI company, they can take their content out of the collective. In the portal, they can also see what their content is being used for, such as RAG or training.

Publishers can ask AI companies for a pay-per-crawl or pay-per-inference fee through the RSL Protocol, for example. “Thousands” are currently using this standard, Leeds said.

But Leeds wanted a new name for the non-profit organization that reflected the publisher collective, to make it clear that this is a licensing model that any publisher can join, he said.

Leeds said ILC is currently in talks with “some” of the major AI companies interested in the licensing collective. He declined to name which ones.

“You can do a deal with a one-off, and that’s fantastic. But that doesn’t invest in the infrastructure of content in general,” he said.

#7365. Why user-first ad experiences build the strongest app businesses

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Today’s mobile users are searching, streaming, scrolling and shopping in bursts as they go about their day. According to RealityMine, the average smartphone user interacts with around 18 different apps per day, racking up to 200 app sessions daily.¹ In each of those moments, attention is won second by second — and lost just as quickly.

Capturing value in those seconds is remarkably difficult. When audience acquisition is expensive, and engagement is just as hard to earn, app studios cannot afford ad experiences that cause churn.

In-app ads are a core part of the overall app experience, yet monetization and user retention are still too often treated as competing priorities. The assumption is that earning more revenue requires heavier ad loads, which damages the user experience, while protecting that experience means leaving money on the table.

“Disruptive ads have a severe, compounding negative impact on both current engagement and future user acquisition,” said Scott Sheffer, vp of global partnerships on Google’s sell-side monetization team. “Disruptive ads not only drive current users away but also prompt negative reviews, discouraging future users from downloading the app. Seems intuitive, but now there is data to back that.”
