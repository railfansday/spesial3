#7438. Giggly Squad’s SiriusXM deal signals a shift from podcast networks to creator ecosystems

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Last month, SiriusXM signed a multi-year deal with creators Paige DeSorbo and Hannah Berner for their Giggly Squad podcast reportedly valued at $160 million. Weeks later, the duo sold out four different nights for a live podcast tour at Madison Square Garden. That unprecedented deal signals a broader shift in podcasting: platforms are competing for creator-led businesses, not just shows.

“It’s even caught us on our heels, honestly, the demand for Giggly,” said Kelli Hurley, vp and head of content and podcast partnership at SiriusXM. “We were prepared, we were all hands on deck, but it has been just unprecedented in terms of what we’re seeing from the marketplace.”

Tom Webster, partner at trade association Sounds Profitable, said that despite podcast reach remaining flat since 2024, 8% of people who have consumed a podcast say they started in just the last 30 days. That’s a steady influx of new customers, and platforms like Spotify, Apple, and SiriusXM are trying to court them.

SiriusXM svp of podcast content Adam Sachs started working in podcasting 15 years ago, and he’s had a bird’s-eye view of the shifting landscape.

“The power of the network was what mattered [back then]. We would have these horizontal networks with a bunch of different shows in the network that all shared a sensibility,” he explained. “Now the approach is way more verticalized. We’re working with specific talent as opposed to a specific network, and helping them build a vertical ecosystem where their fans can engage. It’s sort of flipped.”

Building those vertical ecosystems can involve simple, expected things like helping a creator with their AV setup to far-reaching, off-platform support that includes IRL events.

“When we’re making these deals, we are increasingly thinking about a podcast as audio, video, social, and live. It’s those four legs of the stool, and we want to be a part of all of it,” said Sachs.

SiriusXM isn’t the only platform looking to work with creators beyond just hosting their show. Bryan Barletta, partner at Sounds Profitable and president of Podcast Movement, said Amazon’s investment in the NFL brother duo podcast, New Heights, signals a major shift in the partnership dynamic.

“We’re entering a new phase of podcasting where a distinct upper tier of creators is emerging, with significant separation from those coming up behind them,” he said.

The top tier of podcasting is drawing live audiences at arena scale. Giggly Squad is the first female-hosted podcast to headline Madison Square Garden, and tickets that averaged in the mid-$100s sold out fast. The sheer scope of the Giggly deal led Sirius to take a step back, according to Hurley. “In the past couple of weeks we took a total pause…we’ve got to do a full reset in how we want to go to market with this asset,” she said.

And brands are increasingly looking to get involved at multiple points in a creator podcast’s presence, from branded equipment to distribution support to showing up at IRL events. Now, more brands are heading into partnership talks wanting to “buy a creator,” according to Hurley. They’re asking for exclusivity, but Sirius wants to give them more.

“[We want] to be a one-stop shop,” she said.

Other platforms are offering a variety of ways for creators to connect with brands. Substack recently launched early access signups for dynamic ad insertion and plans for creators to leverage programmatic sponsorships in early 2027. Beehiiv just launched similar tools, dropping dynamic ad insertion, campaign management, and programmatic access just this past week.

“It gives creators more flexibility: run their own sponsor campaigns, put current ads into older episodes, and open unsold spots to programmatic demand when there’s a match,” said Ainsley Rossitto, head of podcast at beehiiv.

Since the mid-2020s, podcasts have aggressively pivoted to video. Sounds Profitable reports that now, only 17% of Americans expect a podcast to be audio-only. Substack’s head of video, Zach Taylor, told Digiday that half of their 350 highest revenue creators were using both audio in video in April 2024. “By February 2025, that number had grown to 82%,” he said.

The inclusion of a video component for podcasts makes it easier for discovery, as show clips can circulate on other social media platforms like YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram.

“Your podcast doesn’t have to be on video, but you have to have video,” said Barletta. Sounds Profitable’s research showed 84% of podcast clip consumers say clips led them to become regular podcast listeners at least sometimes.

Sachs said SiriusXM is seeing algorithmic growth through YouTube because of its built-in discoverability.

There are new pivots on the horizon: short-form content and a return to traditional radio. Sachs said Sirius is looking to do more with creators who are short-form-only content (monetizing their content, empowering them to do live events, and maybe even getting them on the radio), and are also supporting podcasters like Giggly Squad in creating their own radio shows — all in service of grabbing bigger slices of that audience pie.

“The radio audience is very distinct from the podcast audience; it’s a really disparate audience in terms of demographics, so we don’t see that as cannibalization at all. We see it as incremental reach,” Sachs said. “Giggly Squad is going to have a significant radio component, as well.”

#7434. People Inc. is building custom APIs to make its journalism easier for AI agents to use

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

People Inc. is moving toward custom APIs to help AI agents access its content more efficiently.

Rather than making AI systems shift through full webpages, People Inc., is giving them the article text and key details in a simpler format, according to Jon Roberts, People Inc.’s chief innovation officer.

“If you are an agent and you’re reading an HTML page, 90% of the content is junk meant for a browser to read, not an agent,” Roberts told delegates at Digiday’s Media Strategies London event. “It will really distract the agent, and it will be incredibly expensive to process all these tokens that it doesn’t need.”

The custom APIs strip out that extra material, sending the article text in Markdown along with details such as who wrote it and when it was published or updated and other “trust signals that matter.” The move comes as People Inc. shifts its broader investment priorities: the company laid off about 6% of its workforce last week. The cuts underscore the pressure on publishers to find durable businesses as AI reshapes how audiences discover and access journalism.

People Inc. first gave partners access to its content through web crawling, then through Amazon S3 buckets, which let the company send updates and retract content. It is now moving toward custom APIs, Roberts said, because crawling is an inefficient way for AI systems to get information.

Strategically, that could make access more efficient and give People Inc. greater control over how its journalism is delivered to AI partners.

“Every token that needs to be processed is expensive,” said Roberts. “If you throw it the entire HTML header, that’s abuse. Right, that’s not reasonable.”

AI companies are beginning to recognize the importance of knowing when publishers created or updated content, Roberts said. People Inc. is building those details into its content for its own AI tools, then using what it learns to offer AI partners a more useful way to access that material. The publisher is not focused on advertising directly to AI agents, he added.

The urgency comes as traffic from AI agents and agentic browsers grew 7,851% year over year, according to HUMAN’s 2026 State of AI Traffic report.

The challenge is already pressing for publishers trying to distinguish useful, customer-led agents from bots scraping content. “We need to act quickly on it because it’s not going to be something in the future — it’s happening now,” Daily Mail director of SEO and editorial commerce Carly Steven said at Digiday’s Media Strategies London event. “So that is a fairly urgent need that we all need to address and get our heads around,” she added.

Publishers are testing different ways to adapt their content and services for AI systems. People Inc. is moving toward structured access for AI partners. The Economist is experimenting with making marketing and B2B sales material outside of its paywall more easily readable for agents. Time has also converted its pages from HTML to Markdown and has even started to sell ads to those agents visiting its site.

Alongside figuring out which automated requests to allow, publishers are experimenting with how to package their journalism for AI systems. Forbes is also testing how to make some content more usable by AI agents. For its Vetted product reviews, the publisher has created Markdown versions of its content and an “MCP layer” while exploring separate presentations for booths and human readers.

Nina Gould, Forbes’ chief innovation officer, said the approach could help the publisher reach people using AI tools, but emphasized that the strategy is still developing a planned ChatGPT product-recommendation plugin that has not yet gone live. “On something like ads for agents, we’re still working on that,” she added.

#7432. How political creators are juggling platforms to find the three Rs: reach, revenue and rules

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Political creators need platforms that can deliver reach, revenue and clear rules. But no single platform reliably provides all three: YouTube offers ad revenue, Instagram can deliver huge audiences without paying creators and TikTok’s moderation and engagement can be unpredictable.

“A good platform for a political creator does four things,” said Ryan Schram, executive director of the Center for the Creator Economy at Syracuse University. “It helps new audiences find your work, it pays you fairly for that work, it tells you clearly what the rules are, and it lets you keep your relationship with your audience if you leave. Most platforms do one or two of those well. Very few do all four.”

Schram said that political creators gravitate to where political conversations are already happening, noting a 2024 PEW Research study that found that 85 percent of the most-followed news influencers are on X, half are on Instagram, and 44 percent are on YouTube.

“Journalists, campaigns, and elected officials are on X, and its users come for news. The catch is that creators and audiences aren’t in the same places,” Scharm said. “Audiences are larger on YouTube and Facebook, so the most successful political creators publish on several platforms rather than relying on one.”

Ahead of particularly contentious U.S. midterm elections, influencers are shaping narratives and highlighting key issues. Digiday spoke with six political creators and Schram about which platforms they are finding most fruitful, and which they’re struggling with.

YouTube remains a mainstay for many news and politics creators. “YouTube has the largest news audience and pays the most,” said Scharm. “It’s paid out more than $100 billion over four years and shares 55% of ad revenue.”

As far as the algorithm that drives that revenue, a YouTube spokesperson told Digiday it does “not favor or penalize content based on political orientation.” There are instances where YouTube’s systems “elevate high-quality voices,” like when major breaking news happens and the platform gets a special “breaking news shelf” on the homepage.

YouTube has leaned into news creators over the last few years, with its news, civics and podcast partnerships program, led by Brendan Feldman, offering advice on how to grow and monetize their channels. “For these last few years, a lot of focus has been on the real renaissance of journalists coming to YouTube, many in an independent capacity…and how we can best support them in building really vibrant businesses on our platform, connecting with audiences,” said Feldman.

Creator Aaron Parnas said he focuses on YouTube and Facebook in terms of monetization, though it’s only about 1 percent of his revenue pie.

“YouTube is the platform most worth investing in and also the hardest to grow on,” said V Spehar of Under the Desk News. “But if someone was just starting, I’d say focus on YouTube. It’s the most stable and fairly monetizes – but it’s hard to grow. So you may have to create for YouTube first, and then use clips to build discoverability by posting the clips on all socials until you figure out which social platform you perform best on.”

Several of the creators Digiday spoke with said Instagram has good engagement, but at least one mentioned the platform isn’t a revenue source. “I don’t make a penny because Instagram doesn’t pay out for anything, in terms of Reels. So I make zero money on Instagram, but I had 450 million views last month,” said Parnas, who added he gets much higher viewership on the platform than elsewhere, but frustratingly isn’t earning anything for that elevated engagement.

Newly independent news creator Mark Istook, said he found Instagram to be easier to predict, and the comments are far less “poisonous and toxic” than on sibling Meta-owned platform, Facebook.

The jury is split on Instagram’s utility. While creator Grant Hermes found Instagram might as well be a “black box” in terms of understanding its underlying structure, creator Adam Mockler, however, has figured it out, boasting 90 million views a month on the platform.

Mockler pulls in even more views on Facebook, boasting 120 million on the platform which he called an “underdog” that has been “really swinging lately.”

“I genuinely think Zuckerberg changed something when Trump got into office,” he said. “They opened politics back up on Facebook, and it allowed advertisers to buy up spots on political stuff again.” It’s performing so well, Mockler has doubled down, putting more resources and more team members, he did not offer specifics, on the platform, even hiring an intern that helps post on the platform.

Parnas said he also focuses on Facebook because it, along with YouTue, is where the most revenue comes from.

Mockler also told Digiday he’s leaning into Reddit, a platform notoriously difficult to break through.

“We had a 17-year-old high schooler posting on Reddit for a while…I view Reddit as more valuable than TIkTok right now,” Mockler said, adding about 70 percent of the intern’s role was him posting his appearances on CNN or other traditional news platforms in several subreddits, while the rest was on the official Mockler Media Reddit account, all to get people to engage with his content and find him on other platforms. “I view it as internal PR,” Mockler said.

Spehar and others said TikTok has been a tough platform lately, with inconsistent flagging and engagement hampering their revenue and reach.

“[TikTok] blocks political content or mislabels it as ads and live streams focusing on politics can be falsely reported. Overall it’s hard to get your perspective out if you don’t already have a big channel at this point,” Spehar said, adding the platform is still a good place for creators to build, but is more saturated than it once was.

Parnas placed TikTok on the third rung of his monetization scheme, underneath YouTube and Facebook and alongside Instagram and Istook said the platform should be more reliable when it comes to engagement, but is “all over the map” after it closed a deal with the U.S. earlier this year.

“My views on TikTok have remained very, very consistent over the past year,” said Parnas. Yet just a few days ago, one of his political videos got stuck in an “under review” stage for nearly three hours. “I just took it down,” he said.

Istook said he has the biggest audience on TIkTok but found the platform the least predictable in terms of engagement. “Something will do millions of views, and something else will do 1,500, which just is hard to make sense of with a follower count like I have,” he said.

While strategists fear the tech will replace their roles, the majority agree that there are also reasons to be optimistic, according to a WARC report.

The advertising industry has faced significant challenges this year, marked by agency consolidations at major holding companies and sweeping headcount reductions. Between August 2025 and August 2026, the industry recorded a loss of 6,900 jobs, per Emarketer.

While there are many factors behind the cuts, one of the most prominent has been the rise of artificial intelligence across all aspects of the industry. From data analysis to campaign generation, AI’s adoption has been swift across nearly all marketing disciplines, leaving many executives apprehensive about the future.

Still, 66% of agency-side strategists agree there are clear reasons to be optimistic, according to WARC’s Future of Strategy 2026 report.

“We’re not saying the problems are solved, but we are saying the tides may be turning,” said Lena Roland, content director at WARC Strategy. “After a period of layoffs and consolidation, there’s a sense that things might be stabilizing and strategists can [begin] to look forward again.”

The reasons for the guarded positivity are simple: Clients are seeing a need for thinking that is rooted in human insights, Roland explained. Strategy influence with clients jumped to 50%, up from 41% in 2025, per WARC.

“This indicates that clients are increasingly recognizing the value of strategy, particularly at a time when growth is harder to achieve,” Roland said. “AI can help us understand the world as it is; strategists can help us understand what it can become.”

Ironically, AI is helping fuel strategists’ confidence. Nearly two-thirds, 65%, of the 489 agency-side strategists surveyed said AI gives them time for more nuanced, strategic work. Simultaneously, 55% believe the tech will refocus strategy on human perspectives. WARC’s report was fielded in August.

“Strategists are positioned as the best people to be guardians of reality by grounding ideas in real human insights,” Roland said. “In an age of AI, humanity is a competitive advantage. AI can’t replace human judgment or intuition, and it can’t ask the questions that are really worth asking.”

Not yet, anyway. Despite their optimism for the near-term, strategists are more wary about the long-term. More than half of the strategists polled in WARC’s study — 56%, up from 41% last year — are concerned that AI will eventually advance enough to replace them.

“They’re more optimistic about the future, but they’re also realistic about the scale of change,” Roland said. “The strategists that will thrive are those who learn to use AI well, while still bringing the human perspective, critical thinking and commercial understanding that makes their work valuable.”

However, the technology could become a double-edged sword: The same tools that strategists are using to move upstream with clients could also be leveraged by account executives, explained Michael Farmer, chairman and CEO at marketing consultancy Farmer & Company.

“When agencies talk about AI [right now], they’re not talking about how they can do a more strategic job for their clients,” Farmer said. “If they have the will, the senior account executives can [use AI] to offer more strategy.”

Doing so would elevate account executives to more of a business partner role, rather than one more akin to a traffic manager whose job is to get stuff out the door, Farmer said. He explained that over the past 30-some years, the account executive role has been diminished through the rise of programmatic negotiating, the unbundling of media and creative and the introduction of strategists in the late ‘90s and early 2000s.

“They took away a responsibility that the account people already had,” Farmer said. “They used to tell me when I first got started that the strategist was ‘the conscience of the consumer’ … They were there to kind of provide discipline between caring about the brand and caring about the brief that they got from the client.”

Often, that meant rewriting the briefs accordingly, Farmer said. Now, AI can often write those briefs as well — if not better — than a strategist can, which is reflected in the sentiment from strategists that they have more time to think beyond the brief. Agencies and their strategists will need to find how to prove their value beyond their traditional remit.

“Strategy as a discipline has too often been treated as a supporting function, or even thrown in for free, rather than recognized as a valuable service in its own right,” Roland said. “There’s an opportunity now for strategists to become genuine growth partners, helping shape not just how a brand communicates, but where the business goes next.”

Fortunately for strategists, many are already aware of the need to prove business results. In WARC’s survey, 80% of strategists said they must develop stronger evidence to prove their business impact, and 57% said they need a new value proposition and compensation model.

“AI is a great opportunity, but you have to be a strategist to realize that it could help you tap that unrealized potential,” Farmer said.

Which is ultimately why strategists see hope in the face of AI’s headwinds. Businesses will always be under pressure to demonstrate growth and will always need people who can find new ways to achieve it, whether that is on their own or with the help of AI, Roland explained.

“Businesses will always need people who can diagnose and solve problems,” Roland said. “The best strategists will use AI to get beyond the obvious and connect creativity to commercial outcomes, helping businesses see possibilities they couldn’t see before.”

“It’s brought a lot more focus in the industry, both on the client side and certainly in agency land, on the importance and value of these foundational components,” said Jonathan Margulies, managing partner at Winterberry Group.

Here’s the case for and against clients sticking or twisting with LiveRamp.

Upon the deal’s closure, Publicis released a video in which CEO Arthur Sadoun and chief strategy officer Carla Serrano spent six minutes laying out Publicis’ LiveRamp pitch — and attempting to defuse concerns over neutrality and independence of the adtech company’s services.

For Sadoun and Serrano, the pairing’s chief benefit to clients was its “plug and play” nature — the idea that, in using LiveRamp and Epsilon, either as a client of the broader Publicis constellation or not, clients can save upfront investment and act faster. “Clients are trapped between two bad options: surrender their data to the walled gardens for easy scale and lose control, or hire an army of consultants and lose time and a lot of money,” said Sadoun.

Clients that do decide to unplug LiveRamp from their marketing stack will find the search for a new partner an expensive and time-consuming process. LiveRamp is considered to be a market leader, with a huge dataset owing to its larger customer base and partnership deals with commerce networks.

Combining its capabilities with Epsilon makes its position even stronger. Given the IAB projects commerce media spending to grow 13.6% this year, making it the third-fastest growing area of advertising investment after social media and CTV — those capabilities, which are used on the supply-side by the likes of Walmart, Target, Albertsons and Kroger may become more important to CMOs in coming years. LiveRamp is also well-positioned for advertisers hoping to move early into emerging channels like ChatGPT; the firm became OpenAI’s first conversion API partner in June.

For many clients, those arguments will outweigh neutrality worries expressed by Publicis’ holdco rivals (which aren’t, in any case, united in their opposition. Havas, for example, seems set to continue using LiveRamp’s tech in its flagship Converged data platform in North America.)

Furthermore advertisers can now rely upon FTC-enforced rules of the road. Publicis is required to maintain access to LiveRamp by competitors, to set up firewalls to ensure it doesn’t access customer data or confidential business information, and to permit an independent auditor to probe its compliance every six months for two years, and then annually for three years (it doesn’t, however, prohibit the firm from developing integrated products that bridge LiveRamp and its other business units). “It will stay open to every brand, every platform, and yes, every agency,” said Serrano.

Publicis’ ambitions for LiveRamp are tied up in earlier investments in companies like Lotame, Epsilon and Sapient, through which it wants to position itself as a one-stop shop for the entire media investment process. #7391. Digiday+ Research 2026 Creator Marketing Index: A brand guide to social media campaigns

As creator marketing becomes a fixture in many brands’ marketing strategies, creators’ roles are shifting and expanding. Brands may pay creators to produce sponsored content, gift products to them in the hope of organic posts, or rely on a combination of creator- and brand-produced content published on their own social media accounts.

Overall, social media remains the leading channel for creator partnerships: 96% of respondents to Digiday’s first-quarter survey of brands and agencies said they partner with creators to create social media content. Within social media, Instagram (90%), Facebook (80%), YouTube (72%) and TikTok (63%) were the platforms marketers said they used most.

Across these social platforms, brands are taking various approaches to creator campaigns, from sponsored and gifted posts to content formats like product demonstrations, styled photos and event coverage. Understanding how these approaches perform can help brands make informed decisions about creator partnerships, content formats and campaign investment.

To measure the impact creator content has on brand campaigns, Digiday+ Research analyzed the engagement levels on leading creators’ social media campaigns from January to September 2026.

The Digiday Creator Marketing Index collected data from 1,755 social media posts and 20 branded social media campaigns, analyzing the top posts associated with each campaign’s hashtag and scoring them across a set of key dimensions to create a total index average score. Results are dependent upon the list of social media campaigns and the time period of data collection, generating a snapshot of the creator marketing landscape at a specific moment in time.

Digiday’s Index collected social media data from January to September 2026. Due to data restrictions, TikTok views were not captured. Data collection was conducted by Dash Social, an agentic social platform powered by brand-intelligent AI.

The social media campaigns were selected by Digiday’s editorial team.

When considering video content performance by platform, Instagram Reels received far more video views than YouTube’s Shorts and long-form videos, while TikTok videos had higher average engagement in later analysis. However, these results should be considered within the context of the Index’s focus on branded social media campaigns, meaning that the majority of posts included marketing-focused content rather than non-brand-related content, which performs the best on YouTube. Additionally, on YouTube — a video-first platform with a core strength in long-form content — most of the captured posts consisted of Shorts rather than long-form posts.

Average engagement rate — engagement divided by follower count — was only measurable on Instagram. When looking at average engagement rate by format type on Instagram, Reels performed the best among Instagram’s formats at an 11% engagement rate, underscoring the strength of short-form video on Instagram. Still posts came in second with a 7% engagement rate, followed by Carousel posts at 5%. Carousel posts can contain videos, but views were not counted as part of the average engagement rate for Carousel posts with video. Likewise, views were not counted as part of the average engagement rate for Still posts or Reels.

The top 75 posts associated with each campaign’s hashtag were identified based on overall engagement, including likes and comments across platforms. When Digiday analyzed those top-performing posts by platform, with engagement weighted equally across platforms, Instagram accounted for the largest share of posts associated with campaign hashtags.

However, TikTok posts within the top 75 generated higher average engagement than posts on other platforms. In other words, Instagram appeared more frequently among the highest-performing posts overall, while the top-performing TikTok posts generated greater engagement on average.

According to Digiday’s analysis, Instagram is a more consistent platform for performance, while TikTok has greater reach potential and a higher engagement ceiling. For brands running longer campaigns or prioritizing consistency, Instagram offers a more predictable environment for creator marketing. For smaller brands or those with limited budgets, TikTok is likely the better platform choice for creator marketing. While engagement on TikTok isn’t as consistent, its higher engagement ceiling gives creator content a better possibility of reaching a wider audience.

Looking more closely at sponsored and gifted posts, the Index defines them as follows. Sponsored posts refer to content created through a direct partnership between a brand and a creator. Gifted posts feature products that brands have sent to creators, typically as part of a PR effort, without a paid content agreement. Digiday classified posts as sponsored or gifted based on disclosures in captions, including paid labels, disclaimers, and hashtags such as #ad, #paid, #gifted and #PR. In some video posts, creators verbally expressed whether they had been paid by a brand.

The most common content types across both sponsored posts and gifted posts were product demonstrations and style shots. The Index defines a demo as content about a single brand or product that includes a description and shows a person using the item. A style shot is content that showcases a product in a scenic or visually styled setting without an explanation of how to use the item.

Style shots appeared more frequently in gifted posts than they did in sponsored posts, by a ratio of 19 to 15, indicating that creators favor that format when featuring products they have received for free without a formal content agreement. One possible explanation is that demos can appear to be overtly promotional since they feature one product as opposed to a “get ready with me” (GRWM) format that typically showcases multiple brands. Creators may want to avoid producing content that could be interpreted as advertising when it comes to gifted items, according to Digiday’s analysis.

When comparing average engagement rates, sponsored posts outperformed gifted posts across content types, with one notable exception: demos  — the most used content format type — where gifted posts performed best. Style shots displayed the opposite pattern, performing better in sponsored content where they also delivered the highest overall engagement among the content formats analyzed.

One potential factor behind this difference is the level of production and associated costs behind the content. Gifted style shots were often simple flat lays or product images, while sponsored style shots frequently included product videos. Brands often give creators larger budgets to develop content for sponsored product posts. That distinction is particularly important for product categories like fragrance, where brands rely on visual storytelling to communicate abstract qualities like mood and sensory experience.

While engagement provides a strong measure of how audiences respond to creator content, reach — calculated by average views — offers another important metric of performance. Digiday’s analysis found that sponsored posts had greater overall reach based on views than gifted posts, by a margin of 334,055 to 48,008. Within sponsored posts, style shots (707,646), demos (250,764) and influencer events (231,595) had the most views. Among gifted posts, demos generated the most views (81,023), well below sponsored formats.

That margin of difference is likely also linked to budget differences, according to Digiday’s analysis. Brands typically provide creators with paid media budgets to amplify sponsored posts beyond a creator’s existing audience, while gifted posts are limited to a creator’s own audience. As a result, sponsored content can generate both organic and paid reach, while gifted product content relies on organic distribution with more limited reach.

By contrast, lower-ranked campaigns had a weaker presence on brand-owned channels, with no brand-generated posts in many cases. These findings suggest that while creator participation is an important driver of campaign success, brands’ own social media activity also plays a critical role, according to Digiday’s analysis.

Many of the highest-ranked campaigns also included a mix of gifted posts and sponsored posts. Gifted posts came from both macro- and micro-creators, indicating that high-ranked campaigns sent products to a wide range of content creators. However, investment was another important factor. All of the highest-ranked campaigns had large budgets backing them, highlighting the growing role of pay-to-play social media campaigns, where brands must invest in both creator partnerships and amplification to build reach.

Not all campaigns with paid support performed equally, however, suggesting that investment alone does not guarantee stronger performance.

Charlotte Tilbury’s collaboration with Influenster, a product sampling and review platform owned by Bazaarvoice, generated a large portion of the gifted posts for the #exaggereyes campaign. Rather than selecting creators and sending them PR packages directly, the brand relied on Influenster to collect user-generated content for its own use. Most of the gifted posts were demo videos, a format that Digiday’s analysis found generated higher average engagement within gifted posts than sponsored posts.

As a result, the Hydro Boost campaign had the highest share of brand-seeded content — sponsored or gifted posts — at 39% of all posts.

The campaign also incorporated the broader #hyaluronicacid hashtag alongside the branded #hyrdoboost hashtag. This strategy allowed Neutrogena to not only position the Hydro Boost product as a line extension but also connect the campaign to the larger hyaluronic acid product category, potentially exposing the campaign to audiences with a broader interest in hyaluronic acid products.

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.

We are seeking Support Engineers to provide world-class developer support for anyone experiencing issues or seeking guidance when using Supabase. The support team is globally distributed with a strong focus on minimizing time to fix, and works closely with the product engineering and growth teams.

Provide initial and timely responses to all support cases for a variety of topics, from billing or pre-sales requests to optimization or implementation questions.

Triage support cases including directing users to previous answers, code examples, documentation, or escalating to specific technical or business team members.

Monitor multiple feedback channels, e.g. Github Issues & Discussions (check them here: https://github.com/supabase/supabase/issues).

Identify where internal tooling might be developed or obtained to improve support efficiency.

Reproduce issues, create test cases and improve test coverage, if you have experience with testing.

Experienced in customer support, with 3+ years of experience providing developer support to technical customers.

Proficient in SQL (ideally PostgreSQL). You feel comfortable with complex SQL queries. If you have a database administration experience, it’s a plus.

Excellent communication skills and fluency in English.

Skilled in using Linux/Unix operating systems and the command line.

Experienced working with GitHub Issues and Discussions.

Bonus points if you're familiar with popular JavaScript frameworks like React, Vue, and Svelte and have some experience with Node.js.

Having a degree in Computer Science is a plus too.

It’s a full-time position permanently, with four 10-hour shifts covering weekends during APAC Hours: 00:00-10:00 UTC

We are primarily looking for someone in Southeast Asia or India time zones for this shift.

This position covers Friday → Monday based on the above UTC times, so this may vary slightly for your specific timezone.

We hire globally. We believe you can do your best work from anywhere. There are no Supabase offices, but we provide a WeWork membership or co-working allowance you can use anywhere in the world.

Every team member receives ESOP (equity ownership) in the company. We want everyone to share in the upside of what we’re building together.

Use this budget to set up your ideal work environment—laptop, monitor, headphones, or whatever helps you do your best work.

Supabase covers 100% of health insurance for employees and 80% for dependents, wherever you are. Your wellbeing and your family’s health are important to us.

Once a year, the entire company gathers in a new city for a week of connection, collaboration, and fun. It’s a highlight of our year.

We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

Supabase was born-remote and open-source-first. We believe our globally distributed team is our secret weapon in building tools developers love.

Over $1B raised (including our $500M Series F)

We move fast, build in public, and use what we ship. If it’s in your project, we probably use it in ours too. We believe deeply in the open-source ecosystem and strive to support—not replace—existing tools and communities.

New remote jobs delivered to your inbox, every week.
