On this episode of The Speed of Culture podcast, Carina Ertl, chief marketing officer at Bucherer USA, joins Matt Britton to explore how those forces shape modern luxury marketing.

Their conversation examines why the category often reverses traditional demand economics, and how experience creates loyalty long after the purchase.

The also discuss why passionate curators can outperform creators with massive followings, and why AI may make human taste an even stronger competitive advantage.

Carina Ertl is the Chief Marketing Officer at Bucherer USA, part of the Swiss luxury watch and jewelry retailer whose heritage dates back to 1888. Bucherer operates across physical retail and ecommerce, offering luxury timepieces and jewelry.

Carina’s career spans luxury brand building, digital transformation, entrepreneurship, private equity, and nearly a decade at Swarovski. Her experience across markets and business models has shaped an approach to marketing built around curiosity, cultural fluency, and long-term client relationships

02:52 Luxury Flips the Demand Curve — Carina describes luxury marketing as “art and science,” with perhaps a little more art than science. Unlike many categories trying to create demand, luxury often deals with the opposite problem: too much demand for too little product. That scarcity changes the marketing playbook. The goal, now, is to protect desirability while deepening the meaning surrounding the product.

06:39 Certified Pre-Owned Turns Trust Into Value — The secondary watch market is growing, but Carina draws a sharp distinction between generic resale and certified pre-owned. Authentication matters because counterfeiting creates real uncertainty in the category. Certification gives consumers confidence while allowing retailers to unlock the history and storytelling embedded in older pieces, turning resale into its own desirable luxury experience.

11:53 Between Product and Sale: Where the Real Product Lies — For Bucherer, the customer journey does not move directly from product to transaction. Carina sees the experience in between as part of the product itself: community, belonging, identity, storytelling, trust, and eventually repurchase. That makes luxury a long-term relationship business rather than a sequence of isolated sales.

14:27 Trust Always Beats Follower Count — Carina says some of Bucherer’s strongest creator partnerships come from passionate curators rather than influencers with millions of followers. A smaller voice with deep category knowledge and a highly engaged community can carry more credibility than broad reach alone. Therein lies a big lesson for marketers: authority and authentic passion can matter more than audience size.

17:36 AI Makes Human Taste More Valuable — Carina is enthusiastic about AI as a productivity and process tool, but she sees a limit when automation starts replacing cultural judgment. In luxury, anyone can copy a formula. The differentiators are taste, creativity, execution, and knowing what is happening in culture and subculture. As AI makes generic ideas easier to generate, the distinctly human parts of brand building become more important.

21:44 Curiosity Survives Every Marketing Shift — Carina’s advice to future marketing leaders is built around adaptability. Marketing has changed repeatedly throughout her career, from digital transformation to AI, and she does not believe anyone can graduate knowing everything they will need. The durable advantage is curiosity: seek unfamiliar projects, learn the broader business, and stay willing to change as the function changes around you.

A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.
