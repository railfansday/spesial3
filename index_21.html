Amazon, along with its Big Tech cohorts, wants advertisers to trust its AI more, but fresh allegations from federal regulators underline concerns over such platforms.

Last week, the Federal Trade Commission published newly unredacted details from its lawsuit accusing Amazon of secretly inflating advertising prices, with similar charges against Google still fresh in the public mindset.

According to the FTC, Amazon imposed surcharges of up to 50% on ordinary shopping days and 250% during major retail events, generating an additional $87 million during the 2022 holiday season. Amazon disputes the allegations, and the legal proceedings are ongoing.

Amazon recently reported quarterly advertising revenue approaching $20 billion, with  Amazon Ads Agent – an AI-powered platform that’s likely to attract accusations of “black box” from detractors – a key part of its push to automate campaign planning, buying and optimization.

And with Amazon, the third pillar of adland’s triopoly, the key question for advertisers is whether they should hand over more control to a platform when they cannot independently verify how its existing auctions determine prices.

The FTC and 22 state attorneys general accuse Amazon of misleading advertisers about its Sponsored Products auctions.

Amazon presented these as generalized second-price auctions, where advertisers generally pay prices determined by competing bids rather than their maximum bid.

However, regulators allege Amazon introduced undisclosed reserve-price mechanisms that increased charges without corresponding competition, effectively pushing prices closer to first-price auctions.

According to the government body’s allegations, during the 2021 holiday season, Amazon increased average cost per click by 20%, with some advertisers seeing increases exceeding 200%. The complaint also alleges executives considered using reserve prices to address a $40 million revenue shortfall.

The allegations extend to how Amazon explained rising prices, i.e., whether Amazon misrepresented how its auctions worked and what advertisers were charged.

However, Amazon maintains that reserve prices reflect the value of its advertising placements, disputes the FTC’s interpretation, and argues that reserve pricing is not inherently unlawful.

For advertisers, the concern is straightforward: they can see what they paid, but cannot necessarily establish how that price was calculated.

The allegations come shortly after Amazon introduced new products designed to automate more advertising decisions.

At October’s unBoxed conference, Amazon unveiled Ads Agent and Full-Funnel Campaigns, allowing advertisers to set objectives, products, creative and budgets while its technology handles targeting, allocation and optimization.

Amazon says beta testing delivered up to 67% higher return on long-term ad spend and 29% lower costs per new-to-brand customer compared with manually configured campaigns. Those figures are company-reported.

The proposition is that advertisers can reduce manual campaign management and improve performance. However, greater automation also means relying more heavily on Amazon to explain how budgets are spent and why its systems make particular decisions.

While the FTC’s allegations concern Sponsored Products auctions, not Amazon’s new AI products, they still add weight to concerns raised about advertisers’ ability to inspect automated decisions, verify results, and understand fees.

Google’s ad tech antitrust case provides a precedent, although the conduct and markets differ.

A federal court found Google illegally monopolized publisher ad-serving and ad exchange markets – in a separate case, its search practices were also found to be monopolistic. The subsequent remedies stopped short of divestiture but imposed measures intended to improve competition and interoperability.

Both cases concern platforms whose control over advertising infrastructure limits customers’ ability to scrutinize how transactions work.

The difference is that Amazon is now asking advertisers to give its technology greater control over buying decisions, even as regulators challenge the transparency of its existing auction practices. These developments involve different businesses and issues, but they point to a broader accountability problem in digital advertising.

— Lindsay Marty, founder of Above the Bar Marketing, speaking to Digiday about advertisers reassessing their Google Search budgets.

When one of the world’s most valuable companies starts to get serious about AI waste, it’s time to pay attention. Apple is placing a hard cap of $200 on its engineers’ daily AI token use, and may reduce non-engineers’ token allowance to $25, Bloomberg reported last week (Nov. 4).

A handful of ad agencies have already embraced token capping this year — using AI software, or a bespoke tool, to prevent users from consuming more than a set amount of AI compute as the costs of AI transformation become clearer. Apple’s policy change indicates that more companies could follow that path, but there are tradeoffs involved in this strict approach to costs.

Agencies considering capping token usage must weigh up the risk they could douse their employees’ creativity by limiting their access to the tech.

AI costs — from tokens to licensing and subscriptions — are likely as cheap as they’ll ever be. While manageable today, they’re kept low because of the enormous capital expenditures of companies like Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. Eventually, those firms will want a return on those investments, meaning today’s prices might not be stable for long.

With that in mind, marketing agencies are building the infrastructure of AI accountability today, rather than wait for a crisis. Performance agency Roast, for example, has capped the amount of AI tokens its staffers can use since March.

“It’s up to the individual to use those tokens responsibly, and I think the benefit of that is then it’s making people think about what use cases they use tokens for,” John Campbell, head of innovation and AI at Roast, told Digiday.

The company allots a set amount of tokens to each employee within its Claude subscription, Campbell said. Higher individual caps can be provided at particularly busy times. He noted that the limit had stopped some users from relying on the tech for repetitive tasks that might be better delegated to non-AI software tools.

Video and image production generally represents the most token-intensive activity for creative agencies; media agencies experimenting with agentic tools for planning and buying must also grapple with runaway operating costs.

U.S. media agency PMG places a $50 daily limit on staff AI usage through its Alli4U portal. Staff that hit the cap more than once can be assigned a larger token budget, or receive advice on using different AI models, Dillon Larberg, consulting and strategy director, told Digiday in September. “That provides us a human in the loop moment to meet with teams or meet with individuals,” he said.

While agencies might still want to allow staff the flexibility to experiment and play with AI tools they can use tiered capping systems to provide teams with enough leeway to pilot something new, without burning up the budget.

“If you say you have a limit of $30, $25, $10 a day, you’ll find people become very creative in how they get this stuff done based on that constraint,” said Jonathan Whiteside, global executive vp at Dept, which caps team AI usage rather than individual costs.

“Constraints are sometimes useful for us to find new ways of working,” Whiteside added. “People start to think much more carefully about, ‘Well, do I actually need to use AI to do this?’”

AI costs, including the price of tokens, won’t stay low forever, so agencies should make hay while the sun’s shining. Intentionally cutting back before compute costs rise would be like abstaining from MoviePass or WeWork in the glorious days of zero interest rates. Agencies can chew through tokens while they develop the best uses for AI tech now, while it’s cheap to do so.

Some agencies use soft AI caps, rather than a hard limit. “When someone maxes out their spend for that tier, they show their work and their output to their department head,” explained Daniel Gilbert, CEO of Brainlabs, in September. He described the company’s approach as one that embraced “checkpoints” rather than caps. “The department head goes through the work with them, and if it’s good work, then they push them into the next tier.”

Such an approach preserves staff autonomy while keeping a lid on AI spending for agencies that want to to let a hundred flowers, or AI agents, bloom.

There are also means of nudging staff into cost-conscious AI decisions, without placing concrete limits on their usage. Gateway systems, or “gating,” refers to a tool which reroutes a user from a token-intensive AI model to a less powerful (and cheaper) model, if it’s better suited to a user’s project. If they’re using ChatGPT to draft their LinkedIn posts, they probably don’t need Astra, its latest and most powerful model.

Whiteside said that model selection is “a big factor” in keeping costs down, but that gating systems or staff training can provide a more appropriate guardrail than an individual token allowance.

“The perspective of the shot. How long that shot is going to last. Is there going to be voiceover or sound effects or text callouts that are happening?” said Wells.

She has adopted this extensive storyboarding process for two main reasons. First, it allows the brand to literally see what she intends to film and how she plans to edit it all together. Second, it allows Wells to head into filming on solid ground with minimal risk of the brand being dissatisfied when she hands in the final video and asking for reshoots.

“That can really mess me up and kind of create a domino effect in my workflow,” said Wells.

That domino effect is important to avoid because here’s the most crucial part of Wells’s production process to be aware of: She does everything by herself. That includes making custom newspapers in Photoshop (as she did for this recent video for Bayer), building sets, setting up lighting, prepping and styling food products and, of course, filming. “The bulk of my time is spent on set filming, just because I am one person with two hands,” said Wells. She added, “every time I’m setting up a shot, I’m actually setting up two shots because I have the shot that I’m filming and then I have the behind the scenes that’s happening.”

“Music is the very first thing that I choose, even before I’m storyboarding. So even decisions like that have been made at this point a very long time ago. And so it really is just putting the finishing touches on things,” she said.
