#7340. The Trade Desk Brings Back Dave Pickles Amid Growing Pains

Three years after he stepped away, Dave Pickles is coming back to The Trade Desk amid the worst stretch in its history as a public company.

The co-founder and former chief technology officer, who left his role and the company’s board in 2023, will establish and lead a new CTO Council, The Trade Desk announced today. He has stayed on as advisor since leaving.

The council will work with The Trade Desk’s engineering and product teams on its core technology and product roadmap. The company, in a blog post, said that work includes “architectural direction, operational excellence, and key engineering investments—including agentic capabilities and advanced AI-powered advertising workflows.”

“I’m so excited Dave is back. I’ve missed working with him,” CEO Jeff Green said in a statement. “We’ve had so many successes together over the years, and I’m energized to take all of that history and point it toward the future. This is a pivotal crossroads for our company and the industry.”

The announcement lands a week before The Trade Desk is expected to report third-quarter earnings on Nov. 9.

The Trade Desk went public in 2016, hitting $1.1 billion market capitalization on its first day, betting the open web would hold against the walled gardens. Pickles ran the company’s technology through most of the climb that followed.

The company he is rejoining has slowing revenue growth, partners pushing back on its products, and a stock sitting 91% below its peak.

In August, TTD reported second-quarter revenue of $715 million, up 3% year-over-year but about $37.6 million short of analysts’ forecast. It guided third-quarter revenue to at least $650 million, roughly 12% below the same quarter a year earlier, which would be its first YoY revenue decline as a public company.

Pickles framed his return around the company’s existing team. “I’m thrilled by all the progress our engineering and product teams have made in recent years,” Pickles said in a statement. “The foundation here is strong, and I’m excited to support the team and help guide them through a new era of rapid change and opportunity.”

Green and Pickles founded The Trade Desk in 2009. Pickles came from AdECN, an ad exchange Microsoft acquired, where he led a team that built its real-time building exchange.

When he stepped down in 2023, he handed day-to-day technical leadership to Sairaj Uddin, then SVP of engineering, and Gruia Pitigoi-Aron, then SVP of product, both reporting to Green.

The Trade Desk’s technology has been at the center of its troubles since then. The adtech company launched the Kokai platform in 2023, around the time Pickles left. ADWEEK reported that advertisers frustrated with Kokai moved millions of dollars in ad spend away from the platform to Amazon, and the company later sunsetted Kokai.

The Trade Desk has also seen turnover in its finance leadership, including a string of CFO departures. More recently it pushed into AI agents with Koa, its conversational AI interface.

The company did not say whether Pickles is returning full time, rejoining the board or holding an officer title, or how the CTO Council will divide authority with the company’s current engineering and product leaders. The Trade Desk declined to comment on those details and pointed to its blog post announcing the move.

The district offers a wide range of CTE programs, including in the fields of artificial intelligence, cosmetology, IT security, nursing, paralegal and veterinary science.

Student demand is surpassing the number of seats available for classes at West-MEC’s six campuses, which select its students through an application process, said French. West-MEC’s six campuses enrolled about 3,000 students this school year, he said, while 6,000 students typically apply every year for West-MEC’s CTE programs.

“We still have seen a significant demand for programs, and we’re trying to expand as fast as we can to help meet those needs,” French said.

West-MEC also employs about 100 CTE instructors on its campuses, French said, and there are almost zero vacancies for those roles this year. The district’s CTE teacher retention rate is around 90%, he added. As the district continues to increase its course offerings, he said, it plans to have filled an additional 18 CTE instructional roles by the 2027-28 school year.

The district is uniquely well-positioned compared to other schools nationwide, as federal data has shown persistent challenges with CTE staffing.

For instance, 31% of public schools reported difficulties with hiring for open CTE teaching positions during the 2020-21 school year, according to the most recently available survey data from the U.S. Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics. A common reason districts struggle to recruit and retain CTE instructors is that they can’t keep up with competitive industry salaries, French said. While West-MEC also cannot offer comparable industry wages to its CTE teachers, he said, the district is paying “significantly higher than a normal teaching wage.”

The average teacher salary at West-MEC was $78,823 in fiscal year 2025 compared to Arizona's average pay for all teachers of $65,613 in the same fiscal year. However, West-MEC’s average wages have since grown to $85,247 for FY27.

West-MEC receives supplemental state funds based on student enrollment to help cover its competitive teacher pay in addition to supporting its partnering districts with student counseling services and professional development, French said.

He added that West-MEC is trying to tap into that local public support for CTE and momentum as the district continues to expand. Last November, voters approved a $415 million bond to help the district build more campuses in high-demand fields like healthcare, technology and welding, particularly as student interest in its CTE programs surges.

For other school districts and states looking to boost their CTE enrollment, build out their programs and improve staffing, he advises those leaders to not “be afraid to think outside the box” and take advantage of the growing public support for CTE programs.

“If the answer to the question is ‘We’ve always done it that way,’ then you know you have some work to do,” French said, adding that school leaders can also look to other local and national innovative CTE programs. The final rule, set to publish Friday in the Federal Register, will ensure accrediting agencies “prioritize diversity of thought on campuses,” the agency said.

“For too long, accreditors have effectively served as a cartel to protect their interests, rather than the educational value that institutions of higher education are expected to deliver for students,” he said in a statement.

The Education Department accepted public comment on the rule for a month, the minimum window required by law. During that period, which the agency said “afforded the public a substantial opportunity for meaningful input,” it received some 7,000 responses.

Among them were requests by commenters to phase in the new accreditation regulations. The department rejected this request, saying “the eight months between November 2026 and July 2027 is sufficient time to update standards, policies, and procedures to achieve compliance.”

On Thursday, the Council for Higher Education Accreditation expressed disappointment with the final rule.

"Many of the central concerns raised by CHEA and the higher education community remain largely unresolved," Nasser Paydar, the organization's president, said in a statement. #7152. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Meaningful alternative text for images and graphics

Accessible color contrast and visual design

Descriptive links and navigational elements

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding.
