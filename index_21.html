#7291. How brands are banking on live shopping this holiday season

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

The story was first published by Digiday sibling Glossy

Many brands have accepted that, to effectively compete this holiday season, last year’s marketing playbook won’t cut it.

More brands are gravitating toward live shopping, a growing channel that is set to play an outsized influence on shopping trends. According to data from eMarketer, livestream retail sales in the U.S. are set to reach nearly $20 billion this year. And annual spend per buyer on livestreams is growing by 23% to an average of $339.27.

Brands like Tarte Cosmetics, Crocs, Beachwaver and Gap are planning to make use of live shopping, particularly on TikTok, this holiday season. Tarte and Crocs plan to launch exclusive products just for TikTok live shopping, with Crocs’ exclusives including a Disney collaboration. TikTok has seen livestream shopping sales double in the first half of this year, and use of the hashtag #holidayshopping rose 263% year over year in October.

Ziad Bon, founder of Oudware, the 3-year-old fragrance retailer that sells imported fragrances from the Middle East, told Glossy that live shopping has become an integral part of his company’s holiday strategy. Oudware already has a fairly comprehensive strategy, hosting live shopping shows on weekdays for its 215,000 followers. From Black Friday through mid-December, Oudware will be increasing its live frequency, going live on TikTok Shop every day from 2 p.m. to 7 p.m. EST. Oudware already makes more than $200,000 per month from live shopping on average and does more than $20 million in annual revenue. Its products sell for around $30-$100 per bottle.

Bon said it’s important for brands engaging in live shopping to have a plan for each live. Oudware’s live strategy is to spend the first hour of each live with what Bon called the “gift hotline,” where the team takes feedback and requests from viewers on which items and fragrances they want to see go on sale that evening. The next few hours are spent showcasing products and putting them up for sale through the regular TikTok Shop sales channel. The last hour or two of each live are reserved for auctions, a feature on TikTok Shop announced in 2025 and rolled out more broadly this year.

For Oudware, which sells about 3,000 products per day on TikTok Shop livestreams, saving the auctions for last is crucial. Live shopping conversion rates tend to be much higher than standard e-commerce, and consumers are increasingly drawn to auctions due to the possibility of lower prices. Adobe data from this year found that people are nearly 70% more likely to buy something during a live shopping stream if they see others bidding as well.

“Personally, I hate auctions,” Bon said. “A lot of our fragrance manufacturers don’t want their fragrances sold at auction, and the prices are often super low. But as a seller, it’s really hard to get engagement if you’re not doing auctions, since that’s what the customers want.”

Running auctions only at the end of the live has two purposes: It lets Oudware spend the majority of a livestream selling products at their regular prices and saves the auctions for when the live has the most viewers. More viewers means more bids, which means Oudware can sell products at auction for closer to the normal asking price.

Live shopping was a big success for brands last holiday season. TikTok Shop saw over $500 million in sales throughout the holiday period in 2025. Meanwhile, on Black Friday last year, Whatnot saw $75 million in sales in one day, tripling its previous peak from the year before.

TikTok itself is also introducing new features to support live shopping for the holidays. In the past year, big name brands and retailers like Fenty Beauty and Sephora have joined TikTok Shop. Products sold on a live will now have a visible badge if they’re part of TikTok’s Fulfilled by TikTok program (similar to Amazon Prime). Bon said the ability to ship product right after a live ends and have it arrive three days later is likely to be a big draw for customers. And, starting this fall, TikTok Shop will be working with Happy Returns to let shoppers return purchased items to any of Happy Returns’ 10,000 drop-off locations across the U.S.

“Live shopping has become one of the most engaging formats on TikTok Shop, and we expect even more brands to embrace it this holiday season as they look for new ways to connect with shoppers,” said Patrick Nommensen, president of strategic initiatives at TikTok Shop, in an email to Glossy. “What makes Live particularly compelling during the holidays is its ability to turn product discovery into an interactive shopping experience, allowing consumers to watch demonstrations, ask questions, and hear directly from brands and creators before making a purchase.”

#7287. Media Buying Briefing: Buyers grapple with incoming Amazon reporting changes

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This Media Buying Briefing covers the latest in agency news and media buying for Digiday+ members and is distributed over email every Monday at 10 a.m. ET. More from the series →

Changes in the way that key media platforms measure impressions mean that performance and pricing benchmarks may be thrown into disarray come the new year.

Amazon has estimated brands will see 63% fewer impressions for display ads appearing across its retail properties, including its homepage, product pages and search results, according to a notice distributed among advertisers seen by Digiday. It’s due to the platform’s decision to shift from counting impressions the moment they begin downloading to a user’s device, to when they actually render on the user’s screen, from Jan. 1. Google announced a similar transition at the beginning of September, effective Feb. 17; spokespeople from both tech companies declined to comment.

In turn, six buyers who spoke to Digiday said they’re working to amend reporting methodologies and soothe client nerves.

Buyers fear that a reduction in reported impressions could mean that supply is constrained, driving up CPMs. “If there’s going to be fewer impressions, your CPMs will be higher,” explained Hannah Schatz, vp of programmatic operations at Coegi.

At the same time, the stricter impression reporting methodology means that lower-impact display placements further down Store pages will become even less desirable for advertisers — while top-of-page slots that stand a greater chance of influencing shoppers will see an increase in demand.

“There’s going to be probably more competition on the platform,” said Nola Ladd, awareness media supervisor at media agency Collective Measures.

Amazon has not issued guidance regarding the expected impact on pricing or costs, or whether it might update its billing methodology around the new impression reporting, according to Stagwell Media Platform’s global product managing director, Dru Sil.

“The implications are up to us to discover,” he said.

While the drop in impressions might unnerve some clients, it’s unlikely to provoke advertisers to reduce spending. Sil suggested that most advertisers will maintain spending levels, in order to establish new performance benchmarks in January and February.

“I don’t expect clients to like dramatically scale up budgets to try to get ahead of the curve. You’re likely going to continue to run what you’ve been running because you have an expected outcome that you’re trying to obtain from Amazon, regardless of the specific ad format,” said Sil.

He added: “You need to build that baseline anyway, right? And the only way to build that baseline is to be running ads.”

Benchmarking may be the real problem buyers face when they shake off the new year’s hangover.

Automated reporting processes, or media mix models that use impressions as a key input, might need to be recalibrated to account for the expected drop in reported impressions. “If you have a lot of automated processes, optimizations, reconciliations, built around these data sets already, you’re going to have to do a little bit of work,” said Paul DeJarnatt, vice president and managing director of integrated client solutions at Novus.

For most buyers, Amazon’s and Google’s tinkering with impression reporting merely underlines the importance of focusing on outcomes, rather than media KPIs, to understand campaign performance.

“The bottom line is, we don’t expect this to change campaign results. The shift will make reporting metrics look different, but it shouldn’t move ROAS or CPA, which are the outcomes our clients care about,” said Jared Smith, senior director of strategy and innovation, January Digital.

“Impressions, CPMs, they shouldn’t be north star metrics,” said Ernie DeColte, svp of media at indie agency Level. “They’re tool metrics we use to optimize towards business impact. This might be a wake-up call to a few advertisers. Maybe it’s time to think about buying differently.”

Still, communicating this change to clients who might be alarmed when they see impression counts fall off a cliff (or CPMs spike) is something media buyers across the industry are attempting to get ahead of.

“The client, even though that metric is misleading, is used to seeing a big number,” said Ladd.

“They’re used to seeing really low CPMs. They’re used to seeing really great impression volume, and that change and that difference is going to require agencies and media experts to really think about the way that they present success to the client,” she added.

Just because it’s got a donut graph doesn’t mean a report is saying very much. According to an Experian survey of 400 marketers, the profusion of dashboards and data isn’t helping marketers back up their decisions.

“In the past, a company like Unilever, buying big media in Brazil from TV Globo, had a significant competitive advantage. Now you are buying from Meta or from Google or from Amazon or from Walmart or from TikTok, and that model doesn’t favor a scale of purchase. It favors the quality of engagement you generate on the platform.”

—Unilever CEO Fernando Fernandez, speaking during its Capital Markets Day last week, on why better creative could be the secret to making its marketing operation work harder.

#7283. What it will take for CMOs to reach the next frontier of AI-driven growth

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

CMOs at the largest enterprise brands across consumer goods, financial services, B2B and more are all struggling with the same thing: more AI technology has not translated into more business growth. They’re stuck chasing efficiency gains, funding tools that optimize tasks inside functional silos, running pilots that go nowhere and delegating AI decisions to IT teams.

But a few CMOs are doing it differently. Eric Schwartz, CMO of The Clorox Company, told me his marketers are building AI systems that produce highly optimized product content. They’re encoding brand voice, compliance guardrails and seasonal context into agents that work across every brand in their portfolio. Their goal isn’t to save time on doing what human writers can already do. Instead, they want to codify, scale and orchestrate the expertise that increases relevance, engagement and conversion with consumers. That’s a growth play.

This is an operating-model decision that the CMO has to own: give marketers the strategic, system-building work and shift execution to AI agents. That takes much more than a chatbot built for a solo work experience.

“I tell everyone that tries to pitch me about AI capabilities that the last thing I need is a 14th chatbot to save our marketers time,” Schwartz said. “What I need is interoperable functionality.”

Schwartz isn’t alone. A vanguard of marketers are charting the frontier of AI-native marketing. To do this requires three fundamental shifts.

Employees are becoming more efficient with AI, but report that leaders aren’t asking them to reinvest the productivity gains into strategic work; they’re just expected to produce more output. Boston Consulting Group found that 66% of frontline employees receive limited or no guidance on using saved time, and more than half don’t reinvest it into strategic work.

Measuring AI success solely on efficiency invites efficiency’s reward: a smaller budget.

For example, one CMO’s AI-powered content system is saving the company 500 hours a month. But the company’s CFO saw the dashboard and asked: “If marketing can do this with 500 fewer hours, why does the budget stay the same?” The dashboard was supposed to prove value. Instead, it made the case for a cut.

A marketing team at a regulated financial services company chose a different approach. The team was losing 360 hours a week to legal review bottlenecks, manual multi-channel content creation and constant product updates across 800-plus references. The team built a system with compliance encoded into the workflow and distribution automated across 25 channels. The result: 360 hours freed up weekly, 90% less editing time and zero agency spend.

The marketing team reinvested those hours into producing an award-winning ad campaign, not producing more of the same content. In a category where most consumers can’t name three financial lenders, breaking through the sameness drove brand recall, pipeline and market share. The system handled the production, but the humans did the work that won.

The AI opportunity isn’t time saved. Frontier CMOs reinvest that time in architecture, curation and orchestration: designing agentic workflows, encoding brand voice and steering the system to drive growth. The humans who know best what makes their brand different turn saved time into a real advantage.

Historically, marketing and revenue have operated on separate tracks. In B2B, marketing fills the top of the funnel and hands off to sales. In consumer brands, marketing creates content while commerce manages the shelf. In financial services, brand marketing and the advisors who own the relationship rarely connect. In most organizations, no single team owns the moments that decide the sale.

Sixty-seven percent of B2B buyers now prefer a rep-free experience, according to data from Gartner. Consumers and bank customers are doing their own research too. In financial services, buyers are asking AI tools for recommendations before they ever talk to an advisor. These buyers aren’t waiting for a handoff.At the frontier, there are no handoffs. AI agents carry customer data, market signals and brand and compliance context between marketing and revenue teams in real time. Context doesn’t die at a cross-functional boundary. It compounds.

Schwartz described what this looks like at Clorox. The company’s media ecosystem spans retail media, e-commerce and traditional retail, and the goal is to connect performance back to sales — or to the signals that point to sales. Most marketing organizations stop at creating and deploying campaigns. Schwartz wants that signal flowing back to show where to lean in and scale and to shape what gets created next. By closing that loop, a media budget compounds into growth instead of disappearing.

A CMO recently told me his organization has 40 active pilots, all marked green. Everything worked in the lab, but nothing survived contact with the organization. IT loved the tools, but marketing couldn’t use them.

This happens when AI adoption is delegated to IT. The CMO sponsors the budget but doesn’t own the transformation. In Writer’s 2026 survey, more than half of CMOs (52.3%) reported “significant knowledge gaps” about AI. Gartner predicts that by 2027, lack of AI literacy will rank among the top three reasons CMOs are replaced.

At the frontier, the CMO owns the entire marketing AI agenda: selecting tools, redesigning workflows and deciding what should remain human-led. That means making AI adoption a cultural shift, not a software rollout. And that can’t be delegated.

Schwartz is an example of what this looks like in practice. He made AI adoption his own rather than delegating it to an innovation team or task force.

The CMOs making progress are changing how the work gets done, moving from traditional marketing operations toward a new model of driving growth with AI. They aren’t simply doing the same work with AI layered on top, but reimagining foundational structures, systems and strategies for a new era of marketing. The frontier isn’t a place a CMO arrives at. Affordability remains a chief concern, but traditional holiday values like human connection and togetherness are what will move the needle for brands this season, according to Deb Freeman, head of strategy at Gut New York.

“The holidays in general are being more cherished by people,” Freeman said. “Marketers are really trying to understand how to take their brand DNA and plug it into the engine of this holiday spirit in a way that is going to come out on the other side full of humanity.”

For many brands, this year’s holiday season is particularly high stakes as they contend with year-round sluggish sales. Playbooks have included a focus on handmade ad creative and creator tie-ups, along with strategic ways to help consumers comb through an oversaturation of deals. Brands are also prioritizing younger audiences including Gen Z and millennials, cohorts that for the first time will make up the bulk of holiday sales, per Deloitte.

One of the chief questions to be answered this season is how much — and in what capacity — consumers are interested in AI within the shopping experience. Over one-third of consumers report being very or extremely willing to use generative AI tools to help with holiday shopping, according to Gartner research shared with Marketing Dive. The move, which aligns with the beauty purveyor's longtime focus on individuality, represents its first holiday campaign.

“When we looked at the holiday landscape, we saw a sea of sameness and a real opportunity to bring something different to the table,” said CMO Oshiya Savur, in emailed comments.

“These tools are not replacing the more traditional search channels or the traditional places where people tend to start their shopping journeys, like good old Google Search, Amazon, friends and family wish lists and other people’s real life recommendations — it’s just that we can’t mine real-life recommendations for data,” Weiss said.

As they plan around evolving consumer behavior and map out ways to leverage flashy tactics, Gut’s Freeman emphasizes that at the center of every strategy should be a focus on the values that define the season.

“People are looking for connection,” Freeman said in an emailed statement. “They want stories they can relate to, laugh about and feel sentimental toward. They want to feel like they're part of something.”

Developments last week showed that Google faces another potential antitrust setback, this time in Europe, as it prepares for the next stage of its U.S. ad tech monopoly case.

On Nov. 12, an adviser to the European Union’s highest court recommended overturning a 2024 judgment that annulled an earlier €1.49 billion ($1.7 billion) fine against Google over its AdSense for Search business.

In her ⁠opinion, Advocate General Juliane Kokott argued that the EU’s General Court made legal errors when it overturned the European Commission’s original 2019 penalty. Specifically, Kokott concluded that the lower court had imposed excessive requirements on regulators when assessing whether Google’s contractual restrictions harmed competition.

The case concerns agreements Google used with publishers that displayed search advertisements through its AdSense for Search service; i.e., it involves a different business unit than the one under scrutiny in its most recent U.S. trial.

Per the European proceedings’ plaintiffs, between 2006 and 2016, Google imposed contractual restrictions that limited those websites’ ability to display advertisements from rival search advertising providers.

The European Commission identified three types of restrictions: exclusivity clauses preventing publishers from using competing services, placement clauses restricting where rival advertisements could appear, and provisions requiring Google’s prior approval before changes to advertising displays.

The Commission concluded that these practices constituted a single, continuous infringement of European competition law. Google challenged the decision, and in September 2024, the General Court annulled the fine, finding that regulators had failed to examine relevant circumstances adequately, particularly the duration of Google’s contractual arrangements.

Kokott’s opinion, published Nov. 12, argued that the General Court had placed excessive emphasis on the duration of individual agreements rather than considering their collective effect on competition.

In the accompanying press release, the court explained that regulators could assess contracts collectively to determine whether they could produce exclusionary effects. Kokott recommended setting aside the 2024 judgment and returning the case to the General Court for reconsideration.
