#7308. Campaign Trail: Wet Ones puts ‘hand butts’ on the streets

The Edgewell brand worked with BBH USA on an out-of-home campaign it hoped would cause some consumers to do a double take.

It’s cold and flu season, which means it's prime time for consumers to reach for antibacterial hand wipes. And while Wet Ones has been keeping hands clean consistently over the last half century, the Edgewell Personal Care brand had not released a major campaign in years.

Wet Ones decided to change that, seeking to create something fresh, bold and different that could capture the attention of a younger audience. The brand teamed with BBH USA and kept the brief loose: get the brand back out there and in conversations. The result, “Just For Hands,” launched last month and grew from a simple insight: More than 50% of consumers have used hand wipes on an intimate area, according to research commissioned by the brand.

“It was a bit surprising to us because it wasn't something that we had necessarily seen before,” said Nicole Harris, head of marketing for sun and skin at Edgewell. “We can do something with this. This is funny. How can we play on this?”

The insight about how consumers were using hand wipes inspired early rounds of work that played off inanimate objects like peaches. But BBH kept pushing and landed on an idea that the team calls “hand butts:” extreme close-ups of fingers that look like posteriors.

“We wanted to create a double take – that split second when you think you're seeing one thing, and then realize you're looking at something completely different,” said Yohan Daver, executive creative director at BBH USA.

“The craft had to make the illusion feel completely real, and by taking the idea across New York City at scale, we're turning one clever visual device into a much bigger cultural moment,” Daver added.

Created with photographer Ale Burset, a winner of more than 60 Cannes Lions awards, the campaign had a comprehensive out-of-home media plan including LinkNYC kiosks, transit shelters, digital newsstands, wild postings, bus wrapping and a Time Square takeover. Plus, interactive walls dispensed travel packs and individual wipes to pedestrians.

Originally, Wet Ones’ media plan included ads throughout the New York City subway system, where the variety of handheld surfaces like railings and straps provide a “torture test” for the product, Harris explained. That changed when the Metropolitan Transportation Authority vetoed the “Just For Hands” creative.

“When they refused us for being a little too risque, it was perfect,” Harris said. “It added juiciness to the story, but it also forced us to think quickly in terms of the other touch points. How do we want to evolve the media plan to still have the impact, but also take advantage of this from a storytelling perspective?”

Wet Ones’ PR team used the rejection as an angle to drive earned media. The brand also partnered with Kyle Cooke, of reality TV series “Summer House,” for a social stunt that brought a hand butt to celebrity gossip account DeuxMoi. Then, the “Just For Hands” campaign became the target of some friendly banter from Goodwipes, a brand of flushable wipes — not hand wipes — that isn’t a Wet Ones competitor.

“We [wanted] to get a response up in Times Square, so that was probably a record that I've seen, in my tenure for the company, for us to turn around a piece of creative and get it up within hours,” Harris said. That type of speed and agility was central to the “Just For Hands” campaign.

“We kicked it off at the end of August, so really fast turn out of the gate, but [it] led to amazing work,” Harris said. “We're getting great consumer response, but our retailers were really excited about it, too.”

#7292. WTF is the AI training opt-out policy token?

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This article is a WTF explainer, in which we break down media and marketing’s most confusing terms. More from the series →

Some of the largest AI tech companies — including Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI — have agreed to support a new mechanism that lets publishers opt out of having their content used to train AI models, in just a few lines of text.

The proposed standard, called a “policy token” for AI training, comes as publishers seek more control over how tech companies scrape and use their content. They’ve tried blocking AI bots, pursuing legal action and negotiating licensing deals.

At first glance, this AI training policy token appears to offer a straightforward fix. In reality, it highlights why the problem is so complicated.

The idea is that publishers can add a line to their robots.txt file — which websites use to tell web crawlers what content they are allowed to access — to express whether they want their content used for AI training.

Here’s what the policy token looks like, as written in robots.txt: User-Agent: __usage_ai-training

Below that, publishers can write “disallow” or “allow,” to show which crawlers can scrape and which can’t. In the “allow” line, publishers can also offer certain exceptions, such as letting crawlers scrape a press kit.

Typically, publishers need to list each crawler individually in robots.txt and give permission (or not) to crawl their sites. The new AI training policy token would allow publishers to do so in one step. Instead of managing separate instructions for every crawler, they can set broad preferences to participating AI and tech companies.

The proposal was written by Kevin Kelley, a technical lead for standards at Anthropic. Kelley submitted the proposal to the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), an internet standards development organization, on Oct. 30.

Technically, the standard is not official yet. The IETF launched an effort known as AI Preferences (AIPREF) to develop new standards for website owners to express preferences about how their content gets used for AI. Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI have all been involved in the group.

While the largest search and AI providers have agreed to support this policy token, it still needs to be deliberated on by the IETF. And the fact that these major companies have jumped to support this draft raises some concerns, said Alex Springer, technical lead at SPUR, a coalition of publishers working to develop AI standards.

“The IETF exists to allow for individuals to work in public to form consensus about the standards and protocols that make the internet work. The way that the platforms have approached this by announcing their unilateral support of a ‘standard’ that is an unreviewed draft is the antithesis of that,” he said. “They appear to have decided to unilaterally wield their collective weight in a concerted effort to force a new de facto standard in place, one that falls far short of what those content owners require.”

Anthropic says it is rolling out adoption of the signal now.

In a blog post, OpenAI said it would honor the policy token “in the coming months.” It noted that the proposed mechanism would not replace its existing crawler controls.

Google wrote in its own blog post that it aims to support this policy token in 2027. It noted that it builds on the options it already offers publishers, such as Google-Extended, a web crawler control implemented in 2023 that allows publishers to use robots.txt to restrict their content from being used to train Google’s Gemini AI models and ground responses in certain AI products.

Microsoft wrote in a blog post that not only would it comply with the AI training policy token but that it would also offer publishers the ability to opt out of AI grounding, or when crawlers scrape sites to find real-time information. These preferences will replace the existing Microsoft preferences “NoArchive” and “NoCache,” for AI training and grounding respectively, starting Feb. 1, 2027.

Meta didn’t go as far as to say it would honor the policy token, but Andy Stone, a Meta company spokesperson, wrote on X: “Meta, too, strongly support publishers’ ability to express preferences about how their content is used for foundational generative AI training. We believe creating a common signal that conveys such preferences to multiple providers at once is an important step. And we look forward to working with other AI companies on the standards and their implementation across the industry.”

The proposal may appeal to publishers seeking standardized controls. But, like robots.txt, it offers no way to ensure AI and tech companies comply with publishers’ preferences.

Robots.txt preferences are infamously more of a suggestion than an enforceable doctrine.

In fact, data suggests that web crawlers rarely even check publishers’ robots.txt files when scraping their sites. Crawlers operated by the four companies backing the shared opt-out token made roughly 1 billion requests over the past 30 days — but just 0.15% of those requests were for robots.txt files, according to data shared with Digiday by AI visibility measurement firm ScalePost, which tracked activity across more than 50 publisher properties.

Paul Bannister, chief strategy officer at Raptive, called the AI training policy token “window dressing.”

“I think this is useless and is marketing, and far, far, far too late… It does nothing and doesn’t solve the problem at all,” he said.

AI tech companies have a lot of reasons to want to appear to appease publishers. They’re fighting a number of copyright infringement lawsuits, pressure from government regulators, and proposed legislation targeting crawlers.

#7268. Why publishers must act now to protect content from AI scrapers

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Each technology wave brings the same promise: reach, relevance and a seat at the table. When the web arrived, publishers posted content to gain exposure, then spent a decade trying to claw back the revenue they gave away. When social media arrived, publishers threw open their archives to platforms that captured attention, data and advertising dollars. But each wave has left publishers with lower revenues, smaller newsrooms and a greater dependence on big tech platforms.

AI is the latest and potentially most disruptive wave. AI companies have spent four years crawling the web at industrial scale, ingesting decades of journalism to train models that answer questions directly instead of sending readers back to the source. Content that publishers built and funded is being taken without permission, compensation or attribution. AI companies have little incentive to pay while they can access publisher content for free.

Media leaders understand the risk. Yet research from digital analytics and customer engagement solutions provider Piano shows that fewer than 20% of the company’s 500 publisher customers use aggressive AI-bot blocking, while 30% use none at all. Without blocking, there’s no leverage to negotiate.

Some publishers worry that blocking AI means losing the chance to be discovered through AI. But less than 0.1% of publisher traffic comes from AI tools, according to data from Piano. Large language models keep users inside their own environments, so most readers never click through to the source. Anthropic sends back one visit for every 8,692 pages it scrapes; Perplexity, one for every 369. Until those numbers change, appearing in AI results doesn’t bring meaningful value.

Google’s pivot to AI Overviews has added to the pressure. Since AI Overviews launched in May 2024, clicks to publisher sites are down 49%. At Google I/O 2026 in May, Google unveiled a more AI-centric search experience that puts its chatbot-style AI Mode at the center of the search box, potentially pushing the traditional 10 blue links further down the page.

If organic search — currently 25% of traffic to Piano customers, according to internal benchmark data — continues to erode, the economic case for producing original content could collapse. Likewise, as long as AI companies can scrape content for free, they have no incentive to pay.

The path to compensation in the AI era runs through scarcity — and scarcity requires taking action like blocking bots and locking down content to create leverage. Then, publishers can negotiate.Here are four steps publishers can take now to create a path to compensation in the AI era.

Publishers can limit AI companies’ access to their content by declaring exclusions in robots.txt, blocking at the content delivery network (CDN) level, and locking paywalls to recognize only human visitors.

Some AI companies respect publisher rules, but many crawlers don’t. Piano’s analysis of Cloudflare data shows 70% of bot traffic is unverified. Perplexity has been documented circumventing blocking by impersonating Chrome browsers. Tavily openly admits its crawler skips a distinctive user agent specifically to dodge rules that let only Google crawl a site.

Basic bot-blocking may not be enough. Content protection ultimately means locking content for everyone, human or not, because the industry can’t reliably distinguish AI agents from human readers. One of the most secure approaches is requiring all readers to register, using server-side or CDN edge protection to block content from any unknown user.

An agentic commerce future in which AI systems pay publishers per crawl, per answer or per piece of content is real, but it’s not here yet. Platforms like TollBit and Pro Rata and standards like x402 and IAB’s Content Monetization Protocols are developing the frameworks. But no one is making meaningful money from bot marketplaces yet.

A defensive strategy won’t drive growth on its own. The same AI companies threatening publishers also enable valuable tools, including personalization at scale and AI-powered subscription monetization. The next generation of predictive systems will auto-optimize in real time, cutting manual work.

At the same time, an industry that spent 20 years unable to coordinate is starting to unify around how its content is used. The Standards for Publisher Usage Rights (SPUR) initiative is the clearest signal: 30 new members joined at WAN-IFRA’s World News Media Congress in June. The Real Simple Licensing Collective and the News Media Alliance are building similar infrastructure.

Publishers are also taking a harder line on how AI companies access their content. The Atlantic and People Inc. have put aggressive bot-blocking into practice. At the WAN-IFRA June conference, The New York Times’ publisher called on the industry to stop giving content away and demand fair terms.

Publishers should use AI where it creates value while retaining control over the content that powers these systems. #7253. Your Brand Is Your Most Powerful Differentiator

Being an independent agency isn’t the advantage it used to be. There are simply too many choices.

And we can’t differentiate on capabilities anymore either. Most of us offer some combination of strategy, creative, media, production and now AI. And if we don’t have it all (and we’re smart), we have an explanation for how we can scale up when needed.

After more than four decades of leading agencies, I have some advice for my fellow founders and agency leaders: Don’t forget to invest in your own brand.

I know it sounds obvious. But I’ve lived through what happens when you let your own brand rest on its laurels because you’re busy building everyone else’s. The result is you end up having to work a lot harder to show people what you’re capable of, because they’ve already formed a perception.

So let’s invest in our brands so they can work for us. That starts by owning your story.

Ours began in 2004, when I led a management buyout to save our agency from disappearing. Our original 40 employees, who we still call the “Brave 40,” believed in what we were building and decided to stay, and they even pooled their own money to buy the sign that would hang above our door.

We were a little agency from Rochester, New York, determined to make the industry see that great ideas can come from anywhere. We were scrappy and smart, and a little rascally. The kind of agency you never saw coming.

That spirit is still in us, but we’ve grown up a lot over the last 22 years, too. In 2012, we sold to Project Worldwide to gain the ability to scale, because their Detroit roots matched our grit, and because their independent and employee-owned model allowed us to keep our soul. As we grew, we raised the bar across our business, from the talent we attracted, to our work, and the brands we worked with.

Along the way, our brand stopped keeping up with who we’d become, and it also lost the soul that gave us our start. We needed to reflect the agency we’d become while protecting the equity and reputation we’d built.

Now, here’s something that might surprise you: I didn’t lead our rebrand.

I’ve always believed in leading from a place of abundance, and making room for more people and ideas. That means trusting the people around you to see things you might not.

I let go, and the rest of the leadership team led the rebrand with the freedom to challenge everything, including the name on the door.

Keeping my name wasn’t part of the brief. In fact, my only request was that they not call us P+N.

The team brought in outside consultants to challenge their thinking and tested the new positioning with search consultants to understand what resonated and what needed work.

One piece of feedback came through over and over: Whatever you’re saying, go further. Don’t be afraid to be provocative.

It was a good reminder that in an industry where we’re all selling creativity in one way or another, how we tell our own story is our lifeline. We can’t afford to sound like everyone else.

So while many agencies are moving away from founder names in favor of names that describe what they deliver, we went the other direction.

The team chose NAPIER because it represented a mindset and a spirit that had been with us since 2004. That founder who risked everything because she believed in her team, and the people who stuck by her to bring it to life. We’ve always had to outthink, outwork and outshine agencies many times our size. And now we’re owning our roots in a new way, with a simpler name that carries more power.

We also got much clearer about who we’re for. We’re not going after every challenger brand, we’re focused on “sleeping giants”: brands that have strong equity or scale, but aren’t realizing their full potential. These are brands who need to break free from familiar patterns and move differently. It’s what we’ve always done best, but now we have a sharper and more interesting way to articulate it.

On October 22, after 22 years as Partners + Napier, we officially unveiled our new name, visual identity and positioning. It’s been surreal to watch it all come to life, and it’s also proved what I’ve long believed about leading with abundance.

You have to know what makes you special, but you also have to give others room to see possibilities you might not. That’s how you build something that can keep growing beyond you and actually become a legacy.

We are in the business of building brands. We challenge clients to have conviction, to evolve, to get clearer about who they are and to make choices that will set them apart.

So to my fellow agency leaders: Don’t wait for a major business disruption to take a fresh look at your brand. And don’t assume that because you know what makes your agency special, everyone else does too.

Give your own brand the attention you’d give your most important client.

And give the people you’ve brought into your business the room to make it even better.

#7217. The AI-or-Not Debate Is Advertising’s Most Pointless Argument

The most expensive finger in marketing belongs to a cartoon character in “Night in Color,” Dior’s new animated holiday short.

Last week, viewers noticed that character had one joint too many, along with some warped lettering and a lazy eye. Sure enough, the internet outrage machine started getting frothy again.

Online commenters concluded the film was made with generative AI, usually something that CMOs boast about. But it matters here because Jonathan Anderson, Dior’s creative director, had made a big thing about it not being AI, telling Women’s Wear Daily it was “all hand-drawn.”

The film itself is terrific, which is half the trouble. Sylvain Chomet directed a talking-animal fairy tale about Christian Dior and Marlene Dietrich, and Dior released a making-of to show off the craft. Then Ben Wild, who runs a film studio with Netflix and Disney on its client list, said two people who claim to have worked on it told him the motion was largely AI generated, and the furniture and the bees entirely so.

Dior has confirmed none of that. It hasn’t denied it either, and as of Friday three outlets had asked and heard nothing. Director Chomet has denied the use of generative AI but acknowledged on Friday its use to smooth out the animation. But the allegation is possibly the least interesting part of the story, because the argument we are now all having is a fairly ridiculous one.

Almost nothing you watch or read is AI free any more.

Your phone blends several frames before you have finished pressing the button. Photoshop will cheerfully invent the other half of a sky. AutoTune has been quietly rescuing pop vocals for more than 25 years. Your video captions are automatic and the dialogue in the edit suite was cleaned by machine learning. Every sentence of the column you are reading has been read by Claude to check for grammar and factual errors.

The monochrome disparity of real and artificial ended around 2024, a gradient that runs from a bit of noise reduction at one end to a creature imagined and drawn entirely by machine at the other, and anyone who tells you there is a clean line between these points is either selling something or full of shit.

The only sensible questions are where on the gradient you sit and whether you are willing to say so.

Does it matter if a bee was generated? Probably not. Does it matter if you said it wasn’t? Very much.

And if your brand DNA is forged from human creativity and hand-made, even more so.

There is a reason Dior wanted to claim it was hand-drawn. In a feed drowning in machine video, visible/fallible human effort has become a luxury cue. Prada, Porsche, Levi’s and Apple all went manual for the same reason. And Dior wants to be at the same luxurious, hand-drawn party. But a cue only works if it is expensive, and expensive takes time. Wild’s version is that Dior rushed into the film after hearing Prada had an animation legend on board, but you can’t speed up things like this. At least not without AI.

If Dior had said early on that a team of artists drew the keyframes and a machine helped with the gaps, I would not be writing this column. A few purists would have grumbled and everyone would have admired the bees and appreciated that Dior was a modern brand. Instead, it made a claim of pure manual labor, and that became the point of the ad—and its endemic flaw.

Luxury, more than any other category, cannot afford to be caught out. A mass brand can get away with it because nobody expects a Doritos ad to be drawn by monks. But luxury sells provenance first and product second, so provenance is the product, and a story about hands that turns out to be about graphics cards doesn’t just damage an ad’s reception, it invites people to revisit how they think about the brand.

Which is a shame. Because this was a refreshing idea from Anderson. And a lovely bit of advertising. But the bigger lesson is that this debate about AI or not AI is possibly the most pointless in all of advertising right now. And brands do well to avoid it completely. The work is the work. Boasting that it is “AI generated” or claiming that it is “entirely hand-made” are both pointless and increasingly impossible positions to adopt.
