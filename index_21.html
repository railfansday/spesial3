#7205. Dick’s Sporting Goods is turning youth sports into a premium ad business

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Dick’s Sporting Goods has found that there’s big business in youth sports.

The sporting retailer recorded a third quarter GAAP net income of $161 million in earnings this week — more than double the $75 million reported the year prior.

Dick’s execs attributed much of that success to Dick’s Media Network, home to its youth sports app GameChange.

Like many long-tail retail media networks, Dick’s Sporting Goods doesn’t break out its ad business revenue by dollar figure or percentage. Dick’s chief financial officer Navdeep Gupta noted that the company is grappling with supply-chain related costs, higher fuel costs and other pressures, but Dick’s Media Network partially offset those pressures.

“We have talked about these gross margin growth drivers with Dick’s Media Network and GameChanger. Those are continuing to be very differentiated drivers of not only our profitability, but our gross margin expansion,” he said.

Dick’s Media Network is four years old and offers ad units in-store, on its own site, off-site across the open web, social and streaming. Perhaps its biggest get is its live sports via GameChanger.

“They found this gap in the marketplace in terms of sports club video streaming. And they’ve created this app, this solution, and now they inherently have their own O&O [owned and operated] inventory from a media perspective,” said Olivia Hawkins, vp of media strategy at Salt XC.

Dick’s has previously worked with sports brands like Adidas, On, Under Armor and Crocs. However, the sports retailer is actively engaging non-endemic partners too.

Take Chobani, for example. The yogurt brand bought ad space on Dick’s Sporting Goods Game Changer app during the 2026 FIFA World Cup, said Hawkins, who was familiar with the deal.

She did not disclose financial details of the ad buy. The campaign focused on reach and awareness, getting Chobani in front of youth sports enthusiasts, like parents and coaches, Hawkins said.

“They met our expectations in terms of reaching a new audience and delivery, frequency, and all those media metrics — CPM and video completions and all that,” she said, without detailing the campaign’s success.

Dick’s video app CPMs are more premium, hovering around $35, according to one commerce executive who spoke on the condition of anonymity.

“That’s more expensive than big TV. They’re asking for that price for that type of inventory. It’s a great solution — super clever, but it’s expensive,” the anonymous exec told Digiday. In comparison, Netflix CPMs fell from their initial $60 when it launched in 2022 to the low $20s currently.

Seemingly, brands are willing to cough up the dough. That’s the bet Dick’s seems to be making. It makes sense given sports has become the industry’s latest golden goose.

Put some numbers on it. In a survey this year, Dick’s Media and eMarketer reported that 63% of U.S. parents have kids in youth sports and 84% of them said youth sports influence their buying choices.

Now, there’s the question of scale. Dick’s GameChanger app has more than 5 million monthly active users, but youth sports is more niche than something like the National Football League, which averages 18.7 million viewers per game during the regular season.

“It’s a great audience. It’s a great inventory, but it is finite versus other offsite environments that include all of the open web,” Hawkins said.

That leaves Dick’s in the same boat as other retail media networks — or at least every other RMN that isn’t Amazon or Walmart — where continued growth may have to come from beyond retailers’ owned and operated channels.

#7199. Brands race to systematize how to know what makes creator content worth paying for

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Social media trends move faster than brands can keep up with. Now, they’re trying to create repeatable systems to turn organic social attention into something they can evaluate, fund and ultimately scale.

Take General Mills, for example. Home to brands like Cheerios, Cinnamon Toast Crunch and Trix, the CPG brand has spent the past year putting together an internal framework to systematize monetizing viral moments.

Its “talkability score” is a calculation used to measure whether General Mills’ brands are earning more attention with social media creators than they’re buying with paid ads. It’s a loose framework that varies by brand, Mindy Murray, director of influencer and content at General Mills said, and generates a score based on whether users shared it, put their own spin on it and how long interest in the trend lasts.

Murray was mum on the actual mechanics on how it actually determines whether to invest ad spend. “We’re weighing on earning, we’re weighing things like active engagement — so shares likes, comments, saves — because we know that that means someone took an actual effort to do something versus just an impression,” Murray said.

Virality can’t be manufactured, but this “operating system” allows them to “go faster and be smarter,” said Murray.

Over the past year, General Mills has also reorganized its teams to bring social and influencer marketing under Murray’s purview, she told Digiday, to move more quickly and efficiently in converting buzzy organic content into paid ads.

For example, over the summer Reese’s “Eat ‘Em Up” cereal jingle from the early 2000s started trending on TikTok. General Mills noticed the trend, partnered with GloRilla to re-record the rap, tracked real-time activity via its framework, and ultimately turned it into a campaign on streaming platforms and Reese’s Puffs’ YouTube channel. The company boasted a 2.5% year-over-year sales increase as a result, but did not provide specific sales figures. The company did not say specifically how the framework informed that strategy.

Last week, influencer agency Billion Dollar Boy (BDB) rolled out its Creator Investment Planner, a financial modeling tool that aims to help marketers understand the ad spend levels required to plan and “de-risk creator investments,” according to a news release.

BDB’s tool works by calculating blended CPMs from creators and social media platforms. Marketers enter their budget and time frame, and the platform calculates estimates for share of voice, total impressions and earned media value. Per BDB, the model can update as inputs change, where marketers can expect to “de-risk” spend commitments.

And in April, CreatorIQ creator platform and creative analytics and governance platform CreativeX launched a new tool to help brands incorporate creator content into a paid media strategy. The system works on an API, sending creator content from CreatorIQ’s platform to CreativeX to be graded, reviewed by CreativeX’s AI-powered system and optimized by a brand campaign manager. Ultimately, it aims to identify creator posts with potential to do well as an ad before any media buyers open their wallets. Nestlé was the first brand to sign on. The integration was announced on an invite-only basis.

To agency execs, all signs point to brands getting more rigorous about their spend — especially when it comes to squeezing performance out of creators — and do it before the trend disappears.

Creator spend is expected to increase 12% every year until 2030, according to advertising research firm Madison & Wall. In no small part, brand spend on boosting or promoting creator content is fueling that growth.

“Before any sort of budget is unlocked, you have to be able to prove out that there’s a higher propensity for that piece of content for whatever investment you’re making into social to actually move towards a conversion,” said Madison Gaudry-Routledge, evp of social at Viral Nation, a social agency.

Most brands, Gaudry-Routledge said, haven’t come up with a named framework in the same way General Mills has. Brand spend on creator marketing is expected to grow twice as fast as spending on advertising. Naturally, brands are eager to see proof of concept as early as possible.

Viral Nation has its own proprietary framework offered to clients as an add-on service. It’s called Culture Quotient that blends brand awareness, brand affinity and brand velocity into a measurable score to determine a brand’s relevance in cultural conversation. The tool works via AI that analyzes conversations and content, identifies emerging cultural trends and tracks shifts in consumer sentiment, per Gaudry-Routledge. The social exec declined to share any client-specific examples.

These calculations are being built in real time and haven’t yet replaced traditional metrics like share of voice, audience sentiment and engagement.

“As an industry, we’re continuing to try to catch up on operationalizing frameworks and systematizing measurement and still not there yet,” said Kristina Coughlin, svp and general manager of Trevant, a creator marketing agency.

A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

The case represents one in a handful of recent jury decisions to favor employers facing Title VII lawsuits. In March, a jury held for Haribo of America in a former employee’s discrimination and retaliation case, determining that the plaintiff’s dismissal was a mutually agreed-upon separation between the parties rather than retaliation.

And in a 2025 case, a jury sided with Levi Strauss in a lawsuit alleging that a former executive did not receive a promotion because of her pregnancy, gender and age.

#7149. How colleges and schools can grow career-focused dual enrollment

Career and technical education and dual enrollment are “powerful levers” together, said a researcher at the Community College Research Center.

At DeKalb High School and New Boston High School in northeastern Texas, students in healthcare courses use mannequins to practice caring for patients, while students in welding classes fabricate real parts.

They are participating in dual-enrollment programs developed with Texarkana College that are designed to help high school students make progress toward a postsecondary credential before they graduate.

The community college works with school districts to train high school instructors to teach the college coursework. Texarkana also sends academic coaches to both high schools to help students chart a post-high school plan in their chosen field.

The partnership between the high schools and the community college serves as an example of what an October report from the Community College Research Center calls an "unprecedented opportunity” to do more to pair dual enrollment with career-focused education.

The report estimates that more than 1 million of the nation’s 3.8 million annual high school graduates are taking three or more high school credits of career and technical education, or CTE, but not taking any dual-credit courses.

Sarah Griffin, research associate at CCRC and lead author of the new paper, said that matters because 2025 research from the center found that Texas students with a CTE focus were more likely to enroll in college and complete a degree when they took dual enrollment courses in high school than those who did not.

There was also an economic payoff. By age 24, the prior research found, the CTE students who took dual enrollment classes were earning 16% more than CTE students who did not.

“CTE and dual enrollment together are really powerful levers for students because they are facilitating exploration of careers and college programs for students,” Griffin told Higher Ed Dive.

“Because they're so widespread, they're already serving large numbers of students across the country,” Griffin said. “Therefore, they're really practical for practitioners to be able to leverage.”

Shaun Dougherty, professor of education and policy at Boston College and a former senior adviser at the U.S. Department of Education, said the report's call for a greater focus on career-connected dual enrollment is “fairly aligned with current policy” at the state level.

Dougherty pointed to Colorado's Concurrent Enrollment Programs Act and Kentucky's Dual Credit Scholarship as examples of states providing a favorable policy environment for combining dual enrollment with CTE. The Colorado law covers tuition for dual-enrollment students, while the Kentucky program helps high school students in the state pay for dual credit classes at participating colleges.

“While there has been pushback on four-year college-going as a policy priority, career-aligned programs that span secondary and postsecondary levels and that focus on preparation for relevant skills have gotten far more focus,” said Dougherty.

Griffin said it’s paramount for those looking to establish career-connected CTE programs to develop a vision of the optimal dual enrollment experience for CTE students and how it will help them find their way after high school — whether that means going to a community college, a four-year college or a technical program that’s aligned with a specific job.

Griffin recommended a CCRC-developed framework called Dual Enrollment Equity Pathways for those looking to boost dual enrollment for CTE students.

Pursuing these practices to scale up dual enrollment for CTE students will pay off in the long run, Griffin said. The strategy is a practical approach for helping students earn a postsecondary credential and increasing the likelihood “they'll be able to secure a good-paying job by the age of 30,” she said.

As for how a host should plan, “You should have most of your food done by the time guests arrive,” says Newman. At the end of the day, though, as long as a guest arrives ready to keep the energy high and be gracious to their host, they can’t go wrong.

#7110. How Richmond, Virginia, schools are tackling a $680M facilities funding shortfall

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Filardo said that schools’ HVAC systems are complicated. They’re not just for temperature control, but also air filtration and dehumidification. Retrofitting older schools also presents unique challenges.

“In the 1950s, there would be one outlet in a classroom, maybe,” she said. “If you were lucky, one on each wall.”

From the states’ perspectives, Filardo sees a wide range of approaches to start addressing needs. Wyoming, for example, has used coal lease bonuses — mining company payments for extraction permits — and Massachusetts has implemented a statewide sales tax.

Meanwhile, in Virginia, Richmond was one of many seeking to boost facilities budgets. The same measure failed to pass in 32 of the 47 localities statewide.

Kristen Nye, executive director of Virginians for Modern Schools, an organization supporting the measure, said voter education proved to be very important in Richmond’s case. She added that the measure opened up opportunities that may have previously seemed out of reach.

“Let's say those plans haven't been touched in well over a decade,” Nye said. “They're sort of blowing the dust off of them and taking them back out in the community again.”

Richmond City Councilor Katherine Jordan led the city’s legislation that put the sales tax measure on the ballot.

“Very often education is a popular talking point on the campaign trail, but when it comes to budget time, somehow it just doesn't make it through,” she said.

Richmond School Board Member Katie Ricard said that if the measure had not passed, it might have been put back on the ballot for a future year. RPS might also have looked to the state for more funding.

Although the measure passed, the school district will need additional funding, said Ricard, noting that she expects the district to continue applying for grants.

“We have 42 school buildings across the district,” Ricard said. “It just gets really costly to maintain all of them. They are old, and their systems are pretty much hanging on by a paperclip, sometimes until they fail.”

Above: A hallway view of Eric Egan’s Milan apartment.

If any season has the power to inspire a complete aesthetic overhaul, it’s fall. Nature’s changing palette inspires us to follow suit, as each year our mood boards make new space for jewel tones, cozy textures, and a bottomless candle budget. Autumnal trends are so alluring, it almost feels like a risky time to make long-term design decisions. But with the right consideration, that seasonal inspiration can translate into choices that look good year-round.

This fall in particular, designers are urging clients to trust their guts—making sure they see themselves in the spaces they create, rather than blindly following the latest fads. “For me, a trend has to be authentic or it’s just noise,” says designer Chad Dorsey, who’s eager to ditch the spare minimalism of years past for looks with more personality. ELLE Decor A-Lister Kerry Joyce seconds the motion: “By prioritizing personalized storytelling, a space becomes evergreen, evolving as smaller objects are swapped out over time.”

But an expert nudge in the right direction never hurts. This fall, top designers are gravitating toward creative color-drenching, cleverly concealed storage, tactile upholstery, and rooms built around art. Read on for their takes on the fall 2026 trends worth investing in, plus tips on building (as Dorsey puts it) “spaces designed to be lived in, not looked at.”

Fabric and upholstery are trending ultra plush and textured for fall, but when thoughtfully integrated, they feel more timeless than seasonal. “Design is shifting toward how a space feels, not just how it looks,” says Joyce, who’s seeing an increased popularity in fabrics like mohair, velvet, cashmere, and alpaca hair. “This focus on texture does more than elevate a room's aesthetic,” he explains. “It creates a sensory experience that comforts the soul.”

Start your bedroom redesign this fall and your space will strike the perfect balance between fresh and lived-in by spring. Between client requests and a personal home build, Amber Lewis has bedrooms on the brain right now. One trend takes the lead in her eyes: “I love matching the walls to the bed upholstery and bedding so the whole room feels wrapped in one warm color family. It’s calm and peaceful, and that enveloping feeling works in any season.”

Luckily, this cozy trend doesn’t require a complete room overhaul: “The easiest place to start is the bed itself. Reupholster your headboard in a pattern you love, or drape a vintage suzani across the foot of the bed,” Lewis says. “One move like that changes the whole mood of the room without starting over.” To bring in a little dimension and pop, she recommends adding a singular statement, like a lone striped pillow.

If your primary suite needs a more holistic update, Lewis recommends creating a small sitting area. Sourcing vintage chairs and small sofas for primary suites this fall has given Lewis a new perspective on where bedroom design is headed: “True sanctuaries that feel layered, personal, and built for actually living in.”

Color drenching isn’t going anywhere for fall 2026—or long after, according to Dorsey. When choosing a shade for a room, he finds that any color works harder when it’s layered and intentional. Color capping—a term coined by Benjamin Moore referring to painting a room in multiple tones from one color family—can help keep a space from feeling flat or overwhelmed beneath a statement hue.

According to Dorsey, the staying power of color drenching goes hand in hand with the shift away from spareness and toward “collected” spaces: “Neither is really a ‘look;’ they’re about living with things you love, which is why they’ll carry well into spring.”

Designer Regan Baker says that cleverly hidden space solutions are also on the rise. “It could be a TV that slides behind a commissioned art panel, a Murphy bed tucked into a wall of millwork or that serves as a desk when it's stored, or a peg wall that’s a place to hang a jacket one minute and a standing desk the next,” she says. These solutions are tailored to her clients’ spaces (rather than tied to a season or palette), which gives them staying power.

This concept is an easy way to turn the corner you’ve never known how to style into your favorite part of the room. “In an existing home, look at the awkward spots, like an alcove or the wall around a fireplace, and treat built-ins there as a design moment instead of an afterthought,” Baker advises.

If there’s one built-to-last fall trend most designers can agree on, it’s added eccentricity. “Homes are steering away from the sterile, overnight look of a showroom delivery,” Joyce says. “The defining philosophy now focuses on layering modern staples with antiques, secondhand finds, travel mementos, and new pieces that carry a sense of patina.”

Dorsey describes this style as “collected rather than prescribed,” where a high-low mix of vintage and custom elements gel together. And while it may feel loftier than a trending, sold-out piece, Baker says that most clients start with an artistic anchor, then build around it: “We’re designing more homes around a piece of art than around a color trend. That might be wallpaper by local designers, a commissioned piece, or even a palette pulled from a lichen-covered rock found on the property.”

One key takeaway? Curate like a collector. Your reward may be a space that never goes out of style.

Adopting a pinwheel-type plan, the striking 14,000-square-foot house features multiple wings radiating from a central core. These spaces vary in scale and height, and some feature deep overhangs, providing shade and amplifying the design’s dynamic horizontality. “The oversailing roofs create a solar brim that helps keep the rooms cool in the summer,” says Alex Witko, a senior associate at Leroy Street who served as the project lead. “They also amplify the views, including from the primary bedroom, where you can just sit and watch the herons on the nearby spit of land that is a protected wildlife preserve.”

Softening the formal rigor, most of the house is topped with lushly planted green roofs and terraces that offer distinct vistas, while the extensively glazed exterior is clad in handmade white brick on the lower levels and dark-stained wood above. Landscape architect Ed Hollander and his firm Hollander Design collaborated on the 1.7-acre property’s outdoor living spaces, while outfitting the gardens with what he calls “maritime habitat” plantings, a mix of native and non-native species “that are adapted to this sunny, salty, breezy environment.”

Everything in the house’s design was conceived around creating a seamless flow—visual and literal—between inside and outside. The main entry consists of a two-story glass-walled gallery that connects the two largest wings, providing views from the front walkway through the house to a rear garden planted with ginkgo trees and the bay beyond. “In the morning, the sun actually comes up through that ginkgo grove and reflects off the entry hall’s glass pendant lights,” notes Hollander. “Then in the evening, the lights become like starlight, these magic things that dance against the trees.”

Inside, the light-and-dark palette continues with the white artisanal brick, expanses of warm plaster, and pale oak and stone offset by inky-hued wood and blackened-steel accents. Throughout, the predominantly new and custom furnishings, overseen by Eleanor Donnelly of Stelle Lomont Rouhani Architects, stick to a sculptural, pared-down aesthetic, complemented by a contemporary art collection that includes works by Teresita Fernández, Sarah Morris, and Adam McEwen. The horseshoe-shaped main stairwell is one of few rounded architectural elements in the house. With floating treads and a minimalist oculus at the top, there’s a play of light, shadow, and form that shifts as one moves between floors.

Reaching the home’s dramatic second-story rooftop terrace requires taking a separate, private flight of stairs. A seductive spot for sunsets and stargazing, it features a seating area with a fire bowl surrounded by wispy native grasses and plants.

#7011. Title IX sex bias employment case about to get its day in Supreme Court

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A lower court said institutions may not know when accepting federal funds that they are accepting liability under Title IX, when Title VII typically covers employment claims.

School districts could get a hint of how the U.S. Supreme Court will rule on a case that could change how teachers and other school employees bring sex discrimination claims when the justices hear oral arguments next week.

In Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, the 11th U.S. Court of Appeals ruled in 2024 that Title IX’s right to sue doesn’t apply to employees, diverging from at least eight other appeals courts. At odds is whether school employees have the right to sue privately under Title IX outside of protections under Title VII.

Title IX protects anyone who interacts with a school — including students, employees and parents — from sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs and is often invoked by students who are harassed, assaulted or bullied due to their sex. Title IX protections for school employees include, for instance, prohibiting discrimination against teachers due to pregnancy or pregnancy-related conditions, among other things.

Meanwhile, Title VII prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex and national origin.

In Crowther, two University System of Georgia employees — a basketball coach at Georgia Tech and an art professor at Augusta University — said the university system discriminated against them on the basis of sex when it fired them.

Georgia Tech women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph was fired in 2019 shortly after she filed an internal complaint that alleged the university was discriminating against her and her program by not providing the same resources as the men’s program.

At Augusta University, art professor Thomas Crowther was told his contract would not be renewed in 2021 after several students reported allegedly inappropriate conduct in his class — including sexual harassment — which triggered a Title IX investigation. That investigation process and ensuing negative performance reviews, he said, discriminated against him on the basis of him being male.

Joseph and Crowther both reported the alleged discrimination to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, but also sued in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia under Title IX.

The two cases were combined before the 11th Circuit, which said in its 2024 decision that schools may not understand when accepting federal funds that they accept potential liability for damages of Title IX claims by employees, when those types of claims are typically filed under Title VII.

The Association of Title IX Administrators called the Supreme Court case "one of the most consequential decisions impacting the intersection of Title IX and employment law in decades."

Multiple major education and legal organizations have filed briefs in the case, including the National Education Association and the National Women's Law Center.

"The American education system faces a severe and worsening crisis in educator recruitment and retention," said NEA's brief that was filed earlier this year in favor of the former University System of Georgia employees. "Anti-discrimination protections affect whether talented professionals enter, remain in, or depart from careers."

The NEA said that a court decision implicates "a nationwide educator workforce crisis, the professional environment in which recruitment and retention decisions are made, and ultimately the quality of education available to students across the country."

"When sex discrimination drives talented educators from the profession, the consequences extend beyond individual career harm and cause systemic degradation of educational quality," the teachers union said.

Granting teachers the right to sue privately under Title IX would help improve an environment where sex discrimination "has remained pervasive," the NWLC said in its own brief. The center cited teachers' high rates of sexual harassment from their colleagues and students, unequal pay for female teachers despite their overrepresentation in the profession, a lack of leadership opportunities, and pregnancy discrimination, such as a lack of accommodations and supportive school policies.

Such a decision would also help address lower investments in girls' school sports and barriers to STEM in education programs, NWLC said.

Title IX "can only be meaningful for women who work in education if they can enforce their right to be free from sex discrimination, including in court," the organization argued.

However, the Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia maintained in its opposing brief filed in October 2025 that Title IX does not include a private right to sue and that petitioners "had every chance to prove their claims for employment-based sex discrimination under Title VII."

"Had Congress desired that employees of educational institutions have a bonus remedy for employment discrimination under Title IX, unburdened by Title VII’s exhaustion requirements and damage caps, it could have provided as much," the respondents said. "It has not."

The case invokes another — Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, a 2005 Title IX case that was decided 5-4 in favor of the plaintiff.

That case was brought by Roderick Jackson, a high school girls’ basketball coach who spoke out to his supervisors after he noticed that his team was not receiving equal funding and equal access to athletic equipment and facilities. Following his complaints, he received negative work reviews and was ultimately removed from his position.

His lawsuit reached the high court, which decided that Title IX's private right to sue included retaliation claims for complaining about sex discrimination.

"Reporting incidents of discrimination is integral to Title IX enforcement and would be discouraged if retaliation against those who report went unpunished," the Supreme Court said in its decision. "Indeed, if retaliation were not prohibited, Title IX’s enforcement scheme would unravel.”

However, more than two decades later, Crowther will be heard by a much different bench, given the justices in the majority on the Jackson opinion have long since retired or died.
