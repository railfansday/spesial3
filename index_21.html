#7289. Want to Sell an Agency? Launch a Creator Marketing Firm

This story was originally published in On Background with Mark Stenberg, a free weekly newsletter that explores the key themes shaping the media industry. You can sign up for it here.

In recent months, the market for creator and social agencies has been, to put it lightly, brisk.

In September, the Madrid-based social agency Samy acquired Get Engaged, a U.S. social and influencer shop, in a deal valued in the low nine figures.

In October, Billion Dollar Boy Group bought Creator Authority, a business-to-business influencer agency, and the private equity firm Shamrock Capital, which owns ADWEEK, made an eight-figure investment in the social-first agency Saylor.

Earlier this month, the social-first agency LV8 merged with its sister company Mirage Digital on the back of a “high eight figures” investment from Velocity Capital Management. And last week, the private equity firm Falfurrias Management Partners took a majority stake in Freeman & Forrest, another B2B creator agency.

The deals are part of a record year, according to James Creech, founder of the creator M&A advisory firm Quartermast. There have been 30 agency acquisitions in the creator economy so far in 2026, up from 20 in all of 2025 and above the previous high of 28 in 2022, according to Quartermast data.

Overall, there have been 124 M&A creator-adjacent deals this year, the busiest on record, and roughly half of the agency deals have come since July, per Quartermast.

Behind the surge is a crop of boutiques founded during the pandemic-era influencer boom that have matured into businesses worth buying, which are run by founders who need more resources to keep up with what clients are asking for.

A specific set of firms—social-first creative shops, podcast agencies, TikTok Shop specialists, and B2B creator plays—are responsible for the majority of this interest, while the deals themselves are increasingly financed by a new cohort of buyers, including private equity firms, strategic roll-ups, and international firms. All of it is unfolding in a frothy market for creator economy deals, which is just now beginning to consolidate in response to urgent demand across a fragmented space.

“Creator marketing works,” Creech said. “Brands and their agency partners are going to continue to invest there.”

The current rush of creator agency activity has its origins in the pandemic, according to LV8 chief executive Griffin Haddrill.

Lockdown acted as a digital accelerant, pulling budgets toward creators at a moment when television and film had dried up and out-of-home advertising had stopped working.

Because acquisitions typically take nine to 12 months to come together, the resulting deal volume spiked in 2021 and 2022, slowed over the following two years, and has since normalized, Creech said.

The same period produced a wave of new agencies, many of which were founded by executives who saw the emerging relevance of the creator ecosystem and launched startups to service it. Five years later, those boutiques have matured into businesses worth buying, according to Haddrill.

FOR Scoops, Sharp Analysis, and Insider Context

For founders, selling offers the resources to keep up with clients, according to Get Engaged co-founder Ben Hiott.

“Social is becoming the center of how brands build their businesses, and clients are asking us to do more every year,” Hiott said in October.

The wave of dealmaking that followed the pandemic was primarily influencer agencies, according to Creech, while this year, the variety of targets has expanded.

In 2026, buyers have gone after social-first creative agencies, such as Get Engaged, OK Cool, and Sway Social; podcast and audio shops like Ad Results Media and Listening Party Agency; and TikTok Shop specialists including Orca, Uptickk, and Yada.

Private equity is also getting more involved in the space, investing capital after years of financing strategic acquirers, as Bridgepoint does with Samy, according to Creech. This year, Shamrock Capital took a majority stake in Saylor, Velocity became a near-equal partner in LV8, and Falfurrias bought Freeman & Forrest, among other deals.

Serial acquirers are consolidating the field as well. Influencer agency Ykone has made four acquisitions in 2026, and creator company network Residence has made two, while Samy and Publicis remain active, according to Creech. After large platform deals for Influential and Captiv8, Publicis has turned to regional acquisitions, including the French creator agency Point d’Orgue, to enter new markets, according to Creech.

For investors, the opportunity lies in stitching these shops together, according to Haddrill.

“The consumer demand was there, but the agency space was fragmented,” Haddrill said. “Now, investors are seeing this fragmentation and working to bring them together.”

B2B creator marketing has also emerged as its own category.

Creator Authority, whose clients include Amazon, Adobe, and Mastercard, was Billion Dollar Boy Group’s first acquisition, an all-cash, multimillion-dollar deal with an earnout component, according to Business Insider.

Freeman & Forrest, founded in 2024 by Emily Freeman and Forrest Brazeal after they ran influencer programs at Microsoft and Google Cloud, helps tech companies like Amazon and Google reach technical customers through creators.

And more of the deals cross borders: Get Engaged gave Samy a U.S. base after the agency built its business in Europe and Latin America, and dealmaking between the U.S. and the U.K. has picked up in both directions, according to Creech.

As the ecosystem matures, the next wave of dealmaking may come from the middle of the market, according to a creator management executive who asked not to be named.

The largest independents, such as Whalar and Influential, have already sold to Accenture Song and Publicis, respectively, which leaves room for midsize independents, many of which will come to market over the next 12 months, the executive said.

Creator management firms, which run creators’ businesses rather than simply booking their brand deals, are likely the next category to consolidate, the executive added.

The buying, in other words, is far from over, according to Creech.

“It’s mature, it’s scaled, it’s professionalized. The work is not done,” Creech said. “We’re maybe entering middle innings.”

Sara Kehaulani Goo is president of The Washington Post’s creator network, a new division that sits outside of the newsroom and makes sponsored short-form video series with independent creators. Goo, formerly the editor in chief of Axios, launched the first show in April, and the network has since released six series in categories ranging from personal finance to career advice.

Publishers increasingly want to work with creators, but the harder question is why creators would want to work with publishers. In our conversation, Goo and I discussed how The Post’s model works, why its creator content goes through Post fact-checking, and what a media company can offer talent who could go it alone.

Mark Stenberg: You specifically left Axios to head up this new creator division. Can you tell me about the role and how The Post is working with creators?

Sara Kehaulani Goo: I spent 12 years of my career at The Post as a reporter and editor, so it’s been nice to return in a very different role. The theory is simple: Look at where people spend their time, where they get information, where trust is growing, and where revenue is growing across media. The answer to all of those questions is creators. So, we are working with knowledge creators, people who have built big audiences around their expertise, and treating them as equal editorial and business partners.

Sara: We mostly work with creators who’ve built audiences with short-form video in lifestyle topics like personal finance, health and wellness, and career advice. Then, we develop the shows together and publish them together. Last week, we launched Her Next Move with an executive recruiter based in New York. She posts a two-minute video to her audience of a million people as a collab with us, so it reaches both audiences. We amplify it on The Post’s social accounts and publish it on our website and app. That show is sponsored by Uber.

Mark: Why would a creator want to work with the Post? Do they share in the revenue?

Sara: I won’t get into the details, but we pay them for their time. It’s similar to a contributor model, though not exactly like freelancing, because they’re entrepreneurs. A lot of them want to focus on the content, which is what journalists want too. We help build the business around them, and we provide distribution and editorial support when they need it.

Mark: Does their content go through Post fact-checking?

Sara: Yes, through my team. The standards are the same. There’s a lack of consistency in the creator world when it comes to disclosing sponsorships, and to quality in general, and we can bring that. Trust in creators is growing, but people still wonder whether they should trust what they’re watching on Instagram and who paid for it. We can help answer that.

Mark: It’s early, but how big is this now, and where is it headed?

Sara: This year has been a proof of concept. We’ve done six shows with six creators, across health and wellness, personal finance, travel, career advice, and entertainment. Now we’re deciding which ones to renew, and next year we want to do more always-on content. Brands are very interested, because every brand is thinking about its creator strategy, and finding the right creator takes a lot of effort. We take that headache off their shoulders.

Mark: Creators often relish operating on their own. How do you convince them to work with you?

Sara: Having led newsrooms, I’ve always seen my job as spotting talent, developing it, and creating an environment where people can do their best work. This is the same. Creators are entrepreneurs, and the platforms give them plenty of ways to make money on their own. But being an entrepreneur isn’t for everybody, at least not at every level. Media companies have always worked with outside experts. The structure just looks different now.

#7278. 5 Black Friday Truths About Gen Z Shoppers and the Mall

Welcome to Black Friday—the start of the most critical five days of the year for retail brands.

According to Circana, 43% of Americans will do most of their holiday shopping between today and Monday, and nearly a quarter of them will do most of it today, full stop.

This is also the day that brands are focused on Gen Z, because 94% of them plan to shop this week, according to Deloitte, and they’ll spend an average of $752—more than any other age cohort.

It’s a given that many consumers aged 14-29 will spend that money online. But a lot of shopping is also going to happen at the place Gen X made famous: the mall.

You’ll find plenty of zoomers in malls today, in fact, but they’re not all spending the way you think, and some aren’t there to shop at all. ADWEEK waded through a stack of recent market studies and tapped the shoulders of a few experts to plumb Gen Z’s current relationship with the mall and what it means for Black Friday 2026.

Gen X might have been the original mall rats in the 1980s, but today’s 18-29 age group has picked up the mantle.

According to Coresight research released in April, 48% of Gen Z consumers say they’ve hit the mall in the last two weeks. A recent Ipsos survey found that shoppers aged 18-34 were twice as likely as those 55 and up to shop at the mall. And according to a Stagwell study released this year, Gen Z not only spends more time at the mall on a per-visit basis, they also spend $296 per visit on average, the most of any age group.

Gen Z mall affinity seems strongest with women, too. A 2026 study of female zoomers’ shopping activity conducted by snack maker Sunnie and Westfield Rise found that 86% of them praised the mall for giving them “energy they can’t get digitally.”

The reasons why Gen Z likes the mall have as much to do with what the mall is as what it’s not—a small screen. Mall shopping represents a rebound effect in retail form, a response to hours of isolated scrolling.

Coresight’s work states it plainly. “Gen Z has grown up with frictionless digital commerce as a baseline expectation,” researchers wrote, “yet precisely because of this ubiquity, they increasingly seek the tangible, social and experiential dimensions of physical retail.”

It’s worth noting that experts are also starting to see the same dynamic unfolding with Gen Alpha (aged 13 and under), whose spending power and influence is already approaching $100 billion, per Charles Schwab.

“A trip to the mall with friends can be an important first taste of autonomy,” Helenor Gilmour, head of marketing research firm Beano Brain, told ADWEEK. “That shared experience is a powerful part of the appeal of shopping in stores. It is why we expect younger shoppers to embrace shopping events like Black Friday as a reason to visit malls together.”

Brieane Olson, CEO of PacSun, is well positioned to observe the evolving shopping habits of Gen Z. The brand sells its apparel online, in freestanding stores, and at mall locations. It’s easy to assume that a generation that grew up with ecommerce would stick to what they know; or they might defect because they’re tired of it. But the truth lay in between.

“We’re continuing to see just how important physical retail is to young consumers, and there’s a real opportunity for malls heading into Black Friday and the holiday season,” Olson told ADWEEK. “Young people aren’t choosing between digital and physical experiences. They want both, and they expect brands to create meaningful connections across each.”

With over 520 stores across 4.2 million square feet and sales north of $650 million a year, Bloomington, Minnesota’s Mall of America (MOA) is the biggest mall in the continental U.S. According to its 2026 Holiday Shopping Survey, 92% of Gen Z respondents expressed an intent to show up there today, with over half saying they planned to spend once they arrive.

Just as significantly, MOA has charted a symbiotic relationship between the online culture Gen Z consumes and their behavior when they visit.

“Social media may introduce them to a brand, trend or community, but there’s still an appeal to experiencing those interests firsthand,” said a MOA spokesperson. “We’re seeing that with our leasing strategy. Brands such as Edikted and Pop Mart, along with concepts like Bandai Namco and Kpop Nara, reflect how interests that develop online are translating into physical retail experiences.”

“Gen Z may discover what’s next online,” MOA chief business development and marketing officer Jill Renslow added, “but they still want to experience it together in the real world.”

From a brand perspective, it’s not enough that Gen Z merely visits the mall—retailers still need to woo them once they get there.

According to the International Council of Shopping Centers, 60% of Gen Z mall visitors say they go “just to socialize or meet friends.” Sunnie and Westfield North’s work found that 72% of female zoomers would still visit the mall even if it meant they couldn’t buy anything.

But there’s still a marketing opportunity here. Coresight’s study determined that social-media content creation is a big reason for mall visits, calling them “aesthetically curated spaces [that] serve as backdrops for organic and shareable moments.”

The brands that benefit most, said the study, are those that boast “visually engaging environments with Instagrammable decor.”

#7272. Show Your Work: Platforms Must Make Safety Decisions That Brands Can Verify

This column is adapted from the keynote address that X Head of Safety Michael O’Herlihy gave at the recent Brand and AI Safety Summit.

When I was at school in Ireland, math exams had a rule I thought was deeply unfair. You were allowed to just write down the answer—but if the answer was wrong, you got nothing. Zero.

However, if you showed your work, and your method was right but you’d slipped on one line, you still got partial credit.

It took me years to see the point. Showing your work doesn’t just give people an answer. It helps them understand how you got there. An answer on its own asks for trust; showing the work lets you check it.

I’ve spent two decades in the business of judgment calls: what stays up, what comes down, what is eligible for reach. Every one of those judgments is an answer. And for most of that time, our industry has handed people the answer and kept the work hidden. For too long, this has been a black box.

People ask me who makes better calls, humans or machines. Both make mistakes. If you give the same post to a hundred trained reviewers, you won’t get a hundred identical answers. So “human or machine?” is the wrong question. The right one is: Can you see the work?

Today, mostly, you can’t see inside. The systems that decide what you see, and what sits next to an ad, run out of sight. The deal was “trust us.” It held while volumes were manageable, and it stopped working as they grew.

Here’s the good news. AI doesn’t just make decisions faster. It gives us new ways to make those decisions explainable and testable. We now have the opportunity to turn the black box into a glass box: decisions you can trace back to the rules, and people outside who can check them, challenge them and help us improve them.

But a glass box alone doesn’t make a system safe. You could fill one with bad decisions. It would just let people see them. So transparency on its own isn’t the standard—defensibility is. Can this decision be justified under the rules? Transparency provides the evidence: Can someone see how we got there? Verification is the test: Can someone outside check whether the system consistently follows the rules?

Before returning to X, I worked at Reddit, where my colleague Rosa Català and I published a paper earlier this year called Escaping the Agreement Trap: Defensibility Signals for Evaluating Rule-Governed AI.

The usual test for a content-decision system is whether it agreed with a human. Same answer, pass. Different answer, fail. We looked at more than 193,000 real content decisions from Reddit and asked whether each one could be traced back to the written rules.

When the model and the human disagreed, the usual assumption is that the model got it wrong. But roughly four out of five of those model decisions could still be defended under the written rules. Disagreement doesn’t necessarily mean the model was wrong. Often, both opposing decisions were defensible. Grading by agreement is the wrong exam entirely: checking the answer, never the working.

How can two opposite decisions both be defensible? Ambiguity. Two reasonable people can look at the same case, reach opposite conclusions, and both be within the rules. Some ambiguity isn’t a bug, it’s what happens when real people apply rules to real life. But a lot of it can be managed, and it starts with more detailed rules. When we gave the model detailed written rules instead of just bullets, measured ambiguity dropped from 18% to 7%.

So how do we actually build the glass box? One window at a time.

Window one: See how it’s ranked. We open-sourced the For You algorithm, the system behind the feed: ranking, filtering and visibility-limiting labels. It’s on GitHub, and we keep it updated to match what’s running on X. Anyone can read it; you get the code, not a blog post about the code.

Window two: Add context. That’s Community Notes, where contributors can add context to any post. A note is only shown publicly if enough contributors from different points of view rate it helpful. The algorithm is open, and so is the data. Context comes from the community, not from us.

Window three: See what applies to your account. My colleague Keith Coleman’s team built Under the Hood. It shows account holders the visibility decisions that apply to them, and any restrictions applied due to a government request or court order. You don’t have to guess. You can look.

Window four: Understand the decision. This is what we’re building in Safety. Not just making decisions visible, but giving people clear ways to understand them, challenge them and help us get them right. When we label a post, the author will be able to tap the label and see what it was based on, in plain language, not code or model internals. They’ll see what it means for reach: who it won’t be recommended to, and who will see it behind a warning. And they’ll be able to appeal, telling us what we should have seen, so we can take another look and fix it if we got it wrong.

Sometimes people will still disagree, and that’s fine. Agreement was never the goal. What we want to hear is, “I still disagree, but I see why you got there.” A mistake you can see is a mistake you can fix. But there is a limit.

The glass box shows the rules and the decisions, not the answer key. We don’t publish the signals and thresholds we use to catch spam, scams and coordinated abuse. Open enough to verify. Closed enough that it can’t be gamed.

The past few years have tested advertiser confidence across the industry, and no platform, including X, is exempt from that scrutiny. That skepticism is legitimate, and the right response isn’t to ask for trust back. It’s to make trust earnable. Brands need to understand the decisions that shape where their ads appear, because what’s allowed on a platform and what you want next to your ad are two different standards.

The X platform philosophy of “Freedom of Speech, Not Reach” is built on that distinction. Lawful speech can stay up without being amplified. But limited reach should never mean hidden decisions.

Our rules decide what’s allowed; ranking decides what we recommend. Suitability decides what sits next to a brand, and that’s the brand’s call, not ours. Every brand draws that line differently; advertisers shouldn’t have to accept someone else’s definition of what’s suitable for them.

Control means setting your own standard. Transparency means seeing where your ads appear. Independent measurement means verifying whether it’s working. Published rules tell you what should happen. Transparency shows you what happened.

But neither, on its own, proves that a system is working consistently at scale. Verification does. Platforms shouldn’t be the only ones grading their own work, which is why at X we partner with DoubleVerify and Integral Ad Science.

To the platforms: Show your work. Write rules clear enough that decisions can be traced back to them, and let people outside your walls check the results.

To brands and agencies: Ask for it. Make “Can I check this?” a normal question, and ask every platform, including X: Can I set my own suitability standard? Can I see where my ads actually ran? Who outside your company checks it? Safety shouldn’t be something platforms ask you to trust. It should be something you can verify.

Those math exams had it right. Showing your work gives people the confidence to understand how you got there, and when you get it wrong, it gives them the evidence to say so.

“Trust us” belongs to the black box. It’s time to build the glass box. Show your work.

Michael O'Herlihy is Head of Safety at X, where he leads the work to keep people safe across the platform and its AI systems while protecting free expression and increasing transparency. He has more than two decades of senior leadership experience in technology, safety, product, and incident response, including roles at Google, Uber, Meta, Reddit, and X.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

“Let's say those plans haven't been touched in well over a decade,” Nye said. “They're sort of blowing the dust off of them and taking them back out in the community again.”

A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.
