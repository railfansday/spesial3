#7330. #AskNewser: CNN, NBC News and NewsNation Executives Reflect on Their Election Night Coverage

As the dust settles from a historic midterm election that saw the Democratic Party surf a blue wave to capture both the Senate and the House, news outlets are downshifting from special coverage and settling back into regular programming.

With 31 million viewers tuning in Tuesday night across 13 networks, TVNewser recently spoke to NBC News’ evp, creative, innovation & field production Marc Greenstein, whose network was the No.1 network during primetime in total viewers and the Adults 25-54 demo, as well as CNN’s svp, Washington & special events programming Abigail Crutchfield, whose network was the most-watched cable news network in the prized demo, and NewsNation’s political director Chris Stirewalt, whose network in coordination with Decision Desk HQ was the first one to call both the House and the Senate for the Democrats, for a post-election AskNewser on what worked well during their live election coverage.

TVNewser: What worked well from a coverage and technology standpoint on election night?

Greenstein: Steve Kornacki continues to be the best at breaking down the vote, providing context and helping viewers understand not just what is happening, but where the night is headed.

We combined editorial and technology in a new way, allowing Steve to contribute to the linear broadcast while simultaneously hosting his own dedicated streaming feed from the same studio as Savannah Guthrie and Tom Llamas. This allowed us to produce two distinct broadcasts just feet apart, each tailored to its audience, without losing the editorial connection between our talent.

Extensive planning for multiple election scenarios allowed us to react quickly as race calls came in. We deployed multiple correspondents across nearly every battleground state, covering both voting developments and candidate headquarters. This gave us a strong presence in the places driving the story and allowed us to bring firsthand reporting and reaction into the broadcast throughout the night.

We played more than 35,000 graphics across our zones and platforms, delivering results and analysis with speed and clarity. We brought together broadcast, streaming and digital coverage under a consistent editorial and visual approach, while tailoring the experience to each platform. The launch of our dedicated vertical video feed was another important step in reaching audiences in new ways without compromising the depth or quality of our journalism.

Cruthchfield: It was having all of our experts and talent together in one place. It gave the DC bureau great energy, and it was fun to watch the reporters and anchors work together to unpack the results.

Our director, Reza Baktar, designed a phenomenal set that gave us more mobility than ever and made it easy to get to any data set we needed at any moment. I also loved giving John King the ability to analyze the data at the magic wall or to bring his mini magic wall over to our analyst tables to be a part of the conversation. And I loved King’s new vertical magic wall, which opened up new storytelling options.

Stirewalt: You can’t beat the cards. We had such an advantage by having Decision Desk HQ (DDHQ) on our side. I think we did a good job of bringing viewers into the process and explaining what we were doing, but when you get down to it, the advantage of being so far ahead of the competition was just amazing.

TN: What was the biggest surprise of the night?

Greenstein: The biggest surprise was how quickly we were able to call control of the Senate. While there was a general sense that it could be a good night for Democrats, I think the early call caught many of us a little off guard. That said, we had planned for a wide range of scenarios and were prepared to stay on air throughout the night across the broadcast network, NBC News Now and Kornacki Cam.

Another pleasant surprise was how well the AI audio technology worked, allowing us to produce the linear and streaming broadcasts simultaneously from the same studio. We had limited time to rehearse with the technology, so there were certainly some nerves heading into the night. But it performed remarkably well and allowed us to pull off something we had never done before.

Cruthchfield: I think perhaps how quickly we were able to project the major results of the night. We always plan for every scenario, but this Tuesday we got answers from the voters quickly.

Stirewalt: How much work I wasted preparing for close elections in so many places. I was buckled in for tight contests in battleground states. We found out by 8:30 p.m. ET or so that what we really needed to get good at was going deep on places like Mississippi.

TN: Share the tricks you used to keep yourself awake and alert on Tuesday night.

Greenstein: Coffee, Diet Coke, and the adrenaline of being in the director’s chair! In my role, I rarely get the opportunity to direct. The last time was Election Night 2024. So getting back in the chair and working alongside the most talented people in the business, both on air and behind the scenes, pushes me to be at the top of my game. And I love every minute of it.

Cruthchfield: A lot of hot tea, a phenomenal team, and a big bag of gummy bears.

Stirewalt: I don’t need any outside stimulants. When the results started pouring in, I was so thrilled at how DDHQ and our team were doing that I was just high on life.

#7248. Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

President: Thomas Fanning Institution: University System of Georgia Coming or going? Coming

The University System of Georgia’s governing board on Oct. 1 confirmed former utility executive Thomas Fanning as its next chancellor. Walter Iwanenko stepped down as president of Gannon University, effective Oct. 8, the Pennsylvania institution announced the next day.

Gannon is in the process of acquiring nearby Ursuline College, a restructuring that Iwanenko said he committed to seeing the university through. The merger of the two Catholic institutions is expected to be complete by mid-December.

“Now that it is nearly done, I am stepping down to pursue other opportunities,” he said in a statement.

Gannon’s board chose Keith Taylor, who previously led the university for over a decade, to replace Iwanenko starting Oct. 8. It indicated Taylor’s appointment would not be a long-term one, saying he would assist the university “through this transition and the search for a new permanent president.”

President: Daniel Pullin Institution: Texas Christian University Coming or going? Going

Texas Christian University Chancellor Daniel Pullin abruptly resigned on Oct. 22 after less than two years in the role. The announcement came via a campus email sent by Victor Boschini, who headed TCU for over two decades before departing in 2025. Boschini is once again leading TCU after its board appointed him to replace Pullin.

The email did not give a reason for Pullin’s departure.

President: Amy Parsons Institution: Colorado State University Coming or going? Going

Amy Parsons will step down as president of Colorado State University, she said in an Oct. 28 campus letter. Her last day will be Dec. 31.

Parsons, an alumna of CSU, became the public institution's president in 2023 after holding senior leadership positions within the CSU system for nearly two decades.

In an Oct. 28 statement, CSU System Chancellor Tony Frank touted the university’s improved national visibility and the student access initiatives established during Parsons’ tenure. He said the search for her successor will begin in the spring and be led by Rico Munn, the incoming CSU system chancellor who is set to start in mid-2027. Longtime faculty member Rick Miranda, who served as CSU’s interim president in 2022 prior to Parsons' hiring, will again be the university’s acting leader beginning in January.

Like many public institutions, CSU is in a tough place financially amid underwhelming state support. Colorado’s fiscal 2027 budget kept higher education funding flat while capping public colleges’ ability to raise tuition for in-state students at 3.5%. CSU is set to cut some $39 million from its budget next year through staff layoffs and other measures.

President: Marvin Krislov Institution: Bard College Coming or going? Coming

Marvin Krislov will be the next president of Bard College, the private New York institution’s board said on Oct. 29. Krislov currently leads Pace University and previously held the presidency at Oberlin College. Following a unanimous vote, Bard’s board signed Krislov to a three-year contract which will begin July 1.

The announcement comes as Bard continues to navigate leadership challenges and unwanted public attention.

Leon Botstein resigned as Bard president this summer over intense scrutiny focused on his professional relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and Epstein’s resulting involvement with Bard. Botstein had led the campus for five decades.

Jonathan Becker, a longtime Bard senior administrator, has served as acting president in the interim.

Initially, Botstein continued to hold positions at Bard, including a professorship. But he stepped down from those roles this month after Democratic lawmakers made fresh allegations that he may have directly participated in Epstein’s abuse and exploitation of his victims. Botstein denies those allegations.

President: Tonjanita Johnson Institution: Universities of Wisconsin Coming or going? Coming

Johnson will be the system’s first permanent leader since trustees ousted its last president, Jay Rothman, in April.

Rothman had said previously that the board had given him an ultimatum — resign or retire by the end of the calendar year, or face termination by the board. He said he declined to step down because the board did not give him a reason for the request. The president of the board disputed this account, saying at the time that Rothman “was not without notice, nor was this process sudden.”

The coalition’s governance dispute with founding member Antioch University could worsen with the departure of its only independent board member.

A recent board change at the Coalition for the Common Good could plunge the organization even deeper into an internal stalemate as its member universities battle over its structure and future.

She joined CCG shortly after Ohio-based Antioch and Otterbein universities formed it in 2023. To date, the two universities are still the only members.

Her departure leaves the CCG board with eight members — four chosen by Antioch and four by Otterbein. That could make the six-vote supermajority necessary to confirm her replacement as the board’s independent member difficult given the tensions between the universities. Six-vote supermajorities are also needed for some action items of the CCG board.

Pasquerella’s term ended this fall, she confirmed to Higher Ed Dive. She initially resigned on May 20 citing increasing scheduling demands, but agreed to stay on to help with the transition through her term, which ended Sept. 30. Late last week, she wrote to CCG Board Chair Dan Gifford to confirm that her resignation was in effect and if it wasn't, to offer formal notice of her immediate resignation.

The coalition, originally the brainchild of former Antioch leaders, was meant to serve as an innovative network of like-minded institutions that could share resources and work together on programming. This year, however, it has become mired in a fierce governance and legal dispute.

Litigation began in August after CCG’s board voted to dissolve the Antioch Advisory Board of Governors, which oversees the university’s operations alongside a standing committee of CCG board members elected by the Antioch board. Pasquerella was among the board members to vote for the board's dissolution, Antioch President Lori Varlotta told Higher Ed Dive in a previous interview.

Antioch leaders argued that the CCG board’s vote violated the coalition’s bylaws, which mandate that major decisions about the university be first approved by the Antioch standing committee on the CCG board.

Just prior to learning of Pasquerella's resignation, Antioch Board Chair Steve Crandall published an open letter to her, asking for her help in resolving the dispute between Antioch and CCG.

“Your independent position on the CCG Board gives you an opportunity to encourage a fresh look at the choices ahead and help both universities move toward a resolution,” Crandall wrote.

“I continue to care deeply about Antioch and its educational mission," she said. "I sincerely hope the parties can find a constructive path forward that protects the university's interests, supports its students and employees, and allows Antioch to focus on its future."

The governance spat has upended not only the coalition but Antioch’s operations.

Antioch’s board initiated the litigation in August, but an Ohio state court ruled that the body didn’t have standing to sue over breaches in the CCG bylaws. A member of the Antioch standing committee on the CCG board refiled the lawsuit in September.

Colorado’s Poudre School District is moving forward with its plan after years of enrollment declines have led to over 10,500 empty seats.

Poudre’s enrollment declines reflect a larger trend in both Colorado and across the nation, which is also causing many other districts to close schools.

The enrollment challenges in Poudre largely stem from declining birthrates, higher cost of living and fewer young families moving into the area, according to the district. Poudre said in a September presentation that it expects enrollment to fall by 2% annually for the next five years. For every 2% drop in enrollment, the district loses $6 million in revenue.

For years, Poudre has faced enrollment declines that the district has tried to offset by consolidating staff, cutting millions of dollars from its budget, and eliminating hundreds of positions, said Superintendent Brian Kingsley in a September press conference announcing the initial school closure and consolidation proposal.

“Every year that we have protected buildings, we have done it by asking teachers and staff to do more with less,” Kingsley said. “That is not a sustainable way to support the people who teach our students, and it is not producing the experience for all of our students that each of them deserve at every single school. We can be better than that.”

By the 2027-28 school year, Poudre will close three elementary schools and one middle school. In that same period, the district will also consolidate a high school and an alternative education campus into one new school at a location still to be determined. The district will also close a dual language elementary school in 2028-29, but it plans to develop a "more unified" dual language program that will open that same school year.

Another elementary school was approved for closure in 2028-29, unless the Town of Timnath — a local community within Poudre’s district boundaries — secures full funding to cover the costs of building a new elementary school.

Meanwhile, three other elementary schools that were originally recommended for closure were not approved by the board and will remain open. Those three historic schools are located in rural mountainous areas of the district, and their proposed closure received pushback from local community members.

District parents urged the board to continue operating the three mountain elementary schools, noting that closures would force students to commute over 70 miles roundtrip and likely push those families to consider enrolling their children in charter schools, private schools, or homeschooling.

“Our three campuses function as a single, highly efficient school spread across a vast geographic region, sharing staff, specialized teachers, and administrative oversight to deliver some of the highest academic test scores in the district,” a local parent advocacy website said. “Closing any of these campuses doesn’t just shuffle students down the street — it fundamentally disrupts rural life and puts our children at risk.”

Earlier in October, the Board of Education at Adams 12 Five Star Schools in the Denver area approved to close six of its schools in the 2027-28 school year. The decision came as the district reported losing 6,400 students over the last decade and that enrollment decreased faster than initially projected during that period.

Adams 12 also pointed to sharp enrollment declines in Colorado generally, which the district said appears to be dropping faster than many other states. For example, the state’s enrollment reached its lowest point in a decade during the 2025-26 school year — at 870,793 students, according to the Colorado Department of Education.
