#7282. By the numbers: Ad tech’s widening divide, as AppLovin surges and rivals struggle in Q3

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

The latest quarterly earnings from six of the publicly traded ad tech companies reveal a tale of two cohorts, with AppLovin and Unity’s substantial growth contrasting with declining revenues at Criteo, Taboola, and Teads.

The companies, which reported their third-quarter results this week, generated markedly different outcomes during the reported period. AppLovin’s revenue increased 55% to $2.19 billion, while Unity posted 32% growth and Magnite reported a double-digit increase. Meanwhile, three of the six experienced annual revenue declines, with Teads suffering the steepest contraction at 26%.

The results also show differences in profitability, with some companies increasing earnings despite lower revenue. Meanwhile, connected TV remained a source of growth for Magnite and Teads, although it was insufficient to offset the latter’s broader decline.

Overall, the numbers illustrate how unevenly the industry’s growth opportunities are being distributed.

A top-line observation is that the two strongest performers are mobile advertising rivals AppLovin and Unity, for some, a trend that underlines the decline of the open web and the resilience of app-based media — both mobile and CTV — companies.

AppLovin reported $2.19 billion in third-quarter revenue, up 55% year over year, alongside net income of $1.44 billion, an increase of 72%.

The company also forecast Q4 revenue of between $2.365 billion and $2.395 billion, and told analysts that its Q3 revenue exceeded the combined revenue of the other five companies, which totaled about $1.94 billion.

Unity, a mobile advertising rival of AppLovin — the two are involved in a recently filed, ongoing legal dispute — reported revenue of $622 million, up 32%, with the Grow Solutions division, i.e., the division that hosts its media offering, generating $457 million, an increase of 44%. The company also reported that its Vector ad platform grew 126% year over year and 40% sequentially during the reporting period.

Elsewhere, in the cohort, growth was more modest, if not outright in marked decline (see chart below).

Magnite reported revenue of $204.2 million, up 14%, with growth primarily driven by its connected TV business. Meanwhile, Criteo reported Q3 revenue of $416 million, down 11%, while contribution ex-TAC, i.e., after “traffic acquisition costs”, declined 17% to $240 million.

Criteo’s retail media revenue fell 23%, partly reflecting changes involving two (unnamed) clients that reduced business by $20 million, according to executives there.

Meanwhile, Taboola reported revenue of $462.3 million, down 6.9%, amid declining advertising impression volumes. However, the company’s gross profit rose 9.6% to $152.3 million despite the 6.9% revenue decline.

By contrast, Teads (Taboola’s historic rival and one-time prospective merger partner) recorded the largest decline, with revenue falling 26% to $235.1 million. After TAC, its gross profit declined 28% to $93.5 million, according to the filing, with execs there citing continued weakness in open-internet advertising and not reinstating its financial guidance. In total, this equated to a net loss of $325.5 million, compared with a $19.7 million loss a year earlier.

CTV growth also proved an oasis for Outbrain’s executive team, with revenue from the channel increasing 37% year over year. However, this was offset by the fact that CTV represented just 12% of the company’s quarterly revenue, compared with its overall revenue decline of 26%. Teads reported nearly $100 million in year-to-date CTV revenue.

Taken in tandem, these results show that CTV continues to grow despite weaker performance in other areas of digital advertising. However, the contribution of that growth to overall company performance depends on the CTV business’s size relative to other revenue streams.

#7274. Creators see promise in Instagram’s long-form push, but question whether exposure is enough

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Last week, Instagram said users would be able to post videos up to 90 minutes long, a move aimed at attracting long-form YouTube creators. But, aside from offering a handful of select creators between $50,000 and $500,000 to post long-form content through its new “Features” feature, it has offered no concrete monetization pathway for creators, saying it’s “too early to share a long-form monetization model.”

Early reactions are mixed: Without a clear ad revenue plan, creators may hesitate to make original content for Instagram, while others see promise in the platform’s discovery tools and new TV app.

Digiday spoke with seven creator economy experts about what the change could mean for creators, agents, and marketers.

Instagram is known for reach, but engagement on Reels can vary (marketing software platform Cropink reports Reels get 36% more reach than carousels and 125% more than single-photo posts, but engagement lags). And creators question how that engagement translates into income.

Digital strategist and creator Gabriella Zutrau said that Instagram has great discoverability compared to other platforms, which is helpful for creators looking to build awareness. However, she was “disgusted” that it appears, at least for now, that the platform is “offering discoverability as a form of payment.”

Several of the creator managers and creators Digiday spoke with expressed concerns about the lack of an ad revenue structure for long-form content — especially in comparison to what is widely considered the best platform for earning, YouTube. But it’s increasingly difficult to get monetized on YouTube because of the thresholds required, and it may be harder after its requirements change next year.

“Exposure as a form of payment and with the intention of cutting into YouTube’s market share and cutting into the amount of views people are getting on YouTube — it’s just gross,” Zutrau said. “Instagram has never valued creators.”

Jo Wong, chief revenue officer at agentic AI creator commerce platform POP.STORE, agreed.

“If you talk to most creators, they’ll tell you that that’s really where a lot of the brand deals come through… Instagram in general never really had a super strong monetization program for creators off the get,” said Wong. “Frankly, I think they thought they could get away with [not having a long-form monetization model].”

Samantha Walters, vp of talent experience at Shine Talent Group, said she was “surprised” the platform hadn’t offered a competitive or comparable monetization infrastructure. “That’s why YouTube is so successful, and why creators give so much of their time and effort to it,” she added.

Nick Jacklin, president of Shorthand Studios, was more optimistic, though he emphasized that creators should be paid equitably and transparently, and noted that if Instagram starts running ads on long-form content, it’ll break creator trust.

“I’m all in favor of advocating for a similar model [to YouTube] where Instagram makes $1 [per view], some share of that goes to the creator…but if Instagram can show with the creators that do [Features] work early on that you can drive awareness, I think the monetization will follow,” Jacklin said.

A representative from Instagram told Digiday the platform is “exploring ways for creators to earn from brands, fans and Instagram, including performance-based incentives.”

For Alexandra Mathieu, vp of creative and strategy at Open Influence, the competition Instagram’s long-form push could breed is good for talent. “Instagram putting money behind creator acquisition gives managers more leverage in conversations with YouTube,” she said. “It also opens the door to negotiating beyond fees, including exclusivity, distribution, production support, and creative control.”

With monetization up in the air, are creators and creator agencies planning to do a strategic pivot? It depends on the content genre, it seems.

Mustafa Aijaz, vp of media company SoaR, which works with a lot of gamers and streamers, plans to use it as just another distribution platform for now. “We’ll post our content there, why not? But I’m not making content for Instagram at this moment in time,” he said.

Zack Evans, vp of creative strategy at Shorthand Studios, thinks interview-style shows and scripted comedy could work really well on the platform — especially with the revamped Instagram TV app, which he said promotes scrolling and discoverability more than YouTube does.

“Comedy is inherently surprising, so I think with Reels and Features now, being able to just scroll and see a scripted thing playing out, you don’t yet know what it is, and you’re going to be intrigued,” he said. “It’s much more a pure comedy experience…I think that there will be a certain sector of creators who will actually rise to [this] occasion.”

The ability to pull viewers in with short-form content and funnel them to long-form while remaining on the platform could be a boon for any kind of video creator.

“The top of the funnel is Instagram. [We’re always] trying to get them and trying to use Reels, use Shorts, use any kind of way to grab attention for people, and then try to funnel them over to a longer-form piece of content,” said Evans. “It’s really hard, and these platforms obviously aren’t the best of friends, and so they make it difficult for you to do that.”

The discoverability of short-form and the shareability of content on Instagram could help creators build bigger audiences on the platform, which they can then shop to brands.

Both Wong and Mathieu suggested creators might use Instagram’s Features to recycle older content for reach.

“I’ve encouraged creators to actually use their older content and put it on there,” said Wong. “Most of the views on your YouTube come within the first month, for example, or three weeks, then put your older catalog on Features and test into it. See how your reach is doing. What does it mean for you? Are you able to then get better brand deals, or how does that change?”

She’s advising creators to do that before “catalyzing” original content, whereas Mathieu thinks creators should use Features to use “existing programming as a springboard for new, Instagram-native series such as creator-led shows, movie-length content, longer-running series and video.”

It’s clear that creators are still sussing out Instagram’s latest feature. In a survey sent to 50 of Shine Talent Group’s creators, 93 percent said the platform would have to change its revenue policies to entice them to post long-form content; 100 percent already post on the platform.

#7272. Campaign Trail: Wet Ones puts ‘hand butts’ on the streets

The Edgewell brand worked with BBH USA on an out-of-home campaign it hoped would cause some consumers to do a double take.

It’s cold and flu season, which means it's prime time for consumers to reach for antibacterial hand wipes. And while Wet Ones has been keeping hands clean consistently over the last half century, the Edgewell Personal Care brand had not released a major campaign in years.

Wet Ones decided to change that, seeking to create something fresh, bold and different that could capture the attention of a younger audience. The brand teamed with BBH USA and kept the brief loose: get the brand back out there and in conversations. The result, “Just For Hands,” launched last month and grew from a simple insight: More than 50% of consumers have used hand wipes on an intimate area, according to research commissioned by the brand.

“It was a bit surprising to us because it wasn't something that we had necessarily seen before,” said Nicole Harris, head of marketing for sun and skin at Edgewell. “We can do something with this. This is funny. How can we play on this?”

The insight about how consumers were using hand wipes inspired early rounds of work that played off inanimate objects like peaches. But BBH kept pushing and landed on an idea that the team calls “hand butts:” extreme close-ups of fingers that look like posteriors.

“We wanted to create a double take – that split second when you think you're seeing one thing, and then realize you're looking at something completely different,” said Yohan Daver, executive creative director at BBH USA.

“The craft had to make the illusion feel completely real, and by taking the idea across New York City at scale, we're turning one clever visual device into a much bigger cultural moment,” Daver added.

Created with photographer Ale Burset, a winner of more than 60 Cannes Lions awards, the campaign had a comprehensive out-of-home media plan including LinkNYC kiosks, transit shelters, digital newsstands, wild postings, bus wrapping and a Time Square takeover. Plus, interactive walls dispensed travel packs and individual wipes to pedestrians.

Originally, Wet Ones’ media plan included ads throughout the New York City subway system, where the variety of handheld surfaces like railings and straps provide a “torture test” for the product, Harris explained. That changed when the Metropolitan Transportation Authority vetoed the “Just For Hands” creative.

“When they refused us for being a little too risque, it was perfect,” Harris said. “It added juiciness to the story, but it also forced us to think quickly in terms of the other touch points. How do we want to evolve the media plan to still have the impact, but also take advantage of this from a storytelling perspective?”

Wet Ones’ PR team used the rejection as an angle to drive earned media. The brand also partnered with Kyle Cooke, of reality TV series “Summer House,” for a social stunt that brought a hand butt to celebrity gossip account DeuxMoi. Then, the “Just For Hands” campaign became the target of some friendly banter from Goodwipes, a brand of flushable wipes — not hand wipes — that isn’t a Wet Ones competitor.

“We [wanted] to get a response up in Times Square, so that was probably a record that I've seen, in my tenure for the company, for us to turn around a piece of creative and get it up within hours,” Harris said. That type of speed and agility was central to the “Just For Hands” campaign.

“We kicked it off at the end of August, so really fast turn out of the gate, but [it] led to amazing work,” Harris said. “We're getting great consumer response, but our retailers were really excited about it, too.”

#7090. Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

#7084. Abrupt leadership departures at two private universities

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Bard College announced its next leader amid ongoing scrutiny. Plus, a Colorado president will step down after two decades at her university.

Editor’s note: The Leadership Ledger is a monthly roundup of some of the most noteworthy college leadership changes nationwide.

In a month of tricks and treats, colleges across the U.S. got their share of both.

Two public university systems, in Georgia and Wisconsin, announced new leaders following much-discussed departures earlier this year. Conversely, two private universities saw their president abruptly resign in October, with one providing little insight into what prompted the leadership change.

From Texas to Wisconsin, we’re rounding up some of last month’s biggest leadership changes below.

President: Thomas Fanning Institution: University System of Georgia Coming or going? Coming

The University System of Georgia’s governing board on Oct. 1 confirmed former utility executive Thomas Fanning as its next chancellor. Fanning, the sole finalist for the job, is to take office Jan. 1, replacing Sonny Perdue. This will be the first position in higher education for Fanning, who spent more than 40 years in leadership positions at Southern Co. before retiring in 2023.

USG has a history of hiring leaders from outside of academia. Perdue, who announced his intention to retire in April, had a decades-long political career prior to taking the helm of USG in 2022. During his four-year tenure, he installed 14 presidents across the system’s 25 colleges, and USG’s fall enrollment repeatedly broke records. Perdue’s last day is set for Dec. 31.

President: Walter Iwanenko Institution: Gannon University Coming or going? Going

Walter Iwanenko stepped down as president of Gannon University, effective Oct. 8, the Pennsylvania institution announced the next day.

Gannon is in the process of acquiring nearby Ursuline College, a restructuring that Iwanenko said he committed to seeing the university through. The merger of the two Catholic institutions is expected to be complete by mid-December.

“Now that it is nearly done, I am stepping down to pursue other opportunities,” he said in a statement.

Gannon’s board chose Keith Taylor, who previously led the university for over a decade, to replace Iwanenko starting Oct. 8. It indicated Taylor’s appointment would not be a long-term one, saying he would assist the university “through this transition and the search for a new permanent president.”

Shortly after he took office, Taylor said Gannon is preparing for a reduction in force amid a sharp decline in enrollment and a broader institutional restructuring, Erie News Now reported.

President: Daniel Pullin Institution: Texas Christian University Coming or going? Going

Texas Christian University Chancellor Daniel Pullin abruptly resigned on Oct. 22 after less than two years in the role. The announcement came via a campus email sent by Victor Boschini, who headed TCU for over two decades before departing in 2025. Boschini is once again leading TCU after its board appointed him to replace Pullin.

The email did not give a reason for Pullin’s departure.

President: Amy Parsons Institution: Colorado State University Coming or going? Going

Amy Parsons will step down as president of Colorado State University, she said in an Oct. 28 campus letter. Her last day will be Dec. 31.

Parsons, an alumna of CSU, became the public institution's president in 2023 after holding senior leadership positions within the CSU system for nearly two decades.

In an Oct. 28 statement, CSU System Chancellor Tony Frank touted the university’s improved national visibility and the student access initiatives established during Parsons’ tenure. He said the search for her successor will begin in the spring and be led by Rico Munn, the incoming CSU system chancellor who is set to start in mid-2027. Longtime faculty member Rick Miranda, who served as CSU’s interim president in 2022 prior to Parsons' hiring, will again be the university’s acting leader beginning in January.

Like many public institutions, CSU is in a tough place financially amid underwhelming state support. Colorado’s fiscal 2027 budget kept higher education funding flat while capping public colleges’ ability to raise tuition for in-state students at 3.5%. CSU is set to cut some $39 million from its budget next year through staff layoffs and other measures.

President: Marvin Krislov Institution: Bard College Coming or going? Coming

Marvin Krislov will be the next president of Bard College, the private New York institution’s board said on Oct. 29. Krislov currently leads Pace University and previously held the presidency at Oberlin College. Following a unanimous vote, Bard’s board signed Krislov to a three-year contract which will begin July 1.

The announcement comes as Bard continues to navigate leadership challenges and unwanted public attention.

Leon Botstein resigned as Bard president this summer over intense scrutiny focused on his professional relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and Epstein’s resulting involvement with Bard. Botstein had led the campus for five decades.

Jonathan Becker, a longtime Bard senior administrator, has served as acting president in the interim.

Initially, Botstein continued to hold positions at Bard, including a professorship. But he stepped down from those roles this month after Democratic lawmakers made fresh allegations that he may have directly participated in Epstein’s abuse and exploitation of his victims. Botstein denies those allegations.

President: Tonjanita Johnson Institution: Universities of Wisconsin Coming or going? Coming

Tonjanita Johnson will be the next president of the Universities of Wisconsin, following a unanimous vote from the system’s board on Oct. 30. Johnson, a senior administrator with the University of Alabama system, will take office at the start of the new year.

Johnson will be the system’s first permanent leader since trustees ousted its last president, Jay Rothman, in April.

Rothman had said previously that the board had given him an ultimatum — resign or retire by the end of the calendar year, or face termination by the board. He said he declined to step down because the board did not give him a reason for the request. The president of the board disputed this account, saying at the time that Rothman “was not without notice, nor was this process sudden.”
