#6886. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6876. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

Theroux’s sushi artisan, befuddlingly, is an Italian immigrant from just outside Milan. How the hell did he get here?

“I came here by plane,” Francesco jokes. Seven years ago, at 27 years old, he committed himself to making sushi at its highest level. Where to go to complete his training? Kyoto, of course, without a return ticket. Now he’s serving Justin Theroux scallop over soy-sauce-soaked rice with a sprinkle of salt and a zest of lime on top.

With bellies full, it’s time for a Zen garden tour. Kyoto is home to many temples and shrines and gardens both Buddhist and Shinto, Japan’s two national religions. But this garden—situated in the Konchi-in Buddhist temple and named, simply, the Crane and Turtle Garden after the shapes of its central rock formations—doesn’t get much foot traffic. It’s more peaceful than the bigger, more popular tourist-mobbed shrines, ideal for a thoughtful traveler like Theroux.

It’s probably “97 out of 100” in terms of popularity, says tour guide Andrew William. Like the sushi chef from earlier, William is not from Japan. He is from Maryland, not too far from Washington, D.C., where Theroux grew up. Like Francesco, he uprooted himself to perfect his art elsewhere, though instead of making sushi, he designs and builds gardens. He’s worked on big projects in the past, including Ben Stiller’s estate on the north shore of Kauai, Hawaii. William’s pursuit, however, belies the reality of chasing dreams: They often turn to nightmares.

William once wanted to be head groundskeeper of this idyllic garden. But it’s not so easy for foreigners to rise in Japanese hierarchies. He claims to have suffered abuse at the hands of his employers and endured long working hours for low pay—“a lot of bad things to maintain beauty,” he says, still apparently chewing on the irony.

Theroux is no stranger to that feeling. Sometimes, he says of acting, “you’ll have a miserable experience that’s received very well. And you think, God, I was so miserable on that job. And yet the product was so good, ’cause so many craftsmen are bringing their talents to bear on it. The editor really helped it, the music really helped it, the director was very good—although they might have been an asshole.”

Theroux and William sit side by side on a broad wooden step that leads into the temple and overlooks the amorphous stony shapes of the crane and the turtle. He and Theroux are perched, William says, on what Buddhist philosophy considers the ship of life. Out directly in front of them is an expanse of pebbles raked into a motif of alternating swirls and straight lines, an ocean of respective rough and smooth seas. On the other side, the crane and turtle await, representing the harmony that anticipates us at the end of our earthly journey.

There is still an evening of activities to go: A visit to the temple. Matcha and handmade sweets in the temple’s tearoom. An intimate geisha dinner complete with drinks, cultural conversation, and traditional Japanese food. All of which prove to be enriching experiences for travelers. Yet it is here, facing the crane and turtle, where visitors can meditate on their journey and appreciate the unique experience on which they’ve embarked.

“There’s tourism, and there’s travel,” Theroux says. “And I think they are two totally different things.”

Tourism is hoofing it from place to place, checking boxes. Seen that, did this. But travel, Theroux’s travel, is different. It’s about meeting people, making friends beside the worker at the front desk or the technician at the spa, and engaging with their lives, their realities, on a deeper level.

“Anytime you can have access to something unique or an area of interest—whether that’s craftsmanship, arts, music, dance, knives, enamelware, things like that—you’re always going to get a little peek into a country or city that you didn’t know existed,” he says.

Like all his endeavors, Theroux’s Japan Expeditions aren’t perfect—if they were, there’d be no reason for visitors to come back—but they’re as close to perfect as it gets.

#6865. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6812. Inside Danielle Lauder’s Barbie Dream Closet

Designed by AMMOR Architecture, the ultra-pink, mega dressing room is a cinematic fantasy come to life.

Cher Horowitz had a touch-screen conveyer belt in her color-coded closet. Carrie Bradshaw kept her Manolos in an unusually spacious high-fashion-filled walk-in. And Margot Robbie’s Barbie displayed her hot-pink wardrobe like doll’s packaging in her wall-free fantasy villa. Danielle Lauder, the great-granddaughter of Estée Lauder, created her own iteration of the dream closet—this time, in real life.

Her own inspiration? “I always thought of Belle in Beauty and the Beast in the library, just rolling around on her ladder,” she says. “I’m not much of a reader, but as a collector, I knew that’s what I wanted.”

Her wish list for the jewel-box space also included curving walls, glossy pink lacquer, illuminated marble, and a capsule-shaped vanity inspired by the makeup collection she designed for Estée Lauder in 2019. The beauty empire was founded by—and named after—her great-grandmother in 1946; and her father, William P. Lauder, serves as the chairman of the board. (Danielle now oversees her own production company, Act 4 Artists.)

To make her dream a reality, Lauder turned to architects Goil Amornvivat and Thomas Morbitzer, founders of New York City–based AMMOR Architecture. The duo had instant chemistry with Lauder, who was especially taken with the meticulous diorama they created for her.

“Tom and I both love Barbie,” says Amornvivat. “So we tweaked our design process to make an architectural model that was more like a dollhouse, where you could switch out surfaces.” Their efforts worked on Lauder, herself a Barbie fan, and the pair walked away with the commission to not just create the dressing room but to overhaul the entire industrial-style apartment in the West Village of Manhattan, which was then being used as an art studio.

“They both really understood and saw my vision,” says Lauder. “Plus, they were the most excited, which made me more excited. Goil is like the Energizer Bunny.”

The 250-square-foot dressing room is, Lauder says, “the biggest room in the apartment besides the living room.” It’s also where Lauder spends the most time. She enters just off the primary bedroom via an arched passageway finished in custom bronze plaster, emerging from the dark portal into a pink fantasia where her expansive fashion collection is arrayed like a personal boutique. “The idea was for it to be part display area but also a functional closet,” she says.

At the center is a custom pink vanity outfitted with fluted drawers, dual-sided mirrors, and a top made of richly veined Midnight Rose marble, sourced from New York Stone. Lauder keeps her collection of vintage and reissued Estée Lauder compacts in the drawers. “I love collecting them and having them out on display or in a nice drawer,” says Lauder. “They’re beautiful little artifacts.”

On the curving walls, rounded niches display shelving and racks against illuminated stone, part of Lauder’s request for layered lighting. “I love the backlit marble,” she says. “It’s so flattering.” She keeps her enviable bag and shoe collection on permanent display, maintained with help from her housekeeper. Lauder draws from the selection on a daily basis, with particular affection for family heirlooms such as her mom’s vintage Dolce & Gabbana handbags.

Overhead, Amornvivat and Morbitzer devised a futuristic lighting installation, stretching mesh taut over an oval-shaped light source to create a seamless surface. “The light in there is controllable by color, and it's dimmable,” notes Morbitzer. “It’s kind of like a skylight, which was important because it’s the only room without windows,” adds Amornvivat.

The bespoke elements continue: heated floors, an ice maker, a perfume bar, plenty of drawers, and a pair of walk-in closets cleverly concealed behind matching lacquered doors. “The whole room is custom millwork,” says Amornvivat.

And Lauder did get the sliding ladder of her dreams. “That was my hill to die on,” she says. “I’ve always struggled to reach things in my closet, so it was definitely practical. And my grandfather had one in his apartment in the library when I was growing up, and I just loved it.”

Now that the closet is complete, Lauder has a hard time leaving. “I spend more time in here than I do probably even sleeping in bed,” she says with a laugh. The space is remarkably faithful to the original idea that Amornvivat and Morbitzer presented in their dollhouse diorama. “I wanted it to be totally mine, feminine and fun, and still very warm and inviting,” she says. “It’s become my ultimate bachelorette hub.”

#6803. Is The Rings of Power Still Worth Watching?

As The Lord of the Rings prequel enters its third of five planned seasons, let’s admit that Jeff Bezos spent a little too much money making this a reality.

When I was a kid, I was obsessed with something called Mystic Knights of Tir Na Nog. If you weren’t a first-grader in 1998, it was a Fox Kids Saturday morning series about four heroes in medieval Ireland who morphed into armored knights to battle dragons and evil witches. It was Power Rangers by way of Celtic mythology, and it kicked ass. I was a wee bit too young for Hercules and Xena then, and Mystic Knights made sword-and-sorcery stories accessible to my Saturday morning-addled brain. Two years after it went off the air, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring stormed into theaters.

However unfair, it’s through that spectrum of two extreme ends that I see fantasy television like The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power. I have a standard through which I see this genre in this particular medium. On the big screen, fantasy can feel endless, its scope stretching beyond the horizon. On the small screen, you can feel the ceiling over everyone’s heads. Even Game of Thrones had a coffee cup slip through the veil. Which is why, despite the distant relation to Peter Jackson’s immaculate movies, The Rings of Power is quintessential fantasy TV on par with Mystic Knights. That’s not a bad thing—it’s just perspective.

We’re four years and three seasons (lol) into The Rings of Power’s planned five seasons on Prime Video. After all this time, it still hasn’t won over skeptics. There are long and boring reasons why Tolkien faithful have been averse to the prequel show. They have complaints about pacing, plotting, creative liberties from Tolkien’s textbooks of lore, and general execution. Regrettably, they more often than not get mixed up with old-fashioned racism and misogyny. (Valar forbid a dwarf be a Black woman.) But sometimes the worst people you know have a point: The Rings of Power has been an underwhelming experience. Despite its strong start in season 1, the show simply hasn’t escaped the long, long shadow of Jackson’s cinematic trilogy. (Even Jackson never lived up to his own standards. See: The Hobbit.)

Amid the show’s large ensemble, the series bet many of its chips on Charlie Vickers as Sauron. Though handsome, Vickers’ lack of charisma, middling scriptwriting for his role, and a severe lack of sexual tension with co-star Morfydd Clark (as Galadriel) have sadly made Rings of Power somnambulant. Now in season 3, the chance to course-correct is long past, and the rigor mortis of its missteps have now settled in.

Season 1 had a strong start. Sauron is a hottie, and his early relationship to Galadriel carried with it an air of intrigue and yearning until his mask came off. Maybe there could have been a whole show about their forbidden attraction. (Some fans have already coined “Saurondriel.”) The good girl/bad boy pairing is a tried and true tradition, with a subset of the Star Wars fandom possessing a bizarre obsession with Daisy Ridley’s Rey getting it on with Adam Driver’s Kylo Ren. Who are any of us to deny ourselves this pleasure?

But season 2 didn’t seize on nor improve what was its grace. Their characters were kept separate until the finale, where they reunited on the battlefield. It’s a good scene, to be fair, it’s just maddening that what could have been the whole show was just five minutes, including a bloodless sword fight.

Now here we are in season 3, and it’s more of the same. All of the characters are on a journey across Middle-earth, the story is moving along at a steady, if at times meandering pace, Sauron is quite literally plotting everything from his high tower, and there is a clear endpoint at the end of it all: the forging of the One Ring. As of this writing, the season’s four episodes (available to stream now) suggest we’ll get there in due time. There have been some big-name casualties already, and some impressive set pieces that actually feel like things are intensifying at the right temperatures.

Still, this is Lord of the Rings we’re talking about here. For all the lore and imagination Tolkien spilled onto his pages, his was a story still mired in the age-old tradition of good versus evil. That is Rings of Power too, where even morality comes in shades of stark black and white (mostly) and just enough polished VFX and production design to sell the immersion. One could ask, “What more do you want?” Somehow, it’s still not really enough. Even now, there is still too little energy, too little thrust, too little verve—and yes, still too little sex appeal—to warrant undivided attention. The Rings of Power is mighty pretty to look at on a big 50-inch LED screen. (I’m particularly engrossed anytime we follow Daniel Weyman’s Gandalf, who is always strolling through the most picturesque backdrops.) But it’s also far too easy to get distracted by more exciting things, like your laundry machine timer going off. In other words: The Rings of Power is a television show.

For all the money Jeff Bezos spent on making Rings of Power a reality, it’s still just TV. It’s maybe a cruel, cosmic, and frankly righteous joke that the billionaire’s very clear marching orders to his team—“I want my Game of Thrones,” is how he was quoted—have resulted in precisely that. A fantasy television series. Blame the streaming era for pigeonholing everything of this caliber into a blockbuster with lofty expectations. Not all TV should be appointment viewing. Sometimes a TV show can and should just be what it is: Mystic Knights, with a few extra zeroes.

#6767. Robert Pattinson Will Make a Bold, Dangerous, and Entirely Original 007

Timothy Dalton’s James Bond advice should be the only suggestion he follows.

James Bond’s greatest weakness is not megalomaniacal madmen bent on world domination. Those he can handle. The superspy is far more likely to be taken down by well-meaning committees.

That’s why Robert Pattinson’s casting as filmmaker Denis Villeneuve’s new 007 is a bold and promising choice. The two of them together have amassed enough industry clout to craft the character their own way, to make him distinctive and daring in a way, hopefully, that we haven’t seen before.

Some things shouldn’t change, like his seductiveness, the Walther PPK, and the way he likes his martinis to be shaken. But those things only make up the silhouette of James Bond. Each actor must put his own imprint on the core of Ian Fleming’s debonair secret agent, just as the author did in his original novels. Sean Connery added ruggedness to the savoir faire. George Lazenby gave him a soulful vulnerability in his solo outing as the character. Roger Moore sharpened 007’s sense of humor. Each actor leaves his fingerprints behind as he hands it off to the next.

This leads to the essential question for anyone signing up to play James Bond: Are you strong enough to stand out?

It’s not so easily answered. I’ll let Timothy Dalton, my personal favorite Bond, explain: “I have some good friends from the Bond days, some really good people, but they’re trapped. Everyone’s trapped by it in a way,” he told me. “Whoever does it has got to have a very clear sense of what they want to achieve, because everybody in the world has got a different opinion about it.”

To figure out what comes next, we should look back at what came before.

Dalton (who happens to be Christopher Nolan’s favorite Bond too) made that comment to me back in February 2025, when Amazon MGM Studios first acquired the rights to the 007 character.

When he first took on the role in 1987’s The Living Daylights, his choice was to add a renegade wolf quality. But he also recognized that James Bond must remain, ultimately, the good guy, and that movie was entirely defined by an act of mercy. Fleming’s short story “The Living Daylights” makes up the film’s opening scene: Bond has been sent to take out an enemy sniper, but when he sees that his target is a beautiful cellist (played by Maryam d’Abo), he aims his own rifle to distract her rather than kill her.

He then becomes responsible for her life, and his 007 has no problem cutting down any foes who block their paths. Dalton’s Bond was more polished than Connery and less comedic than Moore, but he was led by a kind of stormy passion. He had heart, but it pumped cold blood.

Still, Dalton had to break through that formidable wall of consensus. Bond is not a solo enterprise, of course. The director has a say, as do the producers and the screenwriters, but the danger comes from two places: the studio and the audience itself. As Dalton said, “Everybody in the world has got a different opinion about it,” and those voices have to be defied, just as his 007 refused the order to place a bullet in the enemy cellist’s heart.

Nothing makes hands wring and sweat pour in a studio conference room like something unexpected, and know-it-all supposed “fans” often resist change while paradoxically demanding something fresh and new. He had two outings as Bond, the second being 1989’s License to Kill, in which 007 goes even more rogue, defying Her Majesty’s secret service to embark on a mission of revenge. Then the character became tied up in rights lawsuits for several years, and Dalton chose not to return.

When Daniel Craig took over 007 from Pierce Brosnan in 2005, purists complained about his blue eyes and blond hair. When they finally saw him bring the character to life in 2006’s Casino Royale, those critics soon fell silent. Craig gave Bond a soldier’s mindset. He was a battlefield veteran whose new uniform happens to be a dashing suit. He was successful for so long because as he added his own spin, he capitalized on the best of what came before: He was as rough as Connery and as lethal as Dalton. The humor and playfulness that defined Moore and Brosnan were still present, but they served as a mask for the kind of wounded vulnerability that Lazenby had introduced.

Pattinson is now free to pick and choose whatever qualities he likes from this Brotherhood of Bond, but if he is going to take anyone’s advice, it should be Dalton’s: “Whoever does it has got to have a very clear sense of what they want to achieve.”

Fortunately, no one tells Villeneuve what to do. The filmmaker has spent two decades establishing himself not just as a distinctive artist but as a reliable box-office draw. Those two qualities give him a powerful weapon—clout—that can stave off the onslaught of opinions that will inevitably surround this casting choice.

Just this Saturday, I moderated a Q&A with Villeneuve at the Golden Globe Foundation before an audience of students, and one young filmmaker named Oscar Flores asked the Dune filmmaker, “When is the right time for you to listen to feedback, and when is the right time to listen to yourself?”

Villeneuve gave an answer that perfectly matches Dalton’s advice and increases my optimism about his approach to Bond: “Honestly, people say that I’m humble, but that’s bullshit,” he said, only partly joking. “If I’m asking for $200 million for a movie, what kind of humility is that?”

The suggestion he made to the students was to not take on projects that are outside their control. Start small, stay true, and build up your skill set behind the camera while establishing a reputation that gives you defining power over the movies you make. He warned them not to follow the “siren” song of a big-budget offer if it means ceding control. Instead, Villeneuve said, he found it effective to build up his status with smaller films, moving from 2010’s more intimate Incendies to 2013’s harrowing Prisoners and 2015’s even more sprawling Sicario before leveling up in scale to 2016’s Arrival and 2017’s Blade Runner 2049.

I was able to keep control of them. I was making sure that I was in control of the tools, and control of the language, and getting strong with the crew, being able to and making sure that my voice would be heard. … What I will say to you is: Be careful of the sirens.”

Only after amassing this authority, Villeneuve said, did he feel the time was right to take on his dream project of Dune. “If you made a successful first feature film that cost $200,000 and they offer you a major blockbuster, be careful,” he said this weekend. “Be careful who you are working with, and don’t listen to your ego. Do you really believe that you have the skills to bring it to the screen? Maybe you do. Some people can, by the way. I was not ready to make Dune after my second [film]. ... It took me a lot of movies before I knew that I was able to handle the pressure and making sure that I would be able to control totally, 100 percent, the film. At the end of the day when I look at the movie and the screen, it’s my movie.”

No doubt Pattinson’s casting was his decision, and Villeneuve’s status ensured it was accepted by the powers that be at Amazon MGM Studios. It probably wasn’t a difficult yes, given that Pattinson is currently in his prime. Not only is he The Batman, but he stood out as the conniving usurper Antinous in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey and has earned accolades for his haunting performance as a predator-hunting journalist in Primetime.

Is he a heartthrob? Ask the legion of Twilight fans, and cover your ears for their deafening reply.

Pattinson forged his bond with Villeneuve with another upcoming role in Dune: Part Three, playing the Face Dancer Scytale. He’s a shape-shifting psychological assassin who seeks to undermine Timothée Chalamet’s all-seeing, all-powerful emperor by getting inside his head, and the actor and filmmaker worked closely to figure out the look, mannerisms, and motivations of a creature void of form.

Then Villeneuve thought about it some more. As the director told those students on Saturday, he has a strong point of view, but it’s not inflexible. The kid who dramatically hops down from a tree in his jump-scare introduction to Ron and Harry could very well have been there to bully them. That’s a sneak-attack entrance, proposed by Pattinson himself, and little details like that gave this upright, wholesome character a dose of intrigue.

Pattinson was only 17 when he filmed that. In the more than two decades that followed, he has paid his dues, learned his lessons, and no doubt gotten his share of scars. With Villeneuve backing him, he’s bulletproof. The two of them can take chances, be bold, and defy orders—just like 007 would, just as playing the character demands.

Pattinson is not a paper doll that will fold when the committees come for James Bond.

#6761. Nanofat Is Dermatologists’ Best-Kept Secret for Younger, Fresher Skin

It’s not an injectable filler, and it’s not fat transfer. It’s a secret third thing you’ll want to try.

The last few years have been unkind to our facial fat. We sliced out our buccal pads to create sunken-in, razor-sharp cheekbones. We zapped our skin with thermal devices that accidentally melted fat away in pursuit of boosting collagen. We injected our stomachs with fat-loss GLP-1 medications, leaving our faces looking hollow. Droopy. Structurally unsound. As it turns out, facial fat is critical infrastructure—not just for looking younger, but for your skin's overall health and quality. And while you can’t choose where you lose or gain fat, you can choose to have a doctor siphon it, remix it, and reinject it.

“Adipose tissue is a rich source of growth factors and progenitor cells, and re-injecting fat is probably one of the best ways to regenerate the full potential of healthy skin I've seen in the last decades,” says Patricia Ogilvie, a Munich-based dermatologist and member of the Dior Reverse Aging Board. “There are trends that are not really worth being a trend. I think the restaging of the importance of adipose tissue is very justified.”

You’re probably familiar with fat transfer, a procedure that’s been around for decades. In simple terms, fat transfer takes adipose tissue from one area of your body, like your flanks, liposuctions it, and moves it to another area, like your breasts or cheekbones. “With traditional fat transfer, it’s about restoring volume. And because it’s your own living tissue, it can integrate beautifully and give you very natural-looking results,” says dermatologist Shereene Idriss, medical director and founder of Idriss Dermatology in New York City. “But what’s interesting is that fat isn’t just fat. It also contains regenerative cells, including adipose-derived stem cells, and growth factors that may help improve the surrounding tissue over time. Nanofat is where it gets really exciting.”

Nanofat is part of a new class of regenerative aesthetics offered by dermatologists and plastic surgeons, treatments that don’t just make you look better, but rather address the signs of aging from the inside out by stimulating collagen or repairing tissue. Imagine taking that same fat from a traditional fat transfer and processing it into a fine, milky liquid rich in your own stem cells and growth factors, then injecting or microneedling it back into your face—that’s nanofat.

“Instead of using it to restore volume, you’re using it to potentially improve skin quality itself,” says Idriss. “These cells and growth factors communicate with surrounding tissue and can stimulate processes involved in collagen production, tissue repair, and blood vessel formation. So you’re not just trying to fill a wrinkle. You’re trying to improve the skin itself—its thickness, resilience, and texture—so that wrinkle potentially becomes less noticeable.”

The best areas for nanofat injections include under the eye, a notoriously risky area for hyaluronic acid fillers, and the cheekbones. “The ideal patient for nanofat is someone who's starting to notice thinner, crepier skin, fine lines, or changes in skin texture, especially around the eyes, neck, or chest, and wants to improve skin quality rather than add volume,” adds Idriss, who both has had the treatment done and offers it in her practice. Plus, Ogilvie notes, nanofat doesn’t attract water the way fillers do, so it won’t cause under-eye puffiness (a common post-filler complaint). But because nanofat doesn’t restore volume as much as it reinvigorates your skin, you can inject it almost anywhere you’re noticing signs of aging.

“We do it under tumescent local anesthesia, so you’re awake the entire time, and recovery is usually pretty quick,” says Idriss. “I personally had nanofat and fat transfer in June and was back at work the next day. The biggest downside is some soreness at the fat extraction site, which usually lasts a few days, and you’ll want to avoid strenuous exercise for about a week. Because there’s no general anesthesia, you’re also avoiding the grogginess and recovery associated with being put under.”

Like any cosmetic treatment, it has risks and downsides. Experts note that nanofat isn’t the right treatment for anyone looking to restore significant volume loss or seeking instant results. “Improvement in skin quality can vary from person to person, and it takes months to see the full results,” Idriss adds. “There are rare but serious risks, including infection, vascular complications, and even vision loss when injecting certain areas of the face.” But patients who have tried the procedure say the recovery is quick and easy, with results compounding over weeks, though they ultimately fade away faster than traditional injectables.

Nanofat injections sit somewhere between microfat and hyaluronic acid fillers. Microfat can be lumpy and unpredictable when injected, sometimes leading to lopsided results. Plus, in any fat transfer, only a small amount of fat survives. Fillers, on the other hand, offer long-lasting precision and volume restoration, but patients are tired of looking overfilled and unnaturally plump. Nanofat helps your skin look naturally healthier, fresher, and firmer, at least for a while. After all, it’s made from you.

“I look at this as something you can do periodically throughout your life, but what you do in between treatments is just as important. And that’s why I don’t believe in demonizing any one treatment, including filler,” says Idriss. “When used appropriately, for the right indication, these treatments can all work beautifully together. It’s about knowing what you need, when you need it, and when enough is enough.”

#6744. I Walked After Every Meal for Two Months, and My Digestion and Energy Levels Have Never Been Better

It’s almost 9:00 p.m., and my work dinner in Manhattan’s SoHo neighborhood is finally coming to a close. A 2022 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine found that light-intensity walking reduced post-meal glucose by almost 17 percent. We know walking can do wonders—it can clear the mind, help you lean out, and even boost dopamine—but why is it a solution for feeling less full and avoiding afternoon crashes? “When you walk, your muscles use glucose for energy,” Edens explains. “Muscle contractions move glucose transporters called GLUT4 to the surface of muscle cells, helping draw glucose out of the bloodstream through a pathway that doesn’t depend on insulin.” Since your legs, the vehicle for walking, contain some of the biggest muscles in your body, it makes sense that walking effectively stimulates muscle contractions that move glucose transporters. “Walking breaks are a simple way to support blood sugar management while getting your steps in,” she adds.

While many rely on peptides, green powders, and “magic” supplements, walking is an easy, mindless (and free) way to improve digestion and prevent blood-sugar crashes. “Even two to five minutes after a meal can help, ideally before the glucose peak, typically 60 to 90 minutes after eating,” Kiran Krishnan, a research microbiologist, the founder and chief science officer of the Biome Learning Center, and Dose key opinion leader, adds.

I often joke after large meals that I will have to be rolled home. The good news: Walking can also reduce the overwhelming feeling of fullness. “Walking also helps the gut physically,” Krishnan says, citing a study that found that a slow walk after a meal significantly shortened the time it took the stomach to empty half its contents. “For people prone to bloating, mild [walking] cut intestinal gas retention from 45 percent to 24 percent and eased symptoms,” he adds. Plus, Krishnan says studies show reduced LDL cholesterol levels and improved liver function in those who walked after meals.

Then there’s good old gravity, says Caitlin Hosmer, a Maine-based registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry. “Gravity helps too, which is part of why walking may help people who feel uncomfortably full or get reflux after meals,” she explains. “Gentle movement and staying upright help food move out of the stomach and along the digestive tract.”

Ten minutes isn’t long, but studies show it’s just as effective as a longer walk. A 2025 study published by PubMed Central found that a 10-minute walk lowered glucose measurements by 6 percent and a 30-minute walk lowered glucose levels by 5 percent, meaning just 10 minutes of walking is all you need to help reduce a post-meal glucose spike. If you can walk for 30 minutes, an hour, or even longer—that’s great. But just 10 minutes can work just as well.

What matters most is how quickly you get moving. “Glucose typically peaks 60 to 90 minutes after eating, and moving before that peak is what blunts the spike,” Hosmer says. “A walk puts your muscles to work just as glucose from the meal is flooding into the bloodstream.” At first, I questioned whether I had to walk after every meal. “Put your effort where it counts most: after your largest, most carbohydrate-rich meal,” Hosmer advises, adding that for most, that’s after dinner, but walking after all three meals is ideal.

When in a pinch, I would get up and walk around the office floor and, at home, do air squats, which engage the same large leg muscles as walking and can be just as effective as a post-meal walk.

It may feel strange to pencil in time to walk after every meal, but the benefits I have noticed surpass any inconvenience or awkwardness. I have fewer crashes after I eat, whereas I previously would feel lethargic after every meal. I’ve also noticed improved digestion. It’s also not meant to feel daunting or like a chore. “Your post-dinner walk isn’t a workout. It’s a way of helping your body handle the meal,” Hosmer says. “The one real ‘wrong way’ is skipping it because you think it has to be long or perfect. Even a few minutes of walking, started soon after you finish eating, can blunt the blood sugar rise, and it may help your digestion too.”

You can really put them anywhere you want, but keep in mind where you’ll need extra power accessibility and where it won’t be an eyesore, if that’s a concern. I’m also a big fan of in-drawer charging outlets.

#6714. After Launching McGee & Co., Shea McGee Shares 8 Lessons for Opening a Store

Speaking to you now, on the other side of its debut, there are some things we only learned through actually doing. From the importance of developing a training manual to the do-or-die value of a soft launch, here are eight hard-won learnings from our McGee & Co. store launch.

It was important to us that the shop feel like the homes we create for our clients—layered and collected, with a variety of patterns and objects for your eye to explore. Despite the fact that the dollars-per-square-foot model is the traditional method of conceiving an in-store assortment, it was more important for us to remain true to ourselves with this first location: we’re a designer-led brand, not a merchant-led one.

“I’m getting to see our products through new eyes—and I was the one that concepted them!” says Shea. “When you see them in a store setting, pieces can have a lot more detail than you can see online.”

We started by coming up with the sections we envisioned, like a kids’ area, a kitchen zone, and so on. Then we created planograms to see how much we could actually fit, and reconciled with our merchandising team to see what made business sense. Sometimes, that meant pivoting from the early vision. A lighting gallery we had planned, for instance, ended up shifting: We loved the idea, but when we looked at the percentage of lighting to the rest of our sales, we could see clearly that it was a smarter business decision to use this space to showcase more furniture. Instead of the sprawling lighting gallery we had imagined, we created a moody lounge area that can flex to showcase different pieces.

We don’t think small: Our aspirations are to grow nationwide. But however much we scale, it’s critical that we not lose the warm, personal feeling that comes from a standalone store. As McGee & Co. grows its brick-and-mortar operation, that’s one of my biggest goals. And there’s another layer here, too: Being a personality-led business, the team members in our store aren’t just representing our brand—they’re representing me, for better or worse. The design world can sometimes feel closed-off and unapproachable, and it’s always been the McGee mantra—in our content, in our personality on social media, in how we work with clients—to be warm and accessible. Now that attitude needs to trickle down to our store associates, too.

Before we opened, I spent months developing a comprehensive training guide for our associates. It’s essentially Design 101, McGee-style, covering everything from how many inches I place a rug away from a wall, to how to pair and arrange accent tables, to how to layer bedding. We cover every level of the decorating process. Thank goodness for AI—it’s been a lifesaver: I’ll narrate as we go on our walk-throughs, and use those notes as the basis for the training manual. And it’s not a one-and-done deal. We’ll continue to add to this document as we evolve and grow.

As folks wandered in, we learned, and we tweaked. And two weeks later, we opened officially. That soft-launch period was vital for us in getting the kinks out.

Located in Salt Lake City’s Trolley Square, the “shop,” as Shea calls it, is 7,500 square feet.

People who are aware of your product can go see it in person, give it a sit test, think about it, and then finish the sale online. In store, we can educate them, give them styling suggestions, and show off the quality of the product in ways we can’t online. Even for us, we’re discovering new things about our assortment by merchandising it in the store—we’re seeing it with fresh eyes, and we know our customers will too.

I want to be able to say that we know what it takes to run a store and to make the shopper experience in it as smooth as possible. For instance, I want customers to know how to get to our free design services, and to have a great experience using them. If we can do that, we know we’re on our way to some of our loftier goals, too.

I’m not under any illusions here: This first store is a baptism by fire. Not only are we opening a flagship and planting seeds for our future locations—we’re also doing it in Q4, right before the holidays. But my hope is that at the end of it, we can catch our breath and take a moment to reflect on what worked, what didn’t, and then get to work fixing it. In this business, honesty with yourself is the only way up.

The mint is fresh and slightly spicy, far from anything toothpaste-adjacent, layered with warm nutmeg, sweet geranium, and a soft, velvety rose. It’s a cologne I wouldn’t have immediately associated with the house, until the drydown, anchored by that signature Diptyque patchouli, brings depth and candle-flickering craftsmanship the brand does so uniquely well. Fresh, aromatic, and with a bit of nostalgia baked in, this is a layered gem to wear for all your mint julep-fueled warm-weather soirées.

Staff Notes: "Diptyque's Eau de Minthé is what I imagine Michelin-level mixologists aspire toward when they're crafting their cocktails. At first sniff, it's garden-fresh floral notes are uplifted with subtle minty notes before settling into deep patchouli notes that give it a well-rounded experience that's equally light and grounding." —Gerald Ortiz, style commerce writer

To me, Tom Ford has four all-timers in his Private Blend canon: Oud Wood, Neroli Portofino, Tuscan Leather, and the sultry tobacco-vanilla duality that is Tobacco Vanille. When this spicy, warm gourmand landed in 2007, it helped redefine what a tobacco fragrance could smell like. The note historically skewed fringe or overtly macho, but Ford, of course, reframed it as chic and decadent, pairing rich tobacco leaf with vanilla, ginger, cocoa, woods, and dried fruits. The signature aroma is plush and addictive, with a near-edible warmth that never tips into excess, as sweet vanilla and spice slowly bloom through the drydown. It’s a masterclass in softening a once-polarizing note into something smooth, inviting, and alluring, and still one of the house’s most widely admired scents today.

Staff Notes: “What's not to love about Tobacco Vanille? It's smooth, smoky, sexy, and just the right amount of sweet. Far better than smoking an actual cigarette—and more luxurious, too.” — Trishna Rikhy, associate social media manager

Musc Ravageur has a devoted following, with fans reaching for this alluring, sultry, spiced gem as both a signature scent and for date nights alike. Released in 2000, it helped usher musk (as a note) into a broader, chicer tier of perfumery. The composition is beautifully layered, opening with herbal lavender, spicy coriander, and a hint of bright citrus before melting into clove and a heady rose, all resting on a rich base of musk, vanilla, tonka, amber, and patchouli. Besides being a benchmark for animalic gourmands, it’s one of the most intricately composed scents out there, where, if you read the pyramid, you can pick out each note as it unfolds. We fragrance fanatics love that.

Staff Notes: "This isn't called an icon by accident. I keep coming back to Musc Ravageur year after year. One spray of the warm vanilla, spiced cinnamon, perfectly balanced with clean musk is all it takes for pure chemistry. This is the kind of scent that turns heads." —Reginald Christian, commerce director

London-based Perfumer H has carved out its own unique path, from its apothecary-meets-indie-meets-luxury vibe to its often one-word, subtle creations that smell distinctive yet approachable. Ink is a case in point. As a self-proclaimed bookworm, I was already sold on its inspiration: late nights spent in dimly lit libraries. But the scent itself is an intriguing study, weaving together woods, papyrus (because paper, of course), heady rose, spice, warm ambers, and an incense-like, meditative quality. The result is an addictive, page-turning woody floral composition that feels both cerebral and wearable, the kind of fragrance you rock when you're quite possibly the coolest dude in the room—and likely the most well-read, too.

Staff Notes: “Perfumer H pretty much had me at their tagline: Making Air Visible. But then I smelled Ink, and was completely seduced. It transports me to lamp-lit reading rooms and old-world ateliers. And even better, because it's so light, I can apply it multiple times a day.” —Louis Cheslaw, contributing commerce editor

Well before skin scents became a thing, the indie aromatic heavyweights at D.S. & Durga dropped their now cult-classic I Don’t Know What Eau de Parfum in the late 2010s. It is less a traditional fragrance and more of an enhancer (though for many, it is the main aromatic event). The polar opposite of beast-mode projection, subtlety is its byword, with a verdant, woody air scent you lean in to experience rather than one that announces itself across a room. Iso E Super, a versatile synthetic aroma chemical known for its longevity and velvety skin-like texture, does most of the heavy, or rather light, lifting, blended with bergamot, vetiver, and sandalwood to create one of the best fresh skin scents of all time. Wear it on its own or use it to boost and layer with other fragrances. I don’t know what else to say; it all works.

Staff Notes: “I Don’t Know What has a gentle and clean scent that feels like a whisper compared to more bombastic fragrances out there. The light citrusy hit of bergamot is exactly what I need when layering scents during the warmer parts of the year.” —Jordan Bowman, commerce editor

One of the genre’s most renowned reference vetivers, this Guerlain classic has influenced not just every vetiver cologne, but many fragrances today, period. The composition is less the smoky, rooty side of the note and more early-morning, dewy freshness, clean and crisp, with just enough (good dirty) earthiness underneath to keep it interesting. Citrus and spice lift the opening before it settles into a grassy core, rounded out by touches of tobacco and woods. More than any grand concept, it smells like stepping outside early in the morning, when everything still feels a little cool, damp, and alive. With longevity and that timeless cologne feel, it has remained a benchmark since its 1950 debut and a signature scent for so many.

Staff Notes: “This bottle has been in my rotation for a few years, and it’s a benchmark for anyone into fresh, verdant scents. To my nose, it’s always smelled of drinking a Negroni in a five-star hotel room, or someplace equally polished. I spritz it particularly in the colder months whenever I’m set to meet new people, because I find it's an easy way to make a good first impression.” —Michael Nolledo, commerce director

So many folks whose taste I admire wear Colonia. Endlessly polished and easy to wear, it’s about as close as it gets to a perfect daily driver. First introduced in 1916, the Italian icon still feels as relevant today as it did over a century ago, built on that signature blend of citrus, herbs, and woods, wearing its subtlety and old-world refinement on its (freshly pressed) sleeve. The opening is citrus crisp, before settling into herbaceous notes of lavender, rosemary, and vetiver over a smooth base of patchouli, sandalwood, and soft amber warmth. It carries that classic barbershop fougère-meets-Italian-citrus-grove energy, a cologne your dad (and his dad) wore to work and date nights alike—but that smells just as fresh on you today.

Staff Notes: “'It's hard to pick the perfect cologne for the office’ is something I might say if I'd never smelled this bottle of Acqua di Parma. It smells like biting into a sexy tangerine, but in an inoffensive way that won't raise any eyebrows in your desk clump." —Doug Guida, social producer

Every exemplary fragrance house has a defining undercurrent. With Aesop (who's just as well known for its scents as its skincare), it's a botanical, earthy, elevated thread running through those amber-hued bottles. Rōzu Eau de Parfum is a woody, aromatic, rose-forward composition, not of the sweet or traditional variety, but for those who appreciate richer, headier takes. Rose, alongside iris, is one of my favorite floral notes, and here it's handled with nuance, pairing the dewy, velvety accord with a lift of citrus, then grounding it with sandalwood for creaminess, guaiac wood for incense-leaning flourishes, and vetiver for earthiness. A jasmine accord adds depth to the green, earthy rose aroma—another indie homerun from the Aussie house.

Staff Notes: “I thought I wasn’t a rose-cologne person, but then Aesop mixed in some shiso and jasmine, and it was love at first whiff. Rōzu (the Japanese pronunciation of the English word “rose”), is my go-to when I want a unisex scent that’ll last just a bit longer than the work day, because I don’t want to be That Cologne Wearer at the gym.” —Mari Herrema, marketing and production manager

Perhaps no cologne is as referenced, memed, reviewed, and well-known in modern mainstream fragrance culture as Sauvage. The line can get a little confusing, but it all comes back to a core DNA built on citrus, pepper, and ambroxan, just pushed in different directions. The EDT is ultra-fresh, the EDP adds warmth, and the Parfum goes denser. Then there’s Elixir, the darker, spicier, more aromatic outlier. It’s my favorite of the group, leaning into evening territory with nutmeg and cinnamon bringing gourmand warmth, grapefruit adding lift, and classic lavender giving it that fougère backbone, all resting on a heady base of amber, patchouli, sandalwood, and a subtly sweet licorice accord. Elixir is the refined, yet still slightly mischievous side of Sauvage—spicy, warm, fresh, and it smells good on pretty much everyone.

Staff Notes: “I was gifted a bottle of Dior Sauvage, I think, to give to a man in my life. But I love men's colognes so I decided to keep it for myself. When winter rolled around, I gave it a try and I noticed pretty immediately that the scent had pull. (I got the most interest from an Uber driver who was terribly into my fragrance until I told him it wasn't for women.) Somehow it gave me that fresh-out-of-the-shower burst of energy while also smelling woody, warm, and sexy—perfect for cooler days. I'm happy I didn't give it away.” —Savannah Sobrevilla, associate editor

The fragrances above are all GQ staffer favorites, and I worked closely with the team to bring to life what makes each one worthy of a spot in your olfactory arsenal. Of course, scent is wildly subjective. One person's all-time favorite daily driver might leave someone else scrunching their nose. Tomato, tomahto. That's part of the fun, after all. No universal crowd-pleaser exists, and what smells incredible on one guy might not work for the next.

Now, here's where I get to shine a light on a few personal favorites. From modern classics to under-the-radar niche gems and newer fumes, these are the colognes I love right now, along with a few I think deserve more attention, spanning a range of price points. Consider this a little extra olfactory inspiration, courtesy of yours truly.

I've smelled pretty much everything indie-luxe stalwart Byredo has put out over the years (including the new, fantastic Future Memories), but it wasn't until recently that I finally got my nose on Oud Immortel. It brings together just about every cold-weather note I adore, from oud and smoky incense to earthy patchouli, moss, and tobacco, weaving them into something sultry and almost decadent. But there's also that signature Byredo airiness, an almost skin-scent-like softness that keeps it from feeling too dense or overpowering.

When I first experienced Terre d'Hermès years ago, it immediately became a staple in my rotation. It's one of the best designer colognes ever, and I was, and still am, obsessed with its woody, citrusy elegance. But while the original is best suited to cooler months, with Eau Intense Vétiver, you've got an equally chic, warm-weather counterpart.

French grooming favorite Horace knows how to make fragrances that smell like they should cost far more. Inspired by Freddie Mercury's legendary 41st birthday bash at Ibiza's Pikes Hotel in 1987, this is one intriguing aromatic. The name sounds summery, but to my nose, it's more like a winter holiday celebration, with crisp fir balsam and cypress lending an evergreen character.

I’ve been intrigued by New York-based Vilhelm Parfumerie for years, right down to those distinctive bottles shaped like miniature decanters, and Morning Chess is so, so smooth. It’s a blend of citrus and amber you don’t come across often: fresh and bright on the surface, just like the morning, but a few swift chess moves later, it settles into a regal, leathery warmth with an earthy undercurrent. Checkmate.

I’m a big fan of buzzy Brooklyn house Rite of Way, as all three (yes, just three) of its fragrances are so wonderfully indie and cool. I’m normally not one for aquatics, but its inaugural scent, Outer Realm, won me over by pairing citrus and marine notes with incense to create a scent that’s anything but banal. Citron and grapefruit bring a sharp, juicy brightness, while sea spray and lotus lend an airy, almost otherworldly quality. Then, as it settles, amber emerges, giving the composition a warmth you don't typically associate with aquatics.

Quentin Bisch is one of the most renowned and daring perfumers working today, and to my nose, he has a way with woods, giving them a richness and depth that's become a calling card. His Bois Impérial for Essential Parfums, a niche house known for making high-quality fragrances more accessible, has become a sensation on fragrance forums, and for good reason. Built around Akigalawood, a patchouli-derived ingredient with a rich woody character, it has a spicy, peppery bite, while an invigorating freshness keeps it from veering too heavy.

Los Angeles–based Perfumehead’s exemplary Canadian Tuxedo reminds me of my childhood on Long Beach Island: Evening light dancing off the driftwood, when everything felt quiet, introspective, and creatively stirring. On paper, it sits in the amber family, but it wears like a meeting of resinous woods and coastal air, lifted by bitter orange leaf and coriander. As it settles, the fragrance takes on a warm, herbal quality with a touch of spice and a soft, musky finish that feels almost windswept.

I respect the citrus fragrance family immensely, but as a leading note, it’s never been my everyday go-to. L’Or de Louis completely reframed it for me: a citrus shaped by smoke and gourmand tones that’s become a fixture in my olfactory arsenal. Aromatic indie heavyweights Arquiste have a knack for weaving history into their fragrances, and this one takes us to Louis XIV’s Versailles, where bonfires burned near citrus trees at the Grand Orangery. The gold flecks in the bottle are another nod to its grandiose inspiration, resembling, to me, little flickers of fire suspended in the glass.

I recently watched a podcast where Sarah Sherman lamented that she could no longer get her hands on Santa Maria Novella's Patchouli. That's the kind of attachment people develop to the centuries-old Florentine house, helped also by its gorgeous bottles. I like Opoponax: a honeyed resin with a smoky, incense-like quality. Think monks in cloisters, holy water, dimly lit chapels, and quiet garden courtyards.

Even as Icelandic house Fischersund gains serious attention stateside, it still feels like the cool kid in the room, with a noir-leaning sensibility and music and art woven into its indie DNA. I adore No. 23, the flagship scent, which opens dark and smoky, almost charred, before softening into resinous woods, spice, and a hint of sweet citrus with a faint animalic undercurrent.

It's not often I get to shine a light on a gem in Creed's storied catalog that isn't one of its many heavy hitters, but Bois du Portugal is a bit of a slept-on classic that currently has me hooked. An old-school gentleman's fougère, it's fresh, woody, spicy, and above all aromatic, built from little more than bergamot, a hefty dose of lavender, and a blend of woods and patchouli—proof that a few top-quality elements, blended exceptionally well, can speak volumes. Created in 1987, it still smells every bit as relevant today.

Iberian Leather isn't for beginners; it's the kind of scent you have to vibe with to appreciate its artistry and complexity. A richly layered fragrance, it feels like wandering through a damp, historic castle as aged wood, worn leather, and the scent of nearby flower beds wafts through the night air. And the bottle is flat-out stunning, too, looking like something straight out of Game of Thrones.

Ever wondered about the difference between an “EDT” and an “EDP?” It's not actually all that complicated. The primary differentiator comes down to the percentage of perfumed oils used in the product, which dictates how brands classify their scents. These days, we tend to treat “cologne” as a catchall term for any men’s fragrance, but technically speaking, the term is just one of many you should know about. These are the rest.

The classifications above aren't an exact science, of course, but the concentration levels they allude to are a solid indicator of where to set your expectations, and how much a fragrance will cost.

Colognes right now have more personality than ever, and more and more gentlemen are moving beyond the one-bottle approach. Plenty of guys still swear by a signature scent, but many are building rotations based on budget, mood, and setting. As Jake Levy and Matt Belanger, founders of NYC fragrance shop Stéle, told us, “Today folks want variety: one to go out, one to go to work, one for date night, and even one to start a conversation.”

If you’re starting from scratch, keep it simple: start with two bottles—one clean, versatile daily driver and one with a little more edge for nights out. From there, add by season or occasion, like say, something fresh and citrusy for warm weather, something woody or spicy for when the temps drop.

Keep this in mind, too: classic fragrance families like citrus, woods, gourmand, and floral will always have a place, but modern perfumery is more wide-open than ever. As Carlos Huber of Arquiste puts it, if you love smoky oud or animalic leather, there’s no reason it can’t be your best cologne. Wear what fits your aura, not your algorithm.

As one of GQ’s regular fragrance writers, I’ve spent years testing colognes and reporting on the category, while the wider shopping team has spent an inordinate amount of time thinking about what deserves space on a shelf. Together, we cast a wide net, considering household names, indie favorites, heritage heavyweights, and artisanal imprints.

Because not every guy wears cologne the same way, or for the same reasons, we pay close attention to context and budget. We test scents on skin over full days of wear, tracking how they opened, dried down, projected, and lasted over time. Some needed to work at the office, others on date nights, others as an everyday signature. Each winning pick was judged on scent quality, longevity, versatility, and price, along with the simplest test of all: whether we actually wanted to keep spraying it.

The result is a list that reflects a range of tastes, occasions, and price points—from tried-and-true stalwarts to modern disruptors, affordable go-tos to wildly expensive (but wildly worth-it) flexes.

#6697. GQ’s Men of the Year Dinner May Have Found the Next James Bond

Cocktail hour was drawing to a close; the traditional pre-party dinner for the 31st edition of GQ’s legendary Men of the Year event, presented by Johnnie Walker Blue Label, was about to kick off. GQ’s Designer of the Year, Celine’s Michael Rider, and our Director of the Year, Denis Villeneuve, were already posted up inside the Chateau Marmont’s lobby lounge; the guests lingering just past the entrance included Odysseus himself, Matt Damon, building with his Ocean’s partner-in-crime Don Cheadle before pivoting to greet Chris Rock—like Damon, one of GQ’s three Men of the Year issue cover stars. (Andrew Garfield, subject of that third cover, had rolled in a few minutes earlier, resplendently coiffed, immediately gathering his After the Hunt co-star Ayo Edebiri and director Luca Guadagnino into a reunion hug.)

But among the Men of the Year veterans in attendance—like Rock, who scored his first MOTY cover in 1999, circa Bigger and Blacker—there were also plenty of first-timers from the new class of news-makers and paradigm-shakers celebrated in GQ’s 2026 Breakouts portfolio. And that varied mix—of longtime icons and undeniable up-and-comers, from Owen Thiele and Inde Navarette to I Play Rocky’s Anthony Ippolito and electro-rock duo Fcukers—made for a veritable particle accelerator of improbable collisions.

Chicken Shop Date’s Amelia Dimoldenberg in deep confab with Tinashe. Tony’s Stavros Halkias in an across-the-table comedy summit with Chris Rock. Journalist Pablo Torre, whose reporting on Kawhi Leonard and the LA Clippers salary-cap shenanigans shook the NBA, engrossed in conversation with GQ’s own Zach Baron and Villeneuve, who’d just signed off on the prints—for real this time—for his third and final Dune movie, soon to shake the walls of nearly every IMAX theater on Earth.

And Villeneuve wasn’t the only auteur on hand: Between the Dune mastermind, Misty Green writer/director/star Rock, Club Kid’s similarly slashed Jordan Firstman, the La Bola Negra writer-directors jointly known as Los Javis (Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrosi) and Guadagnino, who upon arriving had almost immediately located a cinematically moody lamplit corner to hold court in, the Chateau’s lobby was, quipped GQ Global Editorial Director Adam Baidawi, “an incredible room to be an out-of-work actor in.”

Because journalism doesn’t take a night off—not even GQ’s biggest night—Baidawi also tried, during his opening remarks, to pry some info regarding the identity of the new James Bond (off the record, just between us) out of Villeneuve.

As the director politely demurred—hey, we had to try—another guest piped up from across the table. “Wait,” said Matt Damon, as Baidawi passed the former superspy the mic. “I have a question. Just a thought experiment. What if James Bond were older? And American?” (In the unlikely event that Damon just booked a second iconic-superspy role, you heard it here first.)

Toast time was upon us: Rosalind Eleazar, raising a glass to Chris Rock, recalled her Zoom audition with Rock for Misty Green, which was followed in short order by a missed call from an American number and the two-word text message that would change her life: “Chris Rock.” (That’s right—when Chris Rock calls you from an unknown number and gets your voice mail, he follows up with a text that just says “Chris Rock,” an absolute power-move that is, unfortunately, hard to replicate by the rest of us.)

“Thinking I was being rather witty, I replied, ‘Rosalind Eleazar,’” Eleazar said. “I waited eagerly for his response and—not so much as a laughing emoji. And I quickly realized that I am communicating with one of the funniest people on the planet and I should probably just leave the funny to him.”

Eleazar recalled Rock telling her on set, My movie—your character, giving her the freedom to discover Misty on her own. “And that’s a rare gift,” she said, “and one that I will always be grateful for.”

“I just love you so, so dearly,” Garfield responded, “and I hope we can be in each other's lives forever.”
