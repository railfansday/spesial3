#6939. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The company also cited an IDC projection that the number of AI agents could reach 1.3 billion by 2028.

Sophos researchers reported observing attackers use AI agents within a customer's network to build and test nearly 80 malware modules.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6934. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6928. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6922. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6907. Report: Global Ransomware Attacks Rise 23% in Q3

Global ransomware activity increased 23% in the third quarter of 2026, with organizations in North America and Europe accounting for the largest share of attacks, according to cybersecurity firm NCC Group.

The company's Cyber Threat Intelligence Report Review of Q3 2026 recorded 2,865 ransomware victim claims, up from 2,325 in the previous quarter.

Despite the quarterly increase, attacks declined 26% month over month in September to 821. North America recorded the most attacks that month with 390, followed by Europe with 211.

The industrial sector accounted for 29% of quarterly attacks, while Qilin remained the most active ransomware group for the sixth consecutive quarter, recording 365 victims. The Gentlemen followed with 356, and INC Ransom ranked third with 109.

The report highlighted a July cyber attack against a small UK power generation facility that forced operations to stop for approximately four days.

Although the incident did not affect the national electricity grid, it demonstrated how cyber attacks can disrupt physical infrastructure.

The security researchers said the incident was consistent with techniques previously associated with Iranian-affiliated campaigns, including attacks against internet-exposed industrial controllers.

However, the UK government has not formally attributed the attack, and the techniques used remain unconfirmed.

The report also identified growing security challenges as organizations connect traditional IT networks with operational technology (OT) systems for remote maintenance and centralized management.

The firm recommended network segmentation, stronger privileged-access controls, and continuous monitoring of OT environments.

The research found that more than 40 vulnerabilities affecting enterprise applications, VPN appliances, e-mail gateways, and management platforms were added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog during the quarter.

The report examined Gunra, a ransomware-as-a-service operation that has targeted 53 victims across multiple industries as of Q3.

According to the cybersecurity company, Gunra exploited vulnerabilities CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 in internet-facing Fortinet devices to gain access to corporate networks.

The group also used stolen credentials, session hijacking, and administrative tools to move between systems before deploying ransomware.

Other emerging ransomware groups included Deadlock and Storm, with the latter claiming 78 victims by the end of September.

Researchers also observed initial access brokers advertising compromised VPN, Remote Desktop Protocol, and SSH access, alongside access to cloud identities, source code repositories, and security management platforms.

The quarterly review also highlighted BraZetsu, an AI-enhanced Windows malware framework that evolved into a specialized initial access toolkit.

According to the report, BraZetsu uses AI models to automate victim profiling, analyze compromised environments, and identify valuable corporate systems.

The framework searches for enterprise software, financial data, digital certificates, and cloud credentials, helping operators identify compromised access that can be sold to other cyber criminals.

The cybersecurity firm also identified risks involving legitimate AI agents, including prompt injection, excessive permissions, and unintended access to external systems.

The report cited incidents involving U.S. and Australian government systems as examples of agents accessing resources beyond their intended scope.

Researchers warned that these incidents do not necessarily involve malicious intent but can create consequences resembling conventional cyber intrusions.

#6901. Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

#6895. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

It makes sense, then, that even when she’s inhabiting shadowy figures and dark subject matter, she can still find the light in the women she portrays. Take her much-talked-about role as Circe, the witch who transforms Odysseus’s men into pigs in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey. Morton delivers a haunting performance; just try to take your eyes off her as she builds the intensity, humming as she shapes the men’s faces into beasts with her hands. After the epic drama’s massive opening this summer, in a cast full of A-listers like Matt Damon, Anne Hathaway, and Robert Pattinson, it was Morton that everyone was talking about. Yet Morton saw there was more to Circe than her hardened exterior. Though Morton has been acting for most of her life, her role as Circe has brought newfound attention. “She’s baaack,” Morton says of the buzz around her now. “I’ve always been there—I work quietly and carefully and meticulously, but having the opportunity to be in a Christopher Nolan film has meant that there’s more visibility.” After starting her career in British TV, she charmed audiences with her portrayal of a mute woman in 1999’s Sweet and Lowdown without her ever saying a word, earning Morton her first Oscar nomination. And then for a cohort of celebrities like John Cleese, Chris Hemsworth, and biohacker Bryan Johnson, there’s the belief that stem cell banking could be a well from which youth, cryopreserved, springs eternal. It’s like heavily investing in a call option you hope will pan out later. After visiting a clinic in Panama, Hemsworth posted on Instagram: “With this kind of technology available, I plan to keep making films well into my 100s (or 1,500 in Asgard).”

The discourse around stem cells, according to several experts, ranges from “tremendous” and “promising” to “wildly outrageous,” “misleading,” “harmless nonsense,” and “potentially very dangerous.” “The phrase stem cells has almost become a health halo,” says Timothy Caulfield, professor at the Faculty of Law and the School of Public Health and research director of the Health Law Institute at the University of Alberta. To understand the buzz, you have to go back to 2012, when Shinya Yamanaka, MD, PhD, co-won a Nobel Prize with John B. Gurdon for discovering that you can take mature cells and turn them back into an embryonic state. They called these new, artificially created stem cells induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells. Two decades later, Yamanaka’s findings are now being cautiously used on humans. In March 2026, iPS cell therapies were given conditional and time-limited marketing approval for seven years in Japan for heart failure and Parkinson’s disease, while studies continue to determine their efficacy and safety in clinical trials. In the U.S. in May, a patient received iPS cells in a clinical trial to study treatment of retinitis pigmentosa.

“Stem cells don’t do magic. They do biology,” cautions John O’Neill, PhD, cofounder of Sweet Chemistry and chief scientific officer of Xylyx Bio, Inc., a regenerative medicine company. “They may be capable of incredible, remarkable, and profound therapeutic benefits. But truly understanding, controlling, manufacturing, and scaling [stem cell usage] will take years and years and bazillions of dollars and an incredible amount of human power.” Aside from the conditional exceptions in Japan for treating heart failure and Parkinson’s disease, iPS cells are not available for medical use, clarifies Knut Woltjen, Dior Reverse Aging board member and associate professor at Kyoto University’s Center for iPS Cell Research and Application (the same center that Yamanaka helped create and direct in Japan). He says they “are primarily a research material.”

Nevertheless, do even a cursory Google search and you’ll find people proclaiming that some type of stem cell treatment “worked wonders.” In many cases, it’s uncertain where these stem cells are being sourced from. But some experts are skeptical that these treatments always involve the real thing. Caulfield calls it an “exploitative industry,” saying that “it’s gotten so bad that if you see the phrase ‘stem cells,’ I think that is a red flag for bunk.”

Even if people were receiving real stem cells, those cells can only do so much; many of the treatments marketed as such or as regenerative therapies have not undergone clinical testing to determine their safety and effectiveness, and it would likely take time before they become widely available for use in a nonmedical setting. “Today, the timing of marketing is quicker than scientific or research timing,” says Virginie Couturaud, Christian Dior Parfums scientific communication director.

The banks and places that collect, store, and process them are regulated by the FDA. Still, some states have their own specific licensing, inspection, and oversight requirements that prompt experts to ask, “If you bank your own stem cells, can they be used? Because it won’t necessarily fit into the regulatory environment,” says Robin Smith, MD, a partner at Exoceuticals, an exosome-based skin care brand. Federally, stem cell products that are used as medical treatments need the FDA’s approval and are regulated by the FDA, although Smith notes that several states have carved out exceptions for indications such as pain, wound healing, or orthopedics. In July, Wyoming enacted the Stem Cell Freedom Act, which allows physicians to administer certain non-FDA-approved therapies using a patient’s own stem cells and prohibits medical board sanctions. Wyoming State Senator Eric Barlow told local press: “We’ve got to press on the federal government because they’re not approving things fast enough.”

There is also the concern that because stem cells are so powerful, using your own could carry markers of the very same diseases or ailments that you are trying to treat. “They have so much potency and ability, they could become the wrong thing in the body and get the wrong signals,” O’Neill says. An investigative report by The New York Times in 2024 also found that some banked cord blood samples were contaminated, and that some parents were misled about their value. “We don’t know that the technology that was used X number of years prior will actually still be on par with the quality of the cells that we can generate at that time,” Woltjen says.

There are some proven, heavily researched stem cell therapies, including for certain types of cancer. But most experts don’t approve of using current therapies in a non-medical setting, pointing to the lack of scientific evidence, clinical testing, and regulations. “If you’ve paid $50,000 to go travel someplace to help your bad knee, there’s a placebo response. Unproven therapies have long been supported using testimonials and anecdotes. Good, independent clinical trials that show efficacy just don’t exist,” Caulfield says. He’d like to see more, not less, oversight. “People will say, ‘Your evil bureaucracy, the FDA, is keeping you from accessing these clearly efficacious therapies.’ But you’re not seeing as much regulation as is warranted. There have been situations where clinics have been held to task, but the FDA just doesn’t have the resources. If you layer RFK Jr. on top, the political will to take action has dissipated significantly over the last two years.”

This is not necessarily to discount stem cell–related beauty treatments or products. Exosomes, cell by-products that contain lipids and proteins and can be derived from stem cells, are now commonly seen in skin care formulations. “There are legitimate stem cell–derived factors,” O’Neill says, but “they are not the cells themselves,” using the cow and the milk as an analogy. In Seoul, a stem cell beauty treatment called stromal vascular fraction (SVF) extracts stem cells, growth factors, and other cells from fat tissue, and then reinjects them into the skin. Gabriel Chiu, DOM.D., founder and CEO of Beverly Hills Plastic Surgery, adds that there are strict guidelines for how this is performed. He has used banked and fresh stem cells from fat tissue for fat grafting, and says, “Patients have been very happy with the results.”

For people who have banked their stem cells, even if they aren’t sure how or if they will one day be used, the process offers mental reassurance. Cynthia Sakai, founder of the personal care and lifestyle brand Evolvetogether, banked her son’s stem cells and described it as safeguarding: “I remember thinking that this was something we could only do once, at the moment he was born. We could always decide later that we did not need it, but we could never go back and collect it again.” When Smith was later diagnosed with breast cancer, she felt reassured that her stem cells were there. But even she says, “If you bank your own stem cells, can they be used? Because it won’t necessarily fit into the regulatory environment.”

The experts generally agree that stem cell banking is not a must-do procedure. O’Neill doesn’t like to say “never.” But when I ask how he would advise a friend, he says, “I rarely make definitive statements like this, but in 2026, stem cell banking is not worth it. It doesn’t mean it won’t be worth it someday. But someday will not be next year or the year after.”

#6777. Stop Hunting for Pricey Antiques—Designers Say These 9 Humble Finds Give a Home So Much Character

They’re affordable, widely available, and stylish.

It’s any avid thrifter’s dream to stumble upon an extremely valuable piece of decor while browsing the shelves of their favorite antique store. But while you’re hunting for rare treasures, don’t discount the less-expensive finds—they can be the secret to a home that looks instantly filled with character.

“To me, a collected home is much more interesting when everything isn’t perfect or chosen for its monetary value,” designer Lauren Sullivan of Well by Design tells House Beautiful. “Often, it’s the less precious pieces that give a room its personality.”

Even as older generations are continuing to downsize and offload unwanted things to local thrift stores, it still might be hard to find the diamonds in the rough. Keep reading to find out what Sullivan, and a handful of other designers, say you should be on the lookout for on your next thrifting visit.

The most valuable antiques are the ones that have been passed down through generations. In Sullivan’s own bathroom, the designer keeps her great-grandmother’s vanity set on the countertop, its aged finish providing beautiful contrast with the more contemporary marble and finishes. In her bedroom, the same great-grandmother’s small bedside clock found a place on Sullivan’s nightstand.

“Neither is particularly valuable monetarily, but I love incorporating pieces that have been passed down through generations into a home filled with both old and new,” Sullivan says.

Designers love incorporating artwork into homes to add personality and dimension, but Sullivan, as well as designer Becca Meyer of BB Meyer Design, believe that antique shops are where you can really find some hidden gems.

“Some of my favorite pieces of art have come from flea markets or antique shops for very little money,” Meyer says. “I much prefer these to mass produced prints.” You’re more likely to find pieces with aged textures at these types of shops, and that patinaed quality creates an entirely unique look.

Sullivan explains that she’s actually particularly drawn to prints, as well as smaller paintings and silhouettes. She even has an inexpensive print of The Skating Minister in a vintage frame in one of her guest rooms, pictured above. “These smaller, or sometimes quirky, pieces can bring so much personality to a space without requiring a significant investment,” she adds. They may not be originals, but they’re definitely unique.

Old pottery and ceramics are widely available, and they are usually affordable as well. Designer Brooke Snyder of Design Hutch loves how they make any place feel more curated.

The imperfections these handmade pieces carry, from uneven shapes, variations in glaze, or evidence of years of use, are what help these items bring a warmth to a room, particularly when placed near more refined materials, according to Sullivan. They feel humble, maybe even utilitarian, especially when displayed in a kitchen. Or, better yet, used in a kitchen (as long as they’re safe).

“They're beautiful on their own but also so functional for holding utensils or branches or flowers,” Meyer adds. “They make any space feel a little more lived-in and loved and help tell a story—and they don't break the bank!”

“I love incorporating vintage plates, old copper pots, and woven baskets into a home,” explains designer Joe Waroquier. Though these are run-of-the-mill, everyday objects, they can take on and provide an entirely different life when displayed in the home as decor. Mismatched plates on a wall, a collection of copper pots hanging on a rod or resting on shelves in the kitchen, an old crock filled with branches—these items invite in warmth, texture, and more personality.

“I’m particularly drawn to pieces that show their age, whether it’s a little patina, a worn finish, or an imperfect glaze,” he adds. “I love mixing these finds with more contemporary furnishings because that contrast makes a room feel collected and personal rather than overly designed.”

Mirrors can be intriguing focal points of a room, or they can add dimension to a gallery wall. However you want to style them, Snyder loves finding these reflective pieces at antique malls and vintage shops.

“Purchasing something that you don’t see every day from a retailer allows this unique piece to stand out on its own, without feeling like something you see everywhere or feels too simple,” she explains.

As Snyder puts it, antique table lamps provide high visual impact with low investment. Adding one to your accent table or on a bookshelf is an easy way to make your space feel more curated while inviting in another level of ambience with the lighting.

These vintage lamps in the above space, for instance, were bought from a collector and make the primary bedroom feel much more charming and curated. And unlike antique sconces, there’s no need to hardwire these into the wall.

Besides pottery and lamps, designer Ryann Swan Hackett of Ryann Swan Design loves to find and collect other small tabletop decor for her projects. Think candleholders, vintage coffee table books, and trays—things that can layer a little history and individuality into a room or simple vignette.

“If you’re lucky,” Swan Hackett says of the vintage coffee table books, “you’ll find an old inscription inside, which gives the book a story of its own.”

In terms of midsize accent pieces, Meyer says that she has a soft spot for finding antique stools “because you can pop them into any space to add depth and dimension.” Tuck them beside an armchair in the living room, add them to an empty kitchen corner and place a plant on top, or stick one in a bathroom by the tub for somewhere to keep a drink while relaxing. And if you find an upholstered one that could use new fabric, switching it out for a pattern you love is probably worth it.

“These bring a natural warmth that can be difficult to replicate with something new, and their small size makes them fairly reasonable in price for a vintage furnishing piece,” Meyer says.

A larger piece of furniture is likely always going to be more expensive than a smaller one, but designer Yena Jung of By Yena Designs thinks vintage hutches are the versatile antiques people should pay more attention to. They come in many different shapes, styles, and sizes, and can easily make a home in any room of the house.

“A beautiful vintage hutch can add warmth, charm, and a sense of history to a space,” Jung says. “I especially love how their imperfections and natural patina make them feel collected over time.”

#6750. The Best Conditioners for Fine Hair for Lightweight Hydration and Repair

If you have fine hair, you probably have a conditioner routine that looks a lot like mine: a tiny amount, only on the very ends, rinsed out almost immediately, just in case. For years, I was so afraid of flat roots that I barely conditioned at all, and my ends showed it. They tangled, frizzed and snapped every time I brushed them, and my bangs never quite sat right. On the days I did use a proper amount, I’d end up washing my hair again the next morning because it already looked greasy.

Eventually, I figured out the amount wasn’t the issue. I’d just been using the wrong conditioners. Fine hair has narrow strands, so the rich formulas made for thick, dry hair don’t really sink in. They sit on top and drag everything down. What works better is something light that rinses out clean and still leaves your hair soft enough to brush through. A few also have powerful ingredients like rice protein, biotin, or panthenol, which make each strand feel a bit thicker and look fuller. How you use it matters, too. Keeping most of it on your mid-lengths and ends, and rinsing well at the scalp, makes a bigger difference than you’d expect.

Once you’ve got both the formula and the technique right, second-day hair gets a lot easier to deal with. Below, the best conditioners for fine hair, according to celebrity hairstylists and Vogue editors.

Should I use conditioner for thin, fine hair?

Yes you should use conditioner for fine hair. Skipping conditioner might keep your roots from falling flat, but your ends will end up tangled, frizzy and more likely to break. Just pick a light formula, and don’t expect it to change how much hair you have, even if the bottle says “thickening.” “You’re not actually changing the natural thickness of the hair or stimulating new growth,” says Cook. “You’re creating the look and feel of denser, fuller hair. I love this approach, especially for fine hair that needs body without sacrificing movement or softness.” Kreager is less convinced by thickening formulas, which she says mostly “over cleanse or coat the hair strand,” so she sticks with a lightweight conditioner that keeps hair soft without weighing it down.

How should I apply conditioner if I have fine hair?

Start with less than you think you need and keep most of it on your mid-lengths and ends, unless you’re using a formula made for the scalp, like Medicube’s. Macias also uses this step to detangle. “I start from the ends and gradually work upward, rather than pulling a brush directly through from the roots,” he says. Rinsing matters just as much as applying, especially near the scalp. “One of the biggest mistakes people make with conditioning products is assuming that leaving a little extra behind will provide more hydration,” he says. “In reality, residual from products can sometimes make the roots appear flatter and affect how the hair responds to styling.”

Last month, Eldar Isgandarov attended Julio Torres’s Halloween party dressed as Candace, co-owner of the radical feminist bookstore Women & Women First on the sketch comedy series Portlandia. This was an important place to begin our conversation: Isgandarov wanted to explain why his halo of brown curls was a bit mussed. The night prior, he’d donned a wig cap and a long blond wig. He tried to stick close to his friend—who went as Candace’s partner, Toni—throughout the night. “If you separate, it gets tricky,” he says. Otherwise, “they’d think she’s Velma and I’m Lucius Malfoy.”

Isgandarov, an Azerbaijani actor who lives in Bushwick with roommates and a cat named Cheese, has charmed audiences this fall in his role as Nicky, a self-described “aspiring queer philosopher,” in Jordan Firstman’s directorial debut, Club Kid. Calling Nicky the roommate of Firstman’s Peter would somewhat overstate their arrangement: He’s a video game-addicted layabout crashing in Peter’s rent-controlled loft. When Peter finally kicks him out to make room for Arlo (Reggie Absolom), the 10-year-old son he didn’t realize he had, Nicky moves in with their eccentric downstairs neighbor, Evelyn (Colleen Camp). The two soon become an irresistibly silly comic duo.

Isgandarov admits to being nervous about doing the film at first; he wasn’t sure how its overt gayness would be received back home. But an early meeting with Firstman assured him that he was in capable hands. “My first little rendezvous with Jordan before we started filming, I was like, ‘Oh, this guy is a maestro.’”

He credits the easy, funny chemistry between Nicky and Evelyn to both the atmosphere on set—Firstman encouraged his cast to try things out—and to Camp, whom Isgandarov calls a “generous actor.” “People keep saying we should do a spinoff,” he says, “and I would love to do it, but I would get tired of laughing. We would constantly be breaking. That would be the hardest thing to do.” Their storyline also suited him as someone who is “good with moms” and grew up obsessed with Sunset Boulevard’s Norma Desmond. “I was like, oh my God, I’m in love with this woman. Like, I wanna marry her, and I wanna be with her forever, no matter how fucked-up she is,” he remembers with a laugh.

In December, Isgandarov will appear in Jesse Eisenberg’s The Debut, the story of a woman whose life is transformed when she’s cast in her community theater’s latest production. “They are your mentors, but they’re also not,” he says of working with stars Julianne Moore and Paul Giamatti. “Like, they’re your best friends on set.”

His first day shooting was also his first day ever on a professional set; until then, he’d only done student films during his time at Savannah College of Art and Design. His first scene set him opposite Giamatti, with many other actors looking on. After a pep talk from fellow castmate Havana Rose Liu, Isgandarov was off. “It was intoxicatingly fun,” he says. “It was like Disneyland, you know? As a kid you go and you’re like, ‘Oh my God! Magic is real.’”

As a former theater kid, he also thrilled at the chance to work on a film about musical theater. He identifies as a Sondheim fanatic, referring to Sunday in the Park with George and Passion as two of his favorite shows. (As it happens, none but Sondheim muse Bernadette Peters, who originated the role of Dot in Sunday, makes a cameo in The Debut, though she and Isgandarov had no scenes together.) He recalls sitting out a visit to Basement—the notorious, semi-exclusive nightclub in Queens—with his friends in order to watch a proshot at home. “And the good thing about the fact that I didn’t go is that they got rejected at the door,” he deadpans.

Indeed, despite his breakout role in Club Kid, Isgandarov likes to keep a low profile. “I like hanging out. I like a good, real conversation. Not that you can’t have that when you’re partying, but a club can be very overwhelming at times.” Sometimes even a club kid needs a night in.
