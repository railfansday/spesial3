#7029. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The operational question is how organizations will keep delegated authority current. A continuing assignment needs a human owner who can revise its scope and decide when to stop it. Otherwise, the convenience of leaving work with an agent can outlast the reason it was authorized.

#7025. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#7021. Claude Intros Sonnet 5.5 with Model-Switching Cybersecurity Safeguards

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

#7016. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#7011. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

#6893. It’s Not Too Late to Plant These 5 Perennials for a Gorgeous Fall Garden

Add cheer to gray days with these late-season planting picks.

Consistent wind and rain might not exactly be “gardening weather,” but you can still add color to your garden this autumn. If you have a bare patch to fill, some established perennials can be planted now—as long as the ground isn't waterlogged or frozen.

As autumn deepens and the last of the summer-flowering plants begin to fade, our gardens can start to look a little tired. Add the persistent drizzle and gray skies, and it's easy to feel that the color has gone for the year.

The good news is that there's still time to brighten things up. Established autumn-flowering perennials are a quick and reliable way to bring color and seasonal interest to your garden, and a mild, wet spell can help newly planted roots settle in.

The key is to check the conditions first: avoid planting directly into waterlogged soil, which can suffocate plants, or when the ground is frozen. If the soil is too wet to work, choose pots with good drainage and keep them somewhere sheltered from strong winds.

As Charlotte McGrattan, hardy nursery stock buyer at Hillier Garden Centers, adds, newly planted perennials “require watering well while they establish.” Rain can help with that, but check the soil rather than assuming your plants have had enough: containers, in particular, may still need watering.

Here, McGrattan shares five autumn-flowering perennials you can plant now.

Heleniums are tough, hardy, and easy to grow. If your soil is very wet or compacted, it's best to plant them in a pot positioned in a sheltered spot away from the wind.

“Heleniums add an abundance of color, bursting from mid-summer through into autumn. There are hundreds of varieties to choose from, from hot yellows and reds to vibrant oranges,” McGrattan says.

In these mild temperatures, it's also not too late to plant heliopsis, also known as “false sunflower.” “They not only make impressive, colorful features in borders and beds, attracting an array of pollinators but can also make for lovely cut flowers,” says McGrattan.

“They grow in clumps with branching stems that give a bushy habit, and the flowering period is between six and eight weeks. To encourage buds to form, deadhead any spent flowers.”

While spring is generally the best time to plant penstemons—which are available in shades of purple, pink, white, and blue—you can still plant them in pots now.

“With some varieties being bi-colors combining two shades, they bring welcome color to the garden,” says McGrattan. “Penstemon thrives in well-drained soil and is very tolerant to drought conditions.”

Crocosmias are a great way to bring both height and vibrant color to your autumn borders, as long as you plant established plants with roots instead of growing them from corms (bulbs). “Originating from South Africa, they require fertile, moist, but well-drained soil,” McGrattan adds.

You can also still plant potted Japanese anemones in late autumn, provided the ground is neither frozen nor waterlogged. “Japanese anemones boast cup-shaped pink or white flowers on the top of tall stems and can spread rapidly, so can be a great means of filling borders,” says McGrattan.

Rainbow Rowell is the bestselling writer behind the novel Corinne. Here, she explains why it was finally time to come forward.

In 2022, the novel Corinne was published. At the story’s center is Corinne, a woman who walked away from family and her fundamentalist upbringing but finds herself unable to forget Enoch, her first love. Rebecca Morrow, the name on the book’s cover, was described as a “pseudonym for a New York Times bestselling author,” and though readers guessed at who that author might be, Rebecca Morrow’s identity remained a mystery until now.

Rebecca Morrow is Rainbow Rowell, the celebrated author of Cherry Baby and Eleanor & Park, among others. Corinne isn’t about her life, but it has parallels, and in 2022, publishing something so personal felt impossible. Now denying what she has come to consider her favorite book seems unthinkable. In July 2027, Corinne will be reissued with Rowell’s name on the cover, and in 2028 she will publish Catch Us the Foxes, a Corinne sequel.

Here Rowell talks about the decision to write Corinne—and deciding to finally put her name on it—in her own words.

In the darkest hours of the COVID-19 pandemic—when the future seemed uncertain, and the rest of the world felt far away—I did something very strange for me.

I wrote a book, knowing that I couldn’t let anyone read it.

“I just need to do this,” I told my literary agent. “And then I’ll write something we can sell.” It was a good time to sell. I’d just published three No. 1 bestsellers in a row. “I just need to get this out of me first.”

I wrote the first draft of the novel almost feverishly. It was about a woman who is cast out of her strict Christian church as a teenager and cut off by her family. She comes back home as an adult to reckon with the past—and with the man who was the catalyst for her exile.

Really, the book was about all the things I’d always been too scared to write about, or even to talk about….Faith. Shame. Sex. (More sex. Unclean sex. Forbidden sex. Sex in the same room as a Bible.) And, most thornily, how it feels to leave a fundamentalist church. The way you eventually realize that you can’t leave. That you’ll always take it with you, because you were made there. Baked there. Poured and set.

When I was done with the manuscript, it was my favorite thing I’d ever written. Maybe not my best book, but already the one I loved the most.

“But honestly,” I told my agent, “we can’t do anything with this. It’s too personal. And it’s the very last thing any of my readers would want.” I was writing queer YA fantasy. Not whatever this was.

My agent, being an agent, asked if he could read it anyway.

And he liked it. (He liked my beautiful baby!)

We very quickly began talking about a way to publish the book that might sidestep all my fears and anxieties….

There began my strange—and still hardly believable to me—journey as Rebecca Morrow, the pseudonymous author of Corinne.

It was exciting at first. It was different and fun. A pseudonym! A secret identity! A way to drop a novel into the world without having to talk about it, or sell it. Without having to worry about alienating or recruiting my existing readers.

The pseudonym satisfied that still feverish part of me that needed to let the story out, but was too terrified to stand by it.

Oh, I knew it was absurd to write a book about shame and secrets, and then to keep it a secret. (Don’t think this didn’t come up in therapy!) But at the time, it seemed like the only path forward for Corinne. It was this or let the characters die on my hard drive.

My editor and I agonized over how to handle the pseudonym. Traditionally, authors write fake biographies to go with their fake names. But I didn’t want to get caught in a lie if I was found out. And I didn’t want to seek out undeserved goodwill as a “debut author.”

The marketing department really wanted something they could say about Rebecca Morrow. They suggested “lives in Nebraska,” but even that felt like divulging too much. We settled on “Rebecca Morrow is a pseudonym for a New York Times bestselling author.”

My apologies to the bestselling, usually Mormon, authors who got blamed for this weird book. I didn’t see that coming.

I did expect readers to guess that I was the author. Even though I’d freed myself to write Corinne in a new voice and shaken off some of my more recognizable habits, the book ended up sounding more like me than ever. It was like my voice distilled.

Some people did guess. It didn’t bother me. I still didn’t have to talk about the book. I didn’t have to face it.

Corinne is my favorite of my books and beyond that, Enoch and Corinne, the man and woman at its center, are my all-time favorite characters. It’s hard to explain why…

I always write messy, flawed characters, but these two are my messiest and most flawed. Their love is not the stuff of romance. (This novel is not a romance; it will disappoint you if you come to it looking for reassurance or escape.)

Neither Corinne nor Enoch is based on me, though they carry many of my burdens. This is not a hidden memoir. But they feel so real to me. They make mistakes, and I want to forgive them. I want them to find grace. I think about them still, all the time. I worry about what’s become of them.

Ultimately, that’s why I’m stepping forward to claim the book now. At some point, “not having to talk about Corinne” turned into “not being able to talk about Corinne,” even when I desperately wanted to.

I love this book! I want to put it on my shelf, between Any Way the Wind Blows and Scattered Showers. I want readers who liked Slow Dance and Cherry Baby to discover it.

I loved the characters in Corinne so much that I wrote a sequel about them—Catch Us the Foxes—and I want that book to have a life, too. William Morrow is launching both books, properly—Corinne next July, and Catch Us the Foxes in March 2028—in beautiful hardcover editions.

I revised Corinne for the new edition—I went a little deeper on the church stuff this time, and sharpened the focus on some of the characters. It’s a little longer. And, I think, a little better. My favorite designer, Jim Tierney, designed the new matching covers.

I still get a surge of fear and excitement every time I see my name there front and center.

My therapist says that I’m very good at compartmentalizing—keeping some parts of myself shut away from other parts. I would argue that this is a very useful skill! But it does bad things to your soul, over time...being fractured like that.

But as the years have passed, it’s felt like I’ve left something half done. Like Corinne is a truth half shared. A story half told. I needed to get the book out of me, but I ended up cutting myself off from it. That was never the goal.

I’m grateful to “Rebecca Morrow” for making Corinne possible, but I’m finally ready to bring these characters home.

Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? Whether you’re new to antiquing or simply trying to add more character to a space, lighting is an excellent place to start. “Lighting is the jewelry of the home, so it’s one of my favorite places to bring in something vintage,” says A-Lister Corey Damen Jenkins. “An Art Deco sconce or an antique brass lantern has a warmth to it that’s hard to find in something brand new.”

Adding to an existing space? Jenkins advises starting small: “Swap one overhead fixture or put a pair of vintage lamps on a console, and you’ll be surprised how much the whole room shifts,” he says. “It’s an easy way to add history without starting over.”

“In light of America’s 250th birthday, Americana in general is in vogue,” says Mahon. Among her clients, the trend has manifested in quilts and textiles, portraits of sea captains, trade signs, and ironstone—culminating in an aesthetic she describes as “elevated country—not corny.”

If you’ve noticed that no particular design period seems to reign supreme among designers’ top antique trend picks, it’s not an accident. Heading into 2027, there’s a strong argument to be made that mixed-period design is more influential than any singular reference.

“I love a room that doesn’t belong to any one decade,” Jenkins says. “A nineteenth-century chest with a 1970s lamp on top, or a Lucite chair pulled up to an antique desk. Those are the pairings that make people stop and look twice.” Drake adds that seemingly out-of-place seating is an easy way to add intrigue. “I love including antique chairs as sculptural notes, contrasted against modern upholstery and tables,” he says.

But when it comes to antiquing, there’s really only so much one can plan for. Letting a great find lead the way often turns out to be a better strategy than arriving to an estate sale with a rigid checklist. The best way to keep up with top designers when it comes to antiquing this year? Find a piece that has something to say, place it where it seems to belong, and then try it where it doesn’t.

#6849. Can the Brooklyn Nets Convert a Generation of Newly Minted Knicks Fans?

At the Brooklyn Nets’ home opener against the Charlotte Hornets last week, one longtime fan pointed out the strange dynamic inherent to New York City basketball fandom. He was jealous of places like Boston and Philadelphia, he said, because the entire city organizes itself around one team. (Think the Showtime Lakers or the Grit-and-Grind Memphis Grizzlies.) But in New York, home to both the Nets and the Knicks, fan bases are pitted against each other by fans, media, and sometimes even franchise leaders.

For a long time, the Nets could take solace in their cross rivals' decades-long stretch of ineptitude. That vanished when Jalen Brunson and company lifted the Larry O’Brien in June, leaving Brooklyn with some soul searching to do. “It doesn't make us feel very good,” Nets owner Joe Tsai told NetsDaily over the summer. “It irates our fan base and it is embarrassing.” As The Ringer’s Zach Lowe pointed out on his recently launched Substack, the Knicks’ title cost the Nets “an entire generation of fans.”

I, for one, have never understood why commentators are so obsessed with the size of Brooklyn’s fanbase. I am, obviously, a hugely biased homer, but you don’t hear the same concerns when it comes to other little-brother teams like the Clippers or teams, like Wizards, whose games are sparsely attended. Even as they’ve tanked these past couple of years, the Nets have maintained a 99% attendance rate and are the eighth most-valuable franchise in the entire league. Still, I have to hear talking heads argue that the team should move back to New Jersey? I might be salty, but that’s stupid.

Nevertheless, there is clearly angst about building up a coalition of Nets faithful. On his podcast Curious Mike, where I wish he would be less curious, the team’s star forward Michael Porter Jr. recently said, “When we play Knicks, Celtics, Philly, Lakers, it's mostly their fans. So I made my way over to the team’s training facility in Sunset Park to see how the Nets are tackling this problem with pillars of the present and future.

I thought back to the simple philosophy both Randle and Brown echoed: just win, and people will show up. It’s unclear whether the Nets will ever convert a generation of fans spellbound by the Knicks, or even win the Finals. But I know I’ll be there.

#6845. Better American Sunscreens Are Here—But We’re Still Way Behind the Best

Anyone who’s applied sunscreen only to be left with a chalky white cast, a greasy finish knows American sun care has long been a frustrating experience. The US is decades behind European and Asian counterparts, which have long been blessed with sunscreens that are lighter, more luxurious, and easier to wear every day. For skincare obsessives (this writer included) trips abroad are also opportunities to stock up on superior sunscreens they can’t find at home.

The root of the problem is regulation. The Food and Drug Administration classifies sunscreen as a drug rather than a cosmetic. Because of that distinction, more time and testing is required to get new sunscreens on the shelves, dramatically reducing innovation in American sun protection. But for the first time since the ‘90s, we’ve had some movement on that front. In June of this year, the FDA approved a new sunscreen filter called bemotrizinol—also known as Tinosorb S or BEMT—after years of lobbying in the states by dermatologists, skincare brands, and Congress members. It’s a meaningful step toward better sun care, but the US is still lagging behind.

Bemotrizinol is prized for its ability to provide both UVA and UVB protection while remaining stable in sunlight. That’s a significant advantage over some existing US-approved filters, which offer protection against one type of UV radiation more effectively than the other. BEMT also has the potential to make sunscreen more pleasant to wear: “And because it’s a large, oil-loving molecule, it mostly stays on the surface of your skin instead of soaking in, and it blends into lightweight, non-greasy formulas with no white cast,” says Dr. Joel Spitz, co-founder, chief medical officer, and board-certified dermatologist at Honeydew, a virtual dermatology platform.

Bemotrizinol might be new for the US market, but it’s already been used in sunscreen formulations abroad for 20 years, Spitz says. That long track record helps explain why consumers in the US have spent years coveting the more cosmetically elegant formulas available in countries like South Korea and France. While most consumers would love if major brands like Neutrogena, La Roche-Posay, or Beauty of Joseon brought their European or Asian formulations to the US, those SPFs still contain filters that the FDA hasn’t approved. As a result, many brands are developing entirely new BEMT-centric formulas for use stateside.

Australian label Ultra Violette was the first brand to release a BEMT sunscreen in the US with its Unblock Screen SPF 50 in August 2026. Neutrogena debuted its Ultra Sheer Daily Face Sunscreen SPF 70 in September. Earlier this month French drugstore staple La Roche-Posay put its Anthelios UVDefense Sunscreen SPF 50 up for sale online. Don’t mistake the relatively quick arrival of these products after the FDA’s approval for ease in which brands can develop new formulations. According to Emanuella Louis, La Roche-Posay’s public relations manager, the brand had been working on its new formula for more than two years.

That long lead time reflects the way sunscreen development works: Brands can research and test formulas in anticipation of regulatory changes, then move a product forward when a particular filter gets the green light. Louis says La Roche-Posay has access to filters the FDA may be considering for approval and is “actively working, playing, testing on what possibly those formulas would be.” When a filter is approved, she explains, a formula already in the testing pipeline can be moved up.

Some brands are still tinkering with BEMT formulas for the US. Round Lab, one of South Korea’s most popular sunscreen manufacturers, has sold a version of its SPF in the US since 2024, and is hard at work on a new version of those formulas that contains bemotrizinol.

“Bemotrizinol’s approval in the US is a meaningful change, but it does not mean a Korean formula can simply be brought to the US market as is,” says Koochul Kwon, Round Lab’s chief technology officer. Beyond incorporating the newly approved filter, brands must ensure the ingredients keep the UV filters stably dissolved and working alongside other components, including moisturizing ingredients. The finished product must then meet US requirements for SPF and broad-spectrum testing, product stability, and labeling. With those considerations in mind, Kwon expects Round Lab to release its own bemotrizinol sunscreen sometime in 2027.

A sunscreen is only as useful as a person’s willingness to wear it consistently. Bemotrizinol’s ability to protect against UV damage is important, but its potential to make formulas lighter, less greasy, and more comfortable could be just as consequential to get people to implement it in their everyday skincare routines.

“One of the strengths of Korean sun care is how naturally it has built sun protection into the everyday skincare experience,” Kwon says. “For people to apply enough sunscreen and reapply it when needed, the way it feels matters as much as how well it protects.”

But better filters alone won’t transform American sun care. The real breakthrough will be sunscreens that people actually enjoy wearing—and, as a result, are more likely to apply generously and reapply throughout the day, a key step to ensuring its effectiveness.

Charlotte Palermino, cofounder and chief brand officer of skincare brand Dieux, has been an advocate for easier sunscreen filter approvals ever since she tried her first Japanese sunscreen, Biore UV, a decade ago. She realized sunscreen could stave off skin issues after her dermatologist told her that her hyperpigmentation had been the result of failing to use sun protection. In the years since she has publicly pushed for regulatory reform. She’s hesitant to put an estimated release date on Dieux’s upcoming BEMT-centric sunscreen, simply because the brand is dialed in to ensure its able to deliver a quality, wearable product.

“We’ve gone through this with [Dieux’s] SkinMercy, which is an OTC colloidal oatmeal cream, but for sunscreen, the stability of your sunscreen is so fucking important,” she says. “If my sunscreen separates or if it’s not at that stated SPF 50, if that degrades at all with broad spectrum protection, I would be mortified.”

Palerimo cites brands like Korea’s Beauty of Joseon, which is more popular in the US than in its home country, as evidence that Americans have long been on the hunt for better sunscreen. And though BEMT is a step forward, we still have a way to go. Board-certified dermatologist Jane Yoo has been lobbying Congress to get new sunscreen filters approved for the US and is tracking the FDA’s progress in making those approvals. Currently there are seven other filters—bisoctrizole, drometrizole trisiloxane, octyl triazone, amiloxate, diethylhexyl butamido triazone iscotrizinol, ecamsule, and enzacamene—under the agency’s Time and Extent Application (TEA) pipeline. Those filters are part of the 2014 Sunscreen Innovation Act meant to speed up the process of determining if ingredients are Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective, or GRASE. But here we are 12 years later, and we’re still waiting for their approval.

“A good analogy is we’ve been making sunscreens with both of our hands tied behind our backs,” Palermino says. “We’ve now had one hand released. I would like both hands released.”

#6841. Lupita Nyong’o Brought 3 Dresses—and a Bag of Swedish Candy—to the 2026 CFDA Awards

Never has anyone at the CFDA Awards been this comfortable and stylish. Last night, Lupita Nyong’o celebrated the best of American fashion at the Museum of Natural History with three custom looks for the occasion.

After an afternoon spent getting ready at the Fifth Avenue Hotel, listening to Stevie Wonder and Meshell Ndegeocello while snacking on leftover Swedish candy from Halloween, it was time for Nyong’o to slip on her first of three GapStudio looks by Zac Posen, who was honored last night with the Innovation Award.

For his longtime friend’s fashion triple header, Posen was inspired by the classic Gap T-shirt and—more broadly—American sportswear. “We built the looks on T-shirt jersey, the fabric at the heart of Gap. Easy, understated, comfortable chic,” Posen says. “It’s a classic casual fabric, elevated by the woman who wears it.”

Lupita Nyong’o and Zac Posen at the 2026 CFDA Awards.

Nyong’o kicked off the night in a red two-piece with a gathered crop top and maxi skirt, which she and stylist Micaela Erlanger paired with a denim jacket and jewelry from Elsa Peretti’s Tiffany & Co. collection. Afterwards, she swapped her red-hot two-piece for a plunging white gown to present Rachel Scott of Diotima with womenswear designer of the year, and ended the night in a navy jersey dress with a denim bomber with a jean clutch to match.

The looks were right in line with Nyong’o’s personal tastes. “My off-duty style is relaxed, effortless, and comfortable, but always with a sense of personality. I love pieces that feel easy to wear but still allow me to express myself,” Nyong’o tells Vogue. “Tonight’s looks are a reflection of that—classic, versatile pieces with a fresh approach, inspired by a tried-and-true basic: a T-shirt, but with a modern point of view.”

Erlanger was on hand to help make each look red carpet-ready, adding a very Gap twist with a denim bomber jacket and clutch, plus jewelry from Bucherer, Tiffany & Co., and CFDA accessory designer of the year winner Ana Khouri. “When Lupita and I started discussing the vision for [the night], we kept returning to the beauty of effortless dressing. The process was all about playful reinvention,” Erlanger says. “I wanted Lupita to feel comfortable and true to herself, while elevating an everyday staple into something elevated, modern, and distinctly appropriate for the CFDA.”

#6840. 30 Gifts for Dads Who Swear They Don't Want Anything 2026

Your old man is funny about gifts, ain't he? That guy will wear the same jacket for 20 years, spend a small fortune on something you've never heard of, and insist he doesn't need a thing. More often than not, finding the best gifts for dads involves putting your thinking cap on to mentally trawl through all of the very practical things they already own, along with the stuff that they’d just never use. Fortunately, we've got a few good dads on the GQ staff. Opinionated dads. Handsome dads. Great dads. And they've actually got some ideas.

Still no dice? We'd suggest perusing the rest of our gift guides and signing up for the GQ Recommends newsletter for more gift ideas worth your money.

Simple, generously proportioned, and blessed with a great collar, this checked shirt from Japanese label Frizmworks gets all the little things right. And the price? Legendary.

It's a good idea to pay closer attention to what's been happening at Filson lately. The brand's been digging into its archives and finding fresh ways to offer up its outdoorsmen roots, as evidenced by this Tacoma jacket, an old-school silhouette recast in a handsome denim.

There's a reason a Burberry check scarf costs what it does. The pattern is destined to outlast every passing trend and come back into fashion again and again. And the cashmere? Every bit as luxurious as he'd hope.

A Shaggy Dog for the shaggy ol' dog, y'know. J.Press's sweater is the real deal, made from Shetland wool that's hand-brushed to achieve its signature fuzzy texture. And yes, it's still made in Scotland, where the wool comes from. Tradition is tradition.

There's always a good reason for another pair of work boots, so why not make 'em Western? These meet the safety standards for commercial construction but look handsome as hell doing it.

I love this genre of slipper, the travel kind that zips into its own little case. And these from Luca come at an incredible price, with a perfectly shaped toe box and rubber soles that make them handsome enough to wear out.

Of all the tech you could get him this year, a portable cassette player is probably the last thing he'd expect. This one comes from the nostalgia peddlers at Retrospekt. And if he doesn't appreciate the format, he'll at least appreciate the transparent casing that puts the inner circuitry on display.

Five generations in, JBL still hasn't found a reason to make the Go any bigger. And why would it? The pint-sized speaker continues to deliver outsized sound, with the latest iteration adding 10 hours of battery life and USB-C wired audio support. Still the one to beat in its size class.

Nocs Provisions is the first name in cool binoculars, and now it's got a lo-fi digital camera to match. It's a fun little shooter in its own right, but I love that you can take photos straight through a pair of binoculars or a monocular.

String 'em up or stake 'em in the ground—BioLite's solar-powered site lights make illuminating a campsite (or a backyard, balcony, or any outdoor area) a cinch. If he's a camper, he'll be a happy one.

Twelve South is known for its AirFly Bluetooth dongle for flights, and now it's coming for your car. The new AirFly Drive is a clever little device that does away with the cords, letting him connect to Apple CarPlay or Android Auto wirelessly.

I've reached the point where I trust Anker with just about anything that plugs into a wall. This nifty universal charger uses GaN technology to power up to four devices at once, with interchangeable plugs for the US, UK, and EU. Australia, regrettably, remains off the itinerary.

I'm of the mind that you can't have too many fragrances, and of the three versions of Bleu de Chanel, the original EDT is the one I recommend most. It's the brightest and most citrus-forward of the lot, before settling into something warmer and woodier.

Byredo's new body mists offer a more casual way to wear scent, with a lighter touch and a shorter lifespan than your typical cologne. Mojave Ghost leans the most masculine to my nose, though there's nothing gendered about smelling good.

What a great form factor for a shaver. Panasonic's ARC5 Palm packs five Japanese stainless-steel blades and the power of a full-size electric razor into a handsome little device that fit in the palm of his hand.

A hangnail is a small indignity, but an indignity nonetheless, especially when you're a thousand miles from home. German grooming specialists Mühle have been at it since 1945, and this handsome travel manicure set comes equipped with a nail clipper, scissors, tweezers, and file.

Vermont-based Ursa Major comes up a lot in our grooming coverage, and that's no accident. This set brings together a few of our favorites, including its vitamin C serum, night cream, and face wash. A very solid skincare routine, all in one box.

This beef tallow balm from Sky High Farm, the upstate New York agricultural project with a fashion-world following, is as good as anything for soothing and restoring dry skin.

ICYMI: Menswear's patron saint Todd Snyder linked up with West Elm for a stirring collection of homewares this past year, and the hands-down standout is this walnut valet chair. A handsome place to hang tomorrow's outfit—or yesterday's, if we're being realistic.

No man in his right mind would turn down a pair of L.L.Bean's Wicked Goods, which is practically its own category of slipper. Sometimes the obvious choice is the right one.

I've been meaning to take up sewing for years, but my excuse has always been a lack of space. Then I spotted this mini sewing machine at the MoMA Design Store, and suddenly that excuse isn't so convincing.

It's always better when you make it yourself, and that's certainly true of shibori. This beginner-friendly kit comes with bandanas, tenugui cloths, indigo dye, rubber gloves, and easy-to-follow instructions. Might as well throw a few white tees into the mix while he's at it.

Now that's one helluva coffee mug. Made from stainless steel with double-wall construction to keep it cool to the touch, this industrial number from Copenhagen-based design studio Service Projects comes with a generously proportioned saucer that does double duty as a serving tray.

Before there was John Wilson, there was Phaidon's photographic ode to the world's most ubiquitous building material. Give him a nudge and a wink and tell him it's a “solid” gift. He'll appreciate that.

He should absolutely wear these sunnies from Satisfy and Oakley while running. But does he have to? Not in the slightest.

You might think running requires little more than a pair of sneakers, but you'd be surprised. This vest from Lululemon has pockets aplenty for all the little things he needs to bring along, without weighing him down.

It wouldn't be the holidays without a little tension. Therabody's new massage gun is made for the face, combining gentle percussion with heat to help relieve tightness around the jaw, temples, and cheeks.

A few minutes of cold therapy can work wonders on a puffy, sleep-deprived face. Quiet Hours' facial ice roller makes the ritual dead simple: chill it, roll it across the skin, and let the cold do its thing.

Electrolytes have gotten awfully sophisticated lately. Freaks of Nature's zero-sugar powder goes beyond hydration, with a formula designed to support gut health and the skin barrier, too.

Google's new Fitbit Edge splits the difference between its screenless fitness bands and full-fledged smartwatches, adding a bright OLED display to a slim, handsome tracker. There's built-in GPS, heart-rate and sleep tracking, and up to seven days of battery life.
