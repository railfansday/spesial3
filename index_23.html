#6875. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The operational question is how organizations will keep delegated authority current. A continuing assignment needs a human owner who can revise its scope and decide when to stop it. Otherwise, the convenience of leaving work with an agent can outlast the reason it was authorized.

#6870. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6865. The Inconvenient Truth About the Electricity AI Requires

AI companies are facing political backlash, but they’re staring down an even bigger problem as they look to take over the world.

He-Man liked to exclaim: “I have the power!” AI should be so lucky.

When it comes to the wielders of artificial intelligence, and the data centers needed to fuel it, language and numbers become inescapably extreme: trillions in investment, gigawatts of electricity, the extinction of the human race. It’s almost cartoonish, until you get down to some brass tacks: The power is not there to fuel these dreams of the would-be Masters of the Universe.

“The U.S. grid is not designed for large data-center loads,” says Anubhav Arora, senior director of global infrastructure at Fitch and a coauthor of a recent paper the ratings agency published on data centers. “Securing reliable, sufficient power is the single greatest bottleneck in data-center development,” the paper’s authors write. (Esquire and Fitch share the same parent company, Hearst.)

In the U.S.—which is by far the world leader in data centers—electricity demand from the facilities could reach up to 649 terawatt-hours (TWh) a year by 2030, according to a 2026 Berkeley Lab analysis, or roughly 12 percent of all U.S. electricity consumption. Powering New York City for an entire year requires one tenth that energy.

Meeting global demand for compute could require nearly $7 trillion in capital investment by 2030, including $5.2 trillion for AI-related data centers, according to McKinsey estimates. Estimates from Goldman Sachs Research say that Europe will need to invest more than $3.5 trillion on its grid in the next ten years to cope with increased demand. (China looks to be fine, but it is slow to the data-center boom.)

And the problem is not just a lack of juice. “The actual grid infrastructure . . . is also basically limited in terms of transmission capacity, and the interconnection process is a little bit outdated,” says Arora. That grid—the network of transmission lines, transformers, and power plants that create and distribute electricity to hundreds of millions of users—is constrained, and the interconnection backlog is massive. Put another way: Not only is the shop running low on milk, but the line to get inside is stretching around the block. “You could build a data center in one and a half to two years, but to get interconnection, it takes five to ten years,” Arora says.

According to Cleanview, a company that tracks U.S. power infrastructure, there are currently 1,454 operating data centers, as well as up to 578 under construction and 2,663 proposed. Companies like Alphabet, Meta, and Anthropic are scrambling. With that scramble, there is money to be made. PJM, the largest power-grid operator in the United States, has been trying to devise a new way to accommodate the data-center boom. Its proposed “Bring Your Own New Capacity” (or BYONC) framework would have allowed new data-center projects to buy energy in the form of a battery and add it to the grid. But in October, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected that portion of PJM’s proposal, sending the grid operator back to the drawing board.

Important here is what kind of data centers we’re talking about. Of the more than 3,200 data centers proposed globally, 800 of them are hyperscalers. These are the biggies. The campuses. Sprawling centers with square footage in the millions and power demands upwards of 100 MW (which could power 100,000 homes). Collectively, some estimates put the global power usage at 200 TWh annually. (That’s juice for 11 million homes.)

How can companies even think about the grid beginning to handle that demand? Increasingly, they aren’t. Many hyperscalers are looking to “bring your own power [BYOP],” says Jonathon Smith, associate director of Sustainable Finance Research at Fitch. In other words, build their own power plants. The problem here is twofold: First, very few people on the planet know how to build something like this. You could buy the biggest army the world has ever seen; try moving it without engineers. Second, it poses very real and very dangerous environmental threats.

Recently, the journal Nature Sustainability published a study revealing that the annual carbon dioxide emissions of the largest data centers in the U.S. are equal to that of a small country, like Paraguay or Denmark. And that study looked at data centers from 2024 and 2025. Data centers have exploded since then, with as much as a 190 percent increase from 2025 to 2026.

When companies like Anthropic say, “Don’t worry, we’re not even touching the grid,” it brings up a cartoon situation more akin to The Simpsons than a Saturday-morning serial. Picture it: a giant data center, clean, cool, not an emission in sight, just humming machines and maybe a security guard. But there’s a pipe going out the back, and if you follow it as it snakes around the corner, it connects to a behemoth power plant, burning enough natural gas to power a city, with plumes of smoke assaulting the sky above. Animated, but closer to the reality of BYOP.

“From an environmental-impact perspective, most of these developers are deploying natural-gas-based assets for power, which release GHG [greenhouse gas] emissions and air pollutants,” says Smith. The cleaner alternatives are renewables (unlikely), nuclear (terrifying), and batteries (no environmental picnic, either).

Among the environmental concerns—which inevitably bleed into the social and economic ones—are these centers’ use of water, which they need in great volume to keep those humming stacks of servers cool. While many experts point to these issues as overblown, especially as data centers look to closed-loop systems (there is an initial dump of water that gets cooled and reused), the reason you won’t stop hearing about water use is because 1) it’s concerning, and 2) water is inherently political and always has been. (Traditionally, we’ve needed the stuff to feed our animals, grow our crops, and, you know, live.) Go back to the early chapters of the Bible and what’s causing strife? Water. Go back even further and some of the first wars ever recorded—4,500 years ago in ancient Sumeria—are over the stuff. And data centers have become a fast-moving political football.

A recent Gallup poll indicates that 71 percent of Americans do not want an AI data center near where they live. Those are some very big numbers. It’s important to clarify that the data centers big tech wants to build near dense populations are for cloud computing, and they make up roughly 70 percent of all data centers. While centers for AI computing and AI training are catching up, these can be built farther away, for the simple reason that we’re happy to wait a few minutes for AI to do our taxes but not for the cloud to recombobulate an email or Instagram post.

That’s still a huge number of data centers that need to be near you and me, so what will we do about it? The politicians are listening. New York State has issued a moratorium. There’s legislation limiting the centers on the table in nine other states. Gina Hinojosa, who just pummeled Greg Abbott in the Governor's race in Texas, campaigned on promises of a moratorium for permitting and construction on new data centers. Norway, the Netherlands, and Denmark are leading the charge on moratoriums and other restrictions in Europe as governments catch up with popular opinion.

Moreover, back in the U.S.A., Donald Trump has touted a “deal” with big tech on data centers, the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, supposedly protecting grids from the biggies. But never has Tip O’Neill’s maxim that “All politics is local” been more salient. More than 500 localities are forming their own moratoriums, zoning freezes, and permit pauses. Even after the midterms, how and if to move forward with data centers is a debate for states, counties, cities, townships, and any other geographic entity that makes laws, collects taxes, and breathes collective air.

As the Democrats take over Congress and data centers become every pol’s favorite new cudgel, as the European Union and member states enact regulatory frameworks (often tied to renewable energy and climate), as elected officials turn away from AI’s once sunnily vague promises, something is becoming clear: It’s humans who have the power.

#6862. Claude Intros Sonnet 5.5 with Model-Switching Cybersecurity Safeguards

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

#6841. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6808. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

#6733. 9 Interior Trends Designers Are Officially Ditching in 2027

Some of this year’s biggest design moments may not age well.

The end of the year is a great time to look back and reflect. When it comes to home design, there’s a lot to cover, from new trends to furniture collaboration drops to specific internet-breaking interiors. One of our favorite things to do around this time is figure out what’s coming next in terms of interiors.

We love predicting what’s going to be hot in home design for the upcoming seasons; however, you can’t talk about what’s going to be “in” without also touching on what’s going to be “out.” We asked a handful of designers to share what design choices and interior trends they saw a lot of in 2026 that they’re more than happy to leave behind in 2027. Keep reading to find out the nine things they said.

We spend so much time making our interiors warm and reflective of our personalities, it would be a shame to ruin the look of these spaces by opting for one-dimensional lighting.

“It's time to move beyond relying on a single overhead fixture or wall-to-wall recessed lighting to illuminate an entire room,” designer Kristen Mendoza of Four Brothers Design + Build says. “Flat, overly bright lighting can make even the most beautifully designed spaces feel sterile and uninviting.”

Instead, incorporating layers of lighting to add dimension and create a warm atmosphere is much better for your home. Sconces, picture lights, table lamps, and thoughtfully placed accent lighting at various heights throughout the room, Mendoza adds, give a space a more intimate touch.

“I also recommend using warm 2700K light sources on dimmers to flatter people, highlight architectural details, and bring out the natural beauty of materials,” she explains. “Lighting should be considered early in the design and construction process, not treated as an afterthought.”

Part of the appeal of “grandma chic” decor is that it’s been collected over time. The pieces show their age, the interior is imperfect, and it’s very clearly reflective of the homeowner’s personality and life. However, “there is a difference between a room that feels ‘collected over time’ and one that feels inherited all at once,” designer Stephanie Kraus explains, and she’s tired of seeing this false presentation on display in people’s homes.

“Heavy Roman shades with lots of fabric, chintz on top of chintz on top of chintz, fringe on every edge, etc.,” she adds. “One of these details in a room can be charming, but if you use them all together, the room can start to feel like a period movie set.”

It’s often clear when this eccentric aesthetic is manufactured rather than effortless. Instead of trying to make the “ideal” interior in the span of a month, be patient and work on your home over a greater span of time. That way, the end product will look authentic.

Though curved sofas are massively popular right now and look gorgeous in a photograph, Kraus is ready to leave them in 2026. “A curved sofa rarely seats more than two people comfortably,” she says. “Once guests settle in, everyone ends up sitting awkwardly, and conversation feels harder than it should.”

She’s all for a sofa or couch being something that invites people in to linger over conversations and stay late into the night, and a kidney-shaped seat isn’t going to do that.

While designer Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios loved seeing the return of decorative, playful details like ruffles and scalloped edges, she thinks we’ve gone a bit overboard in 2026. These touches are being applied to just about everything: upholstery, pillows, lampshades, bedding, furniture—the list could go on.

“These details can be incredibly charming, but when they're repeated throughout an entire room, they begin to lose the very character that makes them special,” she says.

Rather than treat every piece of a room with one of these details, Vollrath suggests opting for a scalloped lampshade or a beautifully tailored ruffled pillow instead to still capture that charm without drowning the space in them.

“Limiting these details to one or two thoughtful applications allows them to feel intentional rather than overly thematic,” she adds. “Sometimes, the most interesting interiors are as much about what you choose to leave out as what you include.”

“I'd love to see us move away from selecting materials simply because they imitate the appearance of something more expensive,” Vollrath explains. This goes beyond marble-printed contact paper and burl wood veneer; the designer means on a larger scale, like mass-produced tile that’s made to replicate the imperfections of handmade zellige.

“Part of what makes authentic zellige so beautiful is the craftsmanship and natural variation that comes from its production,” she says. “When those qualities are artificially reproduced, I think we sometimes lose the very thing that made the original material special.”

She would rather see a greater appreciation for beautiful, honest materials at every price point, and if the exact thing a homeowner wants is out of budget, then her solution would be to pivot the idea altogether rather than find something cheaply made that looks like what they desire.

“A classic Carrara marble paired with beautifully considered cabinetry can create an incredibly sophisticated interior, even when something as dramatic as Calacatta Viola isn't within the budget,” Vollrath explains.

We could probably say this every year, but it bears repeating: Don’t solely follow trends! “Just because something is trending doesn't mean it's right for your home,” designer Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox says. “Polka dots are having a moment right now, but they don't make sense in many homes, or dare I say, most homes.”

He suggests thinking of home trends as prompts that should get you to brainstorm new ideas for your space that actually fit your style. Take polka dots again. If you want to introduce a new pattern to your home, but you don’t particularly mesh with polka dots, try a stripe instead.

“If you try to force something you don't love into your home just because it's trending, you're going to drive yourself crazy,” he adds. Let’s leave this line of thinking back in 2026 once and for all.

While we love a good built-in bookshelf and wooden ceiling beam, those types of architectural details don’t always make sense in every single home. In 2026, as personality-filled interiors became more popular for everyone, a lot of homeowners looked into adding this type of millwork to their spaces as a way to add more visual interest and texture.

“Decorative beams, arches, and elaborate millwork can feel forced when they don't make sense with the architecture of the home,” designer Dani Flax of Pearson Design Group argues. “I think there's a tendency to add these elements to make a space feel more interesting, but the result can actually take away from the architecture itself.”

A home in a modern farmhouse style, for example, would look a bit odd with an intricate ceiling medallion and excessive picture frame molding. Design your interior around the existing architecture and the style of your home, don’t incorporate these elements simply because they’re everywhere right now.

The next year is not about playing it safe with your interiors—designers are tired of the stark white home. While ubiquitous gray and white color schemes had their time, they can often make a home feel generic and devoid of character.

“In their place, we're embracing more intentional, tonal palettes and color drenching,” Mendoza says.

You can still have a mostly white house, of course, but take a little risk and mix in some pops of color and pattern here and there, or choose a beautiful neutral wood tone for your cabinetry instead of a bright white paint.

“Taking a considered color, whether it's a deep green, warm clay, or earthy mushroom, and carrying it across walls, trim, and cabinetry creates a cohesive, enveloping effect,” Mendoza adds. “The goal isn't necessarily to introduce more color, but to use it with greater confidence and purpose.”

This is definitely a hot take, but designer John Maloney McNamara of Coosa Interiors is ready to leave velvet upholstery behind. “I’ve tried, but velvet almost always reads as either a 1990s prom limo or a hotel lobby trying very hard,” he explains. “The sheen shows every crush and bruise, and it ages into something that looks tired rather than loved.”

If you love the look of velvet, he suggests going with a mohair fabric instead, saying it has all of velvet’s depth and none of its shine. It also only gets better with age. And though he knows creamy bouclé has been overdone, Maloney McNamara thinks it deserves a reprise, but only in a rich color that can replace velvet still.

Local police have already arrested two men “on suspicion of burglary and trespass,” per The Times. The estate itself was unoccupied at the time of the break-in, according to police, and the suspects remain in custody.

Anmer Hall has a history spanning centuries, but the ten-bedroom retreat has been particularly meaningful to the Prince and Princess of Wales during the course of their marriage. It’s a royal residence filled with equal parts gravitas and style. Take a closer look inside below.

Anmer Hall is in the quaint village of Anmer in Norfolk, a coastal county in northeastern England about a three-hour drive from London. The ten-bedroom Georgian pile, built in 1802, is part of Sandringham Estate, a sprawling 20,000-acre property dotted with churches, parishes, and country houses, that has been connected to the royals since 1862. The estate’s grand residence, Sandringham House, is the country bolthole of King Charles III and Queen Camilla, where royal Christmases are famously hosted. Anmer Hall is just a five-minute drive away.

While you can book tours of Sandringham House and even rent properties on the greater estate, Anmer Hall is a private family residence. The village of Anmer includes a church and quaint cottages, many of which were built in the early 20th century for Edward VII, according to an architectural guide to Norfolk by Nikolaus Pevsner and Bill Wilson. The U.K.’s Office for National Statistics states that Anmer parish had a population of about 250 in 2017.

Anmer Hall was the seat of the Coldham family until Prince Albert Edward (aka the future Edward VII) purchased it in 1896 to be a part of the Sandringham Estate, Pevsner and Wilson note. The two-story house’s “charming” brick facade, made from red and brown brick, features 13 bays and dormer windows in the attic. The house underwent renovations in the years following Edward VII’s purchase.

Over the next century, the house was leased to numerous members of the British aristocracy, including the Duke and Duchess of Kent from 1972 to 1990, and Hugh van Cutsem, one of King Charles III’s best friends. Following the expiration of van Cutsem’s lease, the property was rented to timber company owner James Everett.

In 2017, Meghan Markle and Prince Harry stayed with William and Kate at Anmer Hall, “because the sovereign still won’t permit unwed couples to sleep together under her roof Sandringham,” according to the book Notorious Royal Marriages by Leslie Carroll.

Today, the Prince and Princess of Wales spend most of their time at their new primary royal residence Forest Lodge on the grounds of Windsor Castle—but they use Anmer as a country escape. The family also spent the Covid lockdown at Anmer Hall.

As part of the property’s improvements, the Prince and Princess of Wales tapped architect Charles Morris for the renovation and designer Ben Pentreath—the designer who overhauled Kate and William’s apartment at Kensington Palace, per House & Garden—for the interiors.

Pentreath’s firm describes its approach as one “that draws inspiration in equal measure from the great English practitioners of the 18th and 19th centuries, and from the 1960s and ’70s…but always infused with a fresh, modern sensibility”—and the same style can be seen in snippets of Anmer Hall that eagle-eyed viewers have spotted in the Prince and Princess of Wales’s social media feeds. The home—with its high ceilings and decorative moldings—is painted in soothing, neutral colors (though the dining room is reportedly a deep green hue) and accessorized with houseplants, antiques, and gilt-framed art.

Additional amenities on the property include a pool and a tennis court; Anmer Hall also hosts its own honeybee colony. And one last bonus: The home is even said to be haunted by its own ghost.

#6716. 10 “Timeless” Design Choices Designers Say Will Always Be in Style

Step away from the trends and invest in these instead.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pictured above: A refreshing sunroom with built-in bookshelves by Doniphan Moore.

While embracing home design trends can be fun for a while, it can get really old really quickly. Of course, if you really like something, then by all means, decorate your home with it. However, if you’re simply reaching for the things that are popular at a current moment in time before throwing them out after you realize you don’t actually like them, then it might be time to invest in some more timeless decor.

“Ultimately, timeless design is all about creating spaces that are beautiful, functional, and meaningful,” says Abby Pendergrast, an interior stylist and set designer. “That’s why people are drawn to these classics—they make a house feel like a home, and that never goes out of style.”

Ahead, we asked a handful of designers to share the pieces and choices they deem timeless. From gilded mirrors to striped textiles, keep reading to see the 10 things that designers promise you’ll love for years to come.

Antique chests and dressers will never go out of style because they’re well made piece that only get better with time.

“They were meant to last for many years, acting as heirloom pieces for generations to come,” says Debbie Mathews LeRoy of Debbie Mathews Antiques & Designs. They’re multifunctional to use as storage or a spare surface, and they can easily move from room to room and house to house.

“An antique chest brings history and warmth to any space,” designer Emily Jiles of Maggie Griffin Design explains. “Mixing in an antique chest gives the room a collected feel and adds character that new furniture often can't replicate.”

Golden mirrors add a touch of elegance to any room, and they’re shockingly versatile. They can bring more luxury to a traditional style home, or they can add a historic air to a more modern space. Mathews LeRoy, however, specifically adores Louis Philippe giltwood mirrors.

“It can live happily in a traditional interior or fit within a transitional interior thanks to its simple arched shape with minimal decoration,” she says. “They boast understated elegance and can be beautiful in an entrance hall or foyer, dining room, living room, powder room, or bedroom!”

Like any classic choice, bookshelves will grow with you as your sensibility evolves and changes. They can be endlessly re-styled and adjusted to hold whatever pieces you acquire along the way. Plus, they are a kind of diary of the books you are reading and the conversations you are having.

“Literature never goes out of style. A home without books isn’t a home in my opinion. And a bookshelf acts as a blank canvas to explore your innate curatorial abilities,” says Alex Bass, founder and CEO of Art Advisory & Interior Design Studio Salon 21. “It is also something that grows with you and that you can constantly reorganize. So investing in a good one with timeless appeal is key.”

High-quality natural materials such as wood or stone just get better with time. “Using natural materials in your home is always a good idea,” says Pendergrast. “They bring warmth, texture, and that feeling of authenticity we all crave.”

Wood, whether it’s flooring, furniture, or even ceiling beams, adds a cozy, lived-in vibe, while stone is durable and elegant. “Wood and stone age gracefully, developing character as the years go by. They’re versatile enough to fit into any style, from modern to rustic, which means you won’t have to worry about them ever going out of fashion,” she says.

While we're on the topic of natural materials, let's not forget this inimitable combination. Marble and wood both have a knack for adding character and aging beautifully, and they're not just for kitchens. Think: Wooden chairs pulled up to a marble-topped dining table or a wood coffee table or console with a marble top. The combination is “both timeless, modern, and neutral while bringing a luxurious feel to your space,” says Bass.

Leather furniture carries memories—as a piece ages, its patina changes. And a leather chair can hold its own—the material has weight and personality that make it an excellent companion to a wide range of nearby colors, fabrics, and patterns. “It fits any type of design aesthetic, whether you’re going for something more mid-century or more traditional,” Bass says. “I love seeing the wear in a piece, the cracks and the wrinkles.”

Sticking with paint colors inspired by nature—such as soft blues, calming greens, whites, and warm tans—helps create a peaceful and balanced space. “These colors are easy on the eyes and make your home feel more like a retreat,” Pendergrast says. “Earthy, natural shades are always in style because they have this effortless way of grounding a space. They act like neutrals, so you can easily switch up your decor over the years without having to repaint the whole place.”

A wooden table, such as an antique farm table, is a timeless piece worth investing in. As demonstrated in this space designed by Clara Jung of Banner Day Interiors, it grounds the room, allowing other decor choices to be light and airy. Whether it’s a coffee table, side table, or made for a dining room, “You’ll always find use for it and it’s something sturdy that will never go out of style,” Bass says.

Stripes are a classic pattern that doesn’t ever feel too trendy. “You can throw them on curtains, rugs, or even wallpaper, and they’ll add a bit of structure without overpowering the room,” Pendergrast says. “Whether you go bold or keep it subtle, stripes always find a way to fit in.” The best part about stripes is how flexible they are. “They work with just about any style, and because they’re so simple, they won’t clash with other patterns or elements in your space,” she adds.

Built-in details such as crown molding or wainscoting give your space instant character. Your home may already have these features, but if it doesn’t, they are often simple additions you can implement. “These features add a little polish without feeling too stuffy, and they make the room feel more custom and unique,” Pendergrast says. “Crown molding and wainscoting have been around for ages, and they’re not going anywhere.” These architectural details are subtle ways to add depth and elegance to a room. Even better is that they can blend with any style, whether your vibe is traditional or modern.

#6714. On the Couch with Valentina De Santis

The third-generation Lake Como hotelier shares her favorite places to travel, shop, and dine—both in Italy and abroad.

If Eloise had the Plaza, Valentina De Santis had the Grand Hotel Tremezzo, the luxury Lake Como resort her family has owned and operated for three generations. A Como native who spent her early career consulting at Bain & Company, she began working at the property in an official capacity in 2010, the year of its centenary. “Unofficially,” she tells us, “I clocked in as a child, running through the corridors and convinced the whole hotel depended on me.”

In 2016, De Santis took over as CEO and, by 2022, she and her parents had bought a nearby 18th-century villa and transformed it into another five-star hotel, Villa Passalacqua. As De Santis’s portfolio continues to expand, she finds comfort in working with her family and the team that’s known her for her whole life: “People who grow with you, believe in what you are building, and care about it as much as you do, that’s everything.”

In our On the Couch survey, we asked De Santis about all the places, people, and pieces that inspire her. She also divulges a few of her best travel recommendations—both in Como and beyond.

Describe your studio in three words: Full. Of. Chaos.

Greatest Influences: My parents, my first and forever creative directors.

Favorite Eras of Design: The Belle Époque, when more was more and every room seemed to have a little champagne in its veins.

Favorite Sofa: A Daisy curved Italian midcentury sofa from the 1950s. It's beautifully sculptural, it's pink. It's Gio Ponti.

Maverick design style trick: Break one rule. If everything in a room is behaving, something is missing.

#6712. 3 Unexpected Places to Paint Before the Holidays, According to Designers

These small paint projects can make a big impact before guests arrive.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pictured above: A living room area designed by Kristen Thomas of Ink & Canvas Design

A fresh coat of paint is one of the easiest ways to give your home a little refresh before the holidays, but you don't necessarily have to take on an entire redesign project to make an impact.

Some of the most interesting places to introduce color are the details you might normally overlook. They're relatively small surfaces, but a little paint can make them feel like intentional design features, adding character and definition without the time, expense, or commitment of repainting every wall.

Whether you want to make a statement, add a little contrast, or create a more interesting backdrop, there are plenty of ways to make a big visual impact with a relatively small project. Ahead, designers share the unexpected spots worth painting before the holidays and how to choose colors that feel right at home.

Window casings are an easy detail to overlook, especially when they're painted the same white as the walls and trim throughout the house. But introducing a little color around the windows “can emphasize the proportions of a window and give the room more definition,” says designer Sarah Cole. Even if you're not ready to go bold, she adds that “a subtle contrast can make a difference.”

For a more traditional look, Kristen Thomas of Ink & Canvas Design recommends pulling from a historical or traditional palette, pointing out that “colored trim was once a sign of status in early American homes” because rich pigments were costly and difficult to come by.

When time is tight before the holidays, this simple painting project can be achieved in a single afternoon. Just don’t forget the painter’s tape.

Don't overlook the design impact of the interior doors in your home—a hallway lined with plain white doors can feel a little forgettable, but painting one or two in a contrasting shade can give the space a more intentional look. Paint it early enough, and you may be able to hang a wreath from it.

Massimo Buster Minale, founder and creative director of Buster + Punch, believes an accent like this “can completely shift the character of a room.” And you don't necessarily need to choose a bright or dramatic color to get that effect. Picking one you love, rather than going all-in on a trendy shade, is key.

“I recommend choosing a grounding color and adding some whimsy with the small touches,” says Lee Crowder, National Director of Design and Model Experience at Taylor Morrison. Before painting, take a look at the doors in adjoining rooms so you can decide whether a single standout door or a more consistent color story makes the most sense.

If you have built-in bookshelves or cabinetry, you already have a natural spot for a little color. Rather than repainting the entire unit, try painting just the back panels. This also gives you the opportunity to declutter what’s already there. Cole recommends this pre-holiday spot because “it adds depth and helps books and collections stand out.” Her advice for choosing a shade? “Pull a color from the room's upholstery, rug or artwork.”

Thomas also recommends painting built-in shelving and cabinetry as a way to create a special feature in the room. “Painting built-ins sets them apart as a special feature and creates a beautiful backdrop for your favorite objects and holiday decor,” she says.

Instead of going for a bold contrasting hue, Vivian Robins at Arketa Design Studio suggests using a color that already appears elsewhere in the space, explaining that this small paint update can work as a “punctuation mark” in the room.

“Sometimes the smallest design changes create the biggest impression within a room because you're drawing out a color that lives in the space,” Robins adds.

#6710. 7 Home Organizing Trends That Will Be EVERYWHERE in 2027, According to Pros

Your clutter-free future starts with what you already own.

Although trends prevail in the interior design world, they also can find their way into other areas beyond decor—such as home organization. Creating orderly spaces can make life a lot easier. While tried-and-true solutions are still around, there are always fresh ways to tackle organizing projects.

As we look to the year ahead, four professional organizers are here to share what they think will be the top organizing trends.

While AI has been all over the place, from work to our personal lives—it appears to be tapering off, according to Lenora O’Keith, decluttering and organizing expert and owner of Aronel Home.

“AI and smart home solutions were everywhere at the beginning of the year, but there’s definitely been a lot of AI fatigue and a push for ‘dumb tech’ lately,” she shares. “When working with clients, a lot of them are over AI being added into everything. They just want simple solutions that work.” This can include things like setting up a household command center and labeling storage containers.

Moving has traditionally been a stressful experience that you were expected to figure out on your own—but that may be changing. Allison Flinn, owner of Reclaim Professional Organizing, says that more people are taking advantage of organizing services when they move.

“You can pack and unpack, but you won't truly have a fully functional and an organized new home if you haven't decluttered prior to moving or aren't intentional during the unpacking process by implementing a system that truly works for your lifestyle,” she says.

Instead of doing what a lot of us do—devising storage systems for each individual space—2027 may welcome the idea that you should treat your home as an entire unit to be organized.

Michelle Urban, owner and founder of The Organized House, says that people are increasingly not treating each room as its own one-off organizing project. She says that instead, they’re looking at how belongings move from one space to another and where they need to be throughout the day. For instance, items like backpacks, shoes, and sports gear may land in the entry, get stored in the garage, and need to be back by the door the next morning.

“When those spaces are planned together, the system supports the family’s routine instead of just moving things from one pile to another,” Urban says.

Viewed more holistically, experts are also seeing that organizing isn’t just an occasional task, but something to stay on top of each week. “I think more homeowners will begin to view organization as ongoing home maintenance rather than a one-time project,” says Ashley Bailey, founder and professional organizer at Everything Orderly. “Just like other aspects of maintaining a home, organizing requires occasional attention as our lives and needs change.”

Flinn says that more and more people are outsourcing household tasks to a home manager or home concierge. In addition to handling things like doing laundry, returning packages, and making doctor’s appointments, these professionals can also help you organize your home in just a few hours a week.

Eco-friendly everything is still as popular as ever, and it even applies to how we organize our homes. While O’Keith says that sustainability never really goes out of style, homeowners are increasingly turning to eco-friendly organizing products—specifically, by avoiding plastic and embracing natural materials like bamboo, rattan, and sea grass for baskets and bins.

But she says that there is a exception for basements and attics. “I always recommend heavy-duty plastic storage [for these areas],” O’Keith says. “Since those spaces deal with dampness, temperature changes, and pests, other materials could get damaged easily.”

While it can be lovely to live in a well-designed, beautiful home, if there isn’t function, you might make your daily life more difficult. That’s why people are going beyond Instagram-ready interiors and are wrapping in plenty of function.

Urban believes that progressively more people are bringing functional storage into a room’s design, and it isn’t just an add-on.

“A credenza can hold games in the living room; a dining room buffet can store wrapping paper and party decor between celebrations,” Urban points out. “As rooms take on more jobs, people want storage that looks like it belongs and does a useful job.”

As we head into 2027, people are thinking about their budgets more and more, which is why using items you already have as storage solutions is cost-effective.

“I'm seeing more clients interested in making better use of what they already own rather than automatically purchasing new organizing products,” Bailey says. For instance, she recently took on a project that transformed a playroom, but used items the family already had.

“Sometimes the best solution isn't another organizing product. It's rethinking how you're using the space and what you already have,” she says. “I think we'll see more emphasis on thoughtful editing and repurposing before purchasing something new.”

#6709. Van Cleef & Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

The maison’s latest high jewelry collection taps into the mystique of the Valley of the Kings

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

From opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the New Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

“There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask shows how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. #6698. Can You Ever Really Get Rid of Cellulite? Experts Weigh In

Four experts separate fact from fiction about the most pervasive cellulite myths

There's good news and bad news about cellulite. We'll start with the latter: No matter how much a product or treatment promises to rid your body of those stubborn dimples, there isn't much that can be done. "The appearance of cellulite is nearly impossible to eliminate completely," says Mark Peters, a board-certified plastic surgeon. “Since cellulite is caused by multiple physiologic and anatomic factors, a wide variety of treatments are used, but most fail to address the root cause of cellulite.”

So what's the good news? "It is important to remember that cellulite is a normal finding in the skin and does not require treatment," says Dustin Portela, a board-certified dermatologist and TikTok sensation practicing in Idaho. So, while you may not be able to eliminate cellulite entirely, there are options to help soften its appearance, if that's what you're after. First, let's get into the basics.

People of all shapes and sizes have cellulite—again, it's both normal and common and doesn't need to be treated. "Cellulite is less a defect of fat cells; it's more structural or vascular between the skin's fat and underlying fiber septae. These fibers pull down the skin, creating divots and uneven surfaces or dimples," says Michelle Henry, a board-certified dermatologist in New York City. "There are many factors that contribute to visible cellulite. Thinning of the skin can make cellulite look worse, as well as other abnormalities—that's why we see cellulite in women of all sizes. We see cellulite in 90 percent of women because of the structural component of the skin."

It's also more commonly seen in white women. "The reasons for this are not fully understood," Portela adds, "and although the mechanism is not fully clear, there is reason to believe that estrogen may play a role in the way that cellulite develops." It can also become more apparent with age. "The visibility of cellulite depends on the quality of your skin; thinner, saggier skin will show cellulite more than thicker skin," says Jessie Cheung, a board-certified dermatologist in Chicago and New York City.

We're going to say this one more time: Cellulite is normal and does not need to be treated. But if you're bothered by its appearance, cutting-edge treatments at dermatologists' and plastic surgeons' offices can help minimize the dimples. "The cellulite-specific treatments are Avéli, Emtone, Resonic, and Cellulaze, but any treatment that breaks up the collagen bundles and strengthens the skin will help to treat cellulite," Cheung says.

Avéli is a commonly recommended option that works through subcision, a.k.a. releasing fibers underneath the skin. "It's a minimally invasive procedure that allows you to go under the skin that cuts and tears fiber septae so it no longer causes that pin cushioning," Henry says. But remember: Nonsurgical doesn't mean it's not still a procedure, though the results are impressive.

If you're looking for the most minimally invasive options that tighten skin, you'll want to talk to your doctor about Resonic, Emtone, BodyTite, or InMode Morpheus8. "Resonic and Emtone use acoustic shockwave therapy to break up the fibers, and Emtone adds radio frequency for additional skin tightening, and a series of treatments is required," Cheung says. Meanwhile, BodyTite is a single radio-frequency treatment that Peters says "targets the laxity of the tissues causing the cellulite.”

Both dermatologists also recommend InMode Morpheus8, which could require three treatments. "It's radio frequency microneedling, which doesn't affect the fiber septae, but instead works to stimulate collagen in the skin to thicken the superficial and dermis layer so that cellulite may become less visible," Henry says. Peters, however, offers a note of caution: "It is also important that patients understand that none of these treatments produce permanent results."

We're not telling you to stop using coffee scrubs and body brushes (we love them, too), but just know that they're not going to dramatically change your cellulite. "There is no topical product or home remedy that has been demonstrated in scientific studies to improve the appearance of cellulite for any extended period of time," Peters says. Results from at-home products are temporary. "Topical application of things like caffeine may temporarily cause some skin tightening, or depuffing, but this will not truly resolve any cellulite, other than perhaps a momentary improvement in appearance," Portela says. Products with peptides help boost collagen production in your skin, Henry adds, which could help reduce the appearance of cellulite.

Viberg, a third-generation, family-owned boot specialist, got its start making footwear for forestry crews in British Columbia, and it still builds every pair in its Vancouver Island workshop. While it doesn’t make boots for smokejumpers and loggers anymore, old-school shoemaking techniques like a channeled insole Goodyear welt and stitchdown construction ensure their footwear retains its heirloom-level durability.

The backstory of the “brodequin” begins with the Mdl. 1893, a hobnail-soled marching boot created for French infantrymen in the late 1800s that remained in use until the 1950s. In the First World War, the brodequin’s iron-studded soles and sturdy uppers proved ideally suited to the muddy conditions of the trenches, and American troops soon adopted them in place of their inferior M1904 Marching Shoes. “To me, it’s the blueprint for many combat boots across different countries after WWI,” says Borsarello. “I love the idea that France inspired others after fighting side by side in the trenches. They’re very simple, yet instantly recognizable at the same time.”

For all of its strengths in the field, the original brodequin—whose nickname pays tongue-in-cheek homage to a medieval torture device—wasn’t what you’d call a comfortable boot, especially by modern standards. “They were so stiff that they were almost impossible to walk in,” Borsarello admits. This created something of a design challenge.

To stay as true as possible to the original, Borsarello and Viberg designed the boots on a period-correct “pied haricot” (bean-shaped) last, with a cotton twill lining, solid brass hardware, and reinforced panels that support the midfoot so the forefoot can move freely. To make the Modèle M1917 something you’d actually want to wear, they replaced the hobnailed soles with rubber ones and swapped the original roughout leather for supple American-tanned Horween. The result is a pair of boots that are as comfortable as they are durable, not to mention surprisingly versatile. In other words, exactly what you’d expect from a meeting of these formidable talents.
