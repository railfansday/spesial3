#7010. Study: Sign-In Problems Are Primary Drivers of Help Desk Tickets

Sign-in problems account for up to 53% of help desk tickets at individual enterprises, but network issues can take significantly longer to resolve, according to a new study from ControlUp.

Analyzing 1,069,110 help desk tickets, the IT operations company identified password resets, account lockouts, and multifactor authentication (MFA) issues as major drivers of support demand.

At one large enterprise, account-related tickets took a median of 11 minutes to resolve, compared with 50 hours for network problems. Meanwhile, 87% of network and phone tickets were reassigned between teams, compared with 20% of account tickets.

MFA enrollment and re-enrollment problems appeared at 40% of organizations, with one in five linking these issues to employees replacing or upgrading their phones. Virtual desktop problems appeared at 55% of organizations analyzed.

Traditional phone support remained a major channel for IT assistance.

Among organizations that recorded how support requests were submitted, 57% reported that phone calls accounted for approximately two-thirds or more of contacts. At one organization, that figure reached 99%.

Machine-generated tickets from monitoring, security, backup, and patching systems appeared at half of the organizations studied. At 15%, automated tickets represented between 25% and 50% of all requests.

The research covered enterprises across retail, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, construction, energy, and IT services.

ControlUp said the findings highlight the need to evaluate recurring problems and their operational impact rather than relying solely on ticket volume and resolution speed.

"IT organizations have traditionally measured support performance by how quickly they close tickets, but that doesn't tell the whole story," said Simon Townsend, senior vice president of marketing and office of the CTO at ControlUp.

"A password reset that takes a few minutes and an infrastructure failure that disrupts an entire location shouldn't carry the same operational weight."

#7007. Lafayette 148’s Emily Smith on 30 Years in Fashion and Finding Her Uniform

In ELLE’s Office Hours series, we ask people in powerful positions about their first jobs, worst jobs, and everything in between. This month, we sat down with Lafayette 148 creative director Emily Smith as the New York-based fashion house celebrates its 30th anniversary. Smith has spent more than two decades at the helm of the brand, working her way up from a part-time freelancer to creative lead. But her fashion ambitions began long before she arrived in New York. Growing up in Atlanta, she ran her own neighborhood art camp before discovering designers like Donna Karan and realizing fashion could be a career. Now, Smith is celebrating the women who have shaped Lafayette 148 through its “Uniform” campaign, which highlights accomplished New Yorkers and their individual approaches to personal style. Below, Smith reflects on her unconventional start, why she’s stayed at the company for over 20 years, and the career advice she wishes she’d followed sooner.

I ran a camp in my neighborhood because, at the time, I thought I wanted to be an art teacher. It was basically babysitting all the neighborhood kids, but I created a flyer, got paid for it, and did the whole thing. I started in second grade, and we did it every year for about eight years. I even started hiring people so we could have two classes going at once. We had swimming lessons. The local news even came and did a segment on it. I loved decorating my classroom and setting everything up. My parents were so nice to give me the garage and basement to do it all. Not necessarily an official job, but my first money-making moment.

I was a bagger at Kroger. It just was not for me.

I was always creative growing up. At a certain point, I discovered Donna Karan. It’s kind of crazy when you see somebody doing something and suddenly realize, Oh, that’s a career. I can do that. I became obsessed with everything in the ’90s, all the New York fashion at the time. That was when I decided I wanted to pursue it. When college applications came around, my mom really encouraged me to go to art school. I started in fashion, and that became my focus. Growing up in Atlanta, I wasn’t necessarily surrounded by a big fashion community. But my dad’s office was across the street from the art museum at one point, and I remember going there and having that exposure. My mom was always crafty and creative, too. She really encouraged us to be who we were.

There’s a language creative people have that’s a little different from the way business people communicate. When I went to art school, I felt like I’d found my people. It wasn’t just fashion people. It was photographers, fine artists, and all these different kinds of creatives. Being around people who thought about things differently was really inspiring. Even today, I thrive around people who have creative insight. There’s always a little more depth to the way they see things.

I interned at Bill Blass for about six months. I moved to New York in July 2001, and we had our fashion show on September 10. I remember Marc Jacobs had his show that day, too, and everybody was celebrating. People were saying, “Just come a little late to work tomorrow.” And then, obviously, September 11 happened. A week later, the entire team was let go, from the top down. What I learned from that experience was the fragility of everything. Don’t take anything for granted. Anything can come and go in a moment. But I also worked with Lars Nilsson and Hervé Pierre Braillard, who were just the kindest people. This industry has a little bit of everything, but I loved that they led with kindness, even when they were under so much pressure. As an intern, I was literally carrying ironing boards down the street and doing a sketch here or there. It was very much about doing whatever needed to be done.

After my internship, I had a brief stint of freelancing. Those were some long months. I remember coming home from one job and my roommates asking how my day was. I said, “Well, I was putting pigs on socks.” I knew I wouldn’t be going back. Freelancing is hard. It’s a grind, but you meet a lot of great people. I actually made some really good contacts during that time. I eventually met Deirdre [Quinn], Lafayette 148’s founder. She met with me on a Saturday, which I thought was incredible. I was really just looking for advice. I wasn’t looking for a job, and I didn’t even know much about the brand. It was only five years old. She was so kind, full of knowledge, and passionate. After I spent a year freelancing and meeting people who seemed so over the industry, her enthusiasm reminded me why I wanted to do this in the first place. She offered me a part-time freelance position, and the rest is history.

In the beginning, security was really important to me. The idea of losing a job and having to pay rent was scary, so that was definitely part of it initially. But really, it was the growth. Within a couple of years, they were sending me to the factory. It was a small, tight-knit company, so I was able to wear a lot of different hats. I had a mentor named Barbara Gast who was traveling back and forth from Italy, and I was learning so much from her. I’d compare notes with friends in the industry and realize how much hands-on experience I was getting. I understood the value of that and really loved being so close to the product. It never got boring. And whenever things did get slightly boring, I’d ask, “Okay, what’s next?” And there was always something. It’s such an entrepreneurial place that you can almost create your next opportunity. That’s what made it so exciting.

I think if you asked Deirdre, she’d have a different answer because she has her own experience of building the company. For me, it’s about having started at a company that was really a factory and being part of the journey of building its identity and what it stands for. I was the kid running up and down the factory floors collecting samples. That hustle and bustle was so innate to who the brand was. To me, it was really exciting to build more of a brand story around that. Thirty years later, we’re still celebrating those roots and making sure we haven’t strayed too far from them, while continuing to evolve. Being part of that journey has been really exciting.

The constant has always been this idea of an accomplished woman. She’s always been at the forefront of who we’re designing for. At the time, it was the career woman. But now, what does that even mean anymore? What does that look like? There are so many different versions of that woman and so many different ways people dress. At the heart of it, she’s a woman who knows what she wants. She’s busy and has a really full life, whether she’s starting her career or ending it. They can all find something in the clothes.

For us, it was about choosing people who inspire us. Number one, they had to be New York women. That was the base. We wanted women who make us want to be better ourselves. From someone like Deborah Berke, the architect, to women who have built incredible careers in other fields, it’s important to shine a light on them. I think back to when I was a kid and how much you need that kind of inspiration. Even at a certain stage in your career, when you’re wondering what the point is, it’s nice to have people who inspire you. These are women who have really built something for themselves, and I think that’s worth celebrating.

Sophie de Rakoff said something during the project that I really liked, and I’m going to steal it: “Dressing with intention.” When you know yourself, you know what works on you. It’s no longer about dressing to impress. There’s a confidence in knowing what works for you and making it work. We’re all so busy. Who has time to overthink getting dressed? You know what works, and you build a system around that. You’re able to create a wardrobe that really works for you. Everybody has a different definition of a uniform, and that was the whole point of the project.

When I first started in the industry, I was a total hippie. I was making my own clothes, and they were terrible. Absolutely falling apart. It was what it was. I think part of the change comes from not having as much time as you did when you were younger. But it’s also about simplicity and comfort. I know that if I’m going to be on the floor pinning something but also have to give a presentation, there are certain pieces that work for both. I can be comfortable and still feel appropriately dressed. I’ve found a handful of things that work for me, and I don’t have to think too much about them anymore. A crisp, oversize white cotton button-down is half my closet. I also love a soft skirt and a big chunky sweater. Those are the pieces I always go back to and interchange depending on the day. I even wear sweaters in the summer. It’s cold in the Navy Yard!

Our pants fit incredibly well. The moment somebody gets into our pants, they’ll love us forever. Then it’s the jacket and the white shirt. Those three things are our gateway pieces, and everything else follows. You need a good blazer and a good leather piece, too. Once you find those foundational pieces that really work for you, everything else becomes easier.

I really want to see more women in our clothes. It’s not just about growing the business. It makes me happy when I see people put on our clothes and feel really good. We work so hard to make sure things fit the way a woman wants them to fit. I just want more people to enjoy that.

Robert Pattinson has been announced as the next 007, and while most of the speculation has understandably focused on how the former vampire/current Batman might approach Britain's foremost secret agent, watch aficionados are asking another question: What will he wear on his wrist?

Watches, after all, are almost as much a part of Bond's wardrobe as his dinner jackets, Aston Martins, and vodka martinis. Sean Connery famously wore a Rolex Submariner, Roger Moore experimented with Seiko's digital watches, and Omega has been 007's watch of choice since Pierce Brosnan strapped on a trusty Seamaster in GoldenEye in 1995. That brand remains, of course, a paid partner of the franchise.

Yesterday's reveal showed no watch on the newly anointed 007's wrist, and Pattinson's own taste in watches is rather different from the rugged, sporty timepieces traditionally associated with Bond.

In fact, he doesn't seem particularly keen on big watches at all.

"Whenever a watch gets too thick and it keeps your cuff up, I don't really understand the appeal," he told one interviewer as recently September. "A lot of guys really like that, but I don't think I can really pull that off. I need to only have a half reveal."

And when asked what he looks for in a watch, he was even more specific.

"I definitely like really thin watches. That's the main thing. In general, I would probably prefer a leather strap."

Which is quite a contrast with the substantial divers we've become accustomed to seeing on Bond's wrist.

The fact is, until recently, Pattinson wasn't much of a watch enthusiast at all. That changed at this year's Oscars, when he wore a then-unreleased rose-gold Jaeger-LeCoultre Master Control Chronomètre Perpetual Calendar. Other actors apparently began asking him about it at the afterparty, and something clicked.

He subsequently became an ambassador for Jaeger-LeCoultre, although his relationship with the brand goes back much further. As a teenager, he borrowed his mother's Reverso to impress his schoolmates. More recently, he's revealed that Bruce Wayne will wear a Reverso in the next Batman film.

On screen, Pattinson's watch choices have been interesting. He wore a Chanel J12 in David Cronenberg's Cosmopolis, while Christopher Nolan's Tenet, in which he played a mysterious secret agent, featured a Bond-adjacent Omega De Ville.

Meanwhile, the announcement has prompted some entertaining activity on Instagram. Within hours of Pattinson being named Bond, Jaeger-LeCoultre posted pictures of its new ambassador wearing the Master Control Chronomètre Perpetual Calendar. Omega, meanwhile, posted an image of its James Bond Seamaster. And today, Jaeger-LeCoultre has been back with more Pattinson pictures.

It's a little social-media tit-for-tat, although there's no reason the two relationships can't coexist. For now, at least, there's a straightforward distinction: Pattinson can wear whatever he likes. Bond has his own watch.

#6998. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

No record exists of what transpired, but oh how brilliant Phillis must’ve been as she convinced those 18 eminent Bostonians that she’d indeed written the poems. The confirmation was printed in the front matter of Phillis’s poetry collection—the first book published by an African soon-to-be American—Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773):

We whose Names are under-written, do assure the World, that the Poems specified in the following Page, were . . . written by Phillis, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave in a Family in this Town. Here we are in this brave new world of AI, an age hurtling headlong toward the peril of superintelligence. But then an anonymous X account (later revealed to be owned by a conservative columnist) posted that Pangram had identified all 34 chapters of the book as having been written with AI. In October, The Atlantic accused writer Simbarashe Kundizeza of using AI to write “The Blue Fighter,” which won the Caine Prize, Africa’s most prestigious short-story accolade.

Germane: While Nazir is Trinidadian, Ballard, Wolfe, Falade, Orélien, and Kundizeza are all Black.

Although all those writers have denied claims of using AI to produce their work, I’m not here to dispute whether they did or to what degree they used it, nor the epistemological paradox of using AI to determine whether a writer used AI to write a story. Of equal importance is that, if we ain’t reached the point where all published writing is scanned for possible use of an LLM, what’s the logic informing which writers are investigated for AI use and which aren’t, as well as the attendant issue of which accused writers suffer the most severe fallout, including the scarlet letter of damaged credibility?

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be judged in light of what’s being described as an era of postliteracy. Of the specter of AI sloptimism becoming so ubiquitous it overruns human output. Of the cataclysmic doom of superintelligence approximating the creative work of a human brain to such a degree that the difference will be negligible to the undiscerning masses.

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be apprehended against the beef between the publishing industry writ large and AI. Against 10,000 authors having copublished an “empty” book in protest of their work being pirated to train its models. Against America’s Big Five publishers suing Meta for copyright infringement. Against the recent unsealed documents that reveal OpenAI executives knew they were training their AI on copyrighted material and, furthermore, that AI-authored books were apt to displace in the market ones authored by humans. “There will be a point where a bunch of artists express worry about what we’re doing here, and we’ll likely ignore their concerns and release anyway,” OpenAI policy director Jack Clark wrote in an internal memo back in 2020. The predicament of Black and brown writers should be weighed against the long and calculated history of portraying them as lesser intellects.

Casting doubt on the possibility of Black literary brilliance is in the spirit of a hubristic Saul Bellow asking, “Who is the Tolstoy of the Zulus?” Of 45/47 portraying beaucoup Black people as “not that bright.” Of attacking DEI to paint us as unworthy or incapable, less we are sanctioned by people in power. The same insidious forces—linked to right-wing Cambridge professor James Orr and conservative billionaire Peter Thiel—stoked the allegations that ended in Cambridge professor Jason Arday dying by suicide. (Arday has been posthumously cleared of plagiarism.)

In stark contrast, best-selling novelist and screenwriter Anthony Horowitz has admitted using AI for his work. Best-selling biographer Katie Prescott extolled AI as invaluable in writing her book on Mike Lynch, the late British tech entrepreneur and AI pioneer. And while she’s been called among other things a “grifter” and a “villain,” South African writer Coral Hart has published more than 370 books using artificial intelligence since 2025 and defended her use of LLMs despite criticism and threats. While she later clarified she used it only for research, Nobel laureate Olga Tokarczuk caused a ruckus revealing she queried AI for writing help. Other than Santiago Schnell, a white Latino, resigning as the provost of Dartmouth for using AI in his published articles and The New York Times cutting ties with Alex Preston for an AI-assisted book review, beyond vocal to tepid criticism, white writers aren’t suffering material consequences on par with those of their Black peers.

Poor Phillis Wheatley never published a second book. Despite advertising six times in the newspaper to find a publisher. Despite announcing she’d dedicate it to Benjamin Franklin. Despite General George Washington extolling her as “favored by the muses.” In fact, in 1784, Phillis died at 30 years young, destitute, having placed the last advertisement for her second manuscript just a few months prior.

Because Big Tech is big-time thriving and race is evermore an oppressive tool, critics are bound to keep casting the work of Black writers as AI assisted, in effect electing themselves nouveau versions of those privileged 18th-century Bostonians. Best believe their indictments are a tacit way to portray the work of Black people as unworthy of patronage, acclaim, awards—aka inferior. But be forewarned: If Big Tech succeeds at AI turning the creative work of human brains passé if not obsolete, it’s near foregone that most writers will lose their ability to earn a living. Indeed, the sad-ass upshot is that if AI prevails, all writers—race be damned—will be fated a Phillis.

#6978. What to Eat for Healthier Skin and Slower Aging

A dietitian breaks down the foods, nutrients, and habits that may support skin from the inside out.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

On the quest to age smarter and slower, there’s a lot that your skin care can do. But perhaps one of the greatest contributions lies in the foods you consistently eat. Of course, diet and exercise are the pillars of good health, proper body function, and longevity, but a recent study done by scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) also reveals that 10 specific diets are linked to slower aging. Experts unpack the findings for ELLE and break down which foods you should incorporate or ditch from your diet to help improve skin health.

To understand the connection between diet and skin health, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry, says it’s important to define the notion of slowing down aging. She points out that this particular DZNE study didn’t look at wrinkles or how old people feel. “It looked at biological aging, measured with epigenetic clocks. These read chemical marks on our DNA (methylation) that shift as our cells age. In about 7,500 adults, the researchers found that following any of 10 healthy eating patterns more closely was associated with slower biological aging,” Hosmer explains. The 10 eating patterns or diets included in the study results are Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based. Hosmer says that while the effect was modest, this kind of study shows an association, not causation—proof that diet is the reason for the positive outcome—so it is still a meaningful signal.

Additionally, Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Plan, tells ELLE that no single food has been satisfactorily studied and shown to be likely, on its own, to slow aging. “Of all the predictors of a long, healthy life, two of the best, which can be improved with diet, are muscle mass and strength, but this leads to a controversial conclusion,” says Gilbert. “The best nutritional intervention to increase muscle and strength is eating a high-protein diet. However, many scientists argue that high-protein diets have the opposite effect on longevity. So, if we can’t even agree on that, you can see that science has a long way to go to try and figure this out.”

One stat Hosmer found particularly striking was that over 70 percent of the biological pathways affected were the same across all ten diets. “I interpret that to mean that no single superfood is doing the work, and it affirms that [a combination of] food, diet, and nutrition impacts us on the cellular level. The foods these patterns share are what matter,” says Hosmer, who, along with Gilbert, breaks down the standouts:

Alongside individual foods, Hosmer says certain diet components have the greatest impact on aging. For starters, protein is a must. “As we age, our muscles respond less efficiently to the protein we eat. This is called anabolic resistance, and in women, the drop in estrogen makes it worse,” she explains. Building and retaining muscle isn’t only about strength; Hosmer says it’s our largest site for handling blood sugar and a strong predictor of independence later in life. “Many women eat less protein just when they need more.”

Gilbert adds that while protein is an important part of a balanced, longevity diet, there are some considerations to keep in mind before adding it to your meal. “I believe that because muscle and strength are so strongly associated with longevity and that protein, particularly later in life, supports them,” he says. The caveat, however, is that there is a potential mechanism (which has to do with the amino acid methionine) whereby too much protein could have negative effects. “Many scientists argue that, because in insects and some other animals, less protein along with calorie restriction increases lifespan, humans would similarly benefit. Also, some cite the so-called ‘Blue Zones’, alleged to show that plant-based, low-protein diets are superior. In my opinion, all of these have been shown to be invalid,” says Gilbert.

Fiber is another essential nutrient that you’ll reap from plant foods and mushrooms. Meat, fish, dairy, and eggs contain no fiber, and because our enzymes can’t break them down, Hosmer says that fiber travels intact to the large intestine to assist. Once there, fermentable fibers become food for the gut microbiome; other fibers mainly add bulk and keep things moving. “As gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the cells lining the gut, help keep that barrier strong, and keep inflammation in check. Fiber also steadies blood sugar and helps the body excrete estrogen the liver has already processed, supporting healthy hormone balance. Yet more than 90 percent of American women don’t reach the recommended 21 to 25 grams a day,” Hosmer explains.

Plant diversity matters, too, and not just quantity. Hosmer points to research from the American Gut Project that found people who ate 30 or more different kinds of plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. “That’s because each plant brings its own mix of fibers and polyphenols, and some bacteria have preferences, so a varied diet feeds a broader community of microbes. A broader community does more jobs. It makes short-chain fatty acids that protect the gut lining, helps regulate immunity and inflammation, turns plant compounds into forms the body can use, and even helps process estrogen.” The variety of plants Hosmer adds seems to matter more than any single “perfect” plant.

You’ve likely heard of healthy fats, meaning omega-3s from fish and the polyphenol-rich fat in olive oil. Adding these to your diet can support cell membranes and help settle inflammation.

Also on the list for Gilbert is vitamin D, which he says studies have shown to be inversely related to the risk of mortality. “This does not prove that low vitamin D is the cause, but it is compelling evidence. Although some results are controversial, low [vitamin D] levels have been linked to all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and infection-related mortality,” he adds. Vitamin D is intimately involved in immunity, too. It protects T-cell immune response.

Although Gilbert says, “The only thing all good nutritionists agree on is that there is no one ‘best’ diet or ‘best’ food,” he warns that for most people, minimizing the intake of sugar and refined, starchy grains and increasing protein intake is, in my opinion and experience, the best approach, but, again, different people can thrive on different diets. “As in the examples I gave, many of these foods contain phytochemicals—plant chemicals like polyphenols, flavanols, cyanidins, and others – which affect disease risk or support systems in the body that are likely to serve you well in old age.”

Hosmer notes several other red flags as contributing to unhealthy skin: Diets dominated by refined carbohydrates and added sugars, heavily processed meat, and regular alcohol. “Each of these drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation researchers call “inflammaging,” and blood-sugar instability.”

It’s made to help close the protein, fiber, and plant-variety gaps that so many women are carrying,” she adds.

The information can be overwhelming, but instead of purging everything in your fridge, Hosmer suggests first focusing on adding good foods instead of restricting less healthful options. “In my experience, that’s more sustainable, both because it feels less like deprivation and because the “good” foods can displace the ones that speed aging up instead of slowing it down. You don’t need to pick the “right” diet and follow it perfectly.”

She highlights that the DZNE study found few people scored well on all ten diets at once, yet better adherence to any of them helped. What matters is the common ground. Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based diets vary on the plate, but they have a common core: more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and less red and processed meat, and added sugar. “That shared core is where I’d start,” says Hosmer, offering this meal planning guide:

Increase your familiarity with, and use of, plant-based proteins. Plant-based proteins offer an excellent package of beneficial nutrients, including fiber (not found in animal products). If you aren’t already familiar, learn how to include whole soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame) in your diet; add beans and peas and lentils to dishes here and there. Nuts and seeds and nut and seed butters count here also.

Build meals around vegetables and fruit. Fill half your plate with them, and choose deep colors like leafy greens, berries, beets, and cabbage-family vegetables. Those colors come from polyphenols, plant compounds that act as antioxidants and feed beneficial gut bacteria.

Make your grains whole and varied. Oats, rye, barley, brown rice, and whole-wheat bread are staples of the Nordic and DASH patterns, the two most consistently linked to slower aging in this study. Try a grain you haven’t eaten before—quinoa? Farro? Millet or teff? They are all cooked the same way—in boiling water, just with different grain-to-water ratios. Note: raise fiber gradually, with plenty of water, so your gut has time to adjust.

Count your plants, not your calories. Aim toward 30 different kinds of plant foods a week. Veggies, fruits, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans, peas, herbs, and spices all count. A homemade trail mix, an interesting salad, or a smoothie with a handful of fruit and veggies can add three or four at once.

Choose your fats deliberately. Most of these patterns favor unsaturated fats over saturated ones. Use extra-virgin olive oil as your everyday fat, and eat oily fish two or three times a week.

Keep red and processed meat occasional, and keep added sugar low. Every one of these diets limits them.

Quality matters. Note that “plant-based” isn’t automatically healthy. A version of the plant-based diet built on refined grains, sweets, and sugary drinks tended to be associated with faster aging, as did a pro-inflammatory eating pattern overall. Food quality matters, not just food category.

Diet is the foundation the other efforts rest on. Collagen supplements and skin care can help, but they can’t make up for a body that lacks what it needs to build and protect tissue. According to Gilbert, collagen supplements contain very high levels of the amino acid glycine, which he says can balance the potential harms of proteins that are high in methionine.

Hosmer further explains that collagen peptides break down during digestion into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then decides where to use them. “To actually build collagen, it needs enough total protein, plus vitamin C. Vitamin C is a required helper for the enzymes that stabilize the collagen structure. It also needs minerals like zinc and copper. So a collagen supplement taken on top of a protein-poor, micronutrient-poor diet is not going to be optimized,” Hosmer says. “Collagen is also an incomplete protein: it’s low in leucine and lacks tryptophan entirely. It can add to your protein intake, but it isn’t a substitute for complete proteins when it comes to muscle.”

On the surface, what we eat affects the skin from the inside. Hosmer notes that polyphenol-rich foods and healthy fats support the skin’s own defenses against oxidative stress, and high blood sugar leads to glycation, which stiffens collagen fibers. The gut matters here, too. “Researchers call the connection the gut–skin axis. When the gut lining is healthy, and the microbiome is well fed, inflammation throughout the body tends to stay lower, and the skin reflects that. External products can only do so much if we aren’t supporting our skin from the inside,” Hosmer says.

Diet plays a major role in overall skin health and slower aging, but Hosmer argues that it’s not the full picture. To optimize more youthful-looking skin, consider other factors that may be equally as important as diet.

In some cases, these routines would be the envy of any younger man! The men had all played some type of sports when they were younger and carried that behavior deep into their adult lives. Activities included walking, weight training, stationary bicycling, swimming, pickleball, tennis, and downhill skiing. Two of them have a regular tennis game, and one of the oldest in the group is still an avid golfer. Most of them go to a gym or have a personal trainer. While a few admitted that they were slower as they got older—a sign of remarkable maturity in a man, to admit that—they were all active.

They are also conscious of what they eat. They agreed that they consume less food than in the past and drink very little, if at all. As Hirsch said, “I never drank much, maybe a beer or glass of wine with a meal. But I don’t drink anymore—it just doesn’t mean that much to me.”

Their attention to eating habits stood out. One thing that especially interested them was GLP-1’s, because of their impact on diet but also other potential benefits. As I shared what I’ve learned about longevity, including the frontiers of research on the topic, they asked question after question about the emergence of GLP-1’s and other classes of drugs that may enhance individual longevity. That kind of constant, engaged questioning was a hallmark of the group.

Only one of the men at the lunch defined himself as “retired.” The others said they were, at most, “semiretired.” While they are not necessarily working full throttle in what was their main career, to a man they all have very busy days—and that busyness, I found, is driven by a voracious curiosity, which is one of the best ways to strengthen and maintain brain health.

Ken Langone is a great example. He came from a working-class family in Queens and is still in awe of his good fortune. He’s taken it to mean that, as long as he’s able, he should be doing everything he can not to squander what he has. “I won big, and I can’t tell you how grateful I am,” he said, adding, “We should be giving, not taking.”

He is in his office three days a week and works from home two days a week, engaged in a public board, three private boards, his family office, and other investments. His commitment to public health is incredibly important to him; he serves as chair emeritus of NYU Langone Health.

“At 91,” Langone says, “my biggest issue is boredom, sitting around with nothing to do.”

Jim Freund, also 91, was a high-powered attorney into his early 60s, did a stint as a mediator after that, and now has a robust life as a writer, photographer, and musician.

He’s written 20 fiction and nonfiction books and has produced 25 albums of music featuring him at the piano. For six years, he has circulated a monthly blog for more than 500 friends and colleagues that includes the details of various projects he’s working on—an essay or short fiction, a piece of music, a portfolio of photos.

One expression of this curiosity is that all the men want to keep current. They follow the news, are dedicated readers, and have a deep curiosity about emerging trends. They don’t just read print; they’re on their phones, Kindles, Audible—anywhere there’s action. And AI—they’re on ChatGPT like everyone else. Some even have digital assistants.

But being current doesn’t just mean trendspotting. It can also mean having hobbies and projects that force you to engage with the world as it is. All of these men had ambitious new endeavors in the works.

Hirsch now has a bimonthly podcast with the running legend Amby Burfoot called “Running: State of the Sport.” Dan Rose, 97, former president and chairman of Rose Associates, the company that built Pentagon City and One Financial Center in Boston, is writing a new book. Patricof and his wife are taking Yiddish lessons. At least one man reported that he was finally learning the piano.

For all of them, it is important that their activities, whatever they may be, connect to deeper meaning and purpose.

Larry Leeds, 97, the former CEO and chairman of the apparel company Manhattan Industries, now works at Buckingham Capital Management five days a week buying and selling securities. It allows him to keep up with trends and investments. But it’s far from the most important thing to which he’s dedicated his energy.

Leeds hopes the leading line in his obituary is the $27 million he raised to launch the Cary Leeds Center for Tennis and Learning in the South Bronx in 2015. Dedicated to athletic instruction and academic enrichment for underprivileged youth—health and head—the center is also an expression of the most important thing in Leeds’s life: his family. It is named after his son, who competed in six US Opens and five Wimbledon championships before dying at the age of 45.

Sitting there in Patroon, I heard stories of how these men had committed to keeping themselves healthy and how their curiosity led them to continue to engage with the world at a point in their lives when they could just as easily have settled into insular routine. But it was the lunch itself that testified to their commitment to their hearts. Ninety years in, they were still making a point of getting together, speaking, laughing, and growing closer to other people.

Upon arriving, as we sat and introduced ourselves, Patricof explained that he wanted to do this lunch not only to learn from one another at this stage of life but also to simply enjoy socializing with peers—like many women do on a regular basis. Women live five to six more years than men; maybe it is their commitment to deep relationships, he suggested, that is one of the key factors of their greater longevity.

Women came up in another way too: Everyone attributed their health and longevity to having a loving partner.

The majority of the men have had incredibly long marriages. Nine of the 12 had been married for more than 60 years. Two had been married for 70 years. Then there was Freund, who joked that he, too, has been married for 60 years—just not all to the same woman. (He is married to his second wife, and they are at 41 years and counting.)

They all also have children and grandchildren, whom they are in touch with more than a few times a week—something any of us who are parents can do.

Rose has a unique ritual. “We have a family video conference every Sunday night with family from all parts of the country. It’s life enhancing and a time to connect and give everyone a chance to share accomplishments and disappointments with each other,” he explained.

At this stage of their lives, many have lost longtime friends, but they all strive to make new friends, some young, some old. In fact, there’s already a plan in place to replicate the lunch so that a bunch of other 90-somethings can be invited into the conversation. Here’s the thing I noticed about this: What got these guys excited about another lunch wasn’t simply that they enjoyed each other’s company or having a laugh with peers about Viagra. They were also inspired by one other. That must count for something.

I walked away inspired, too, and in awe of how these men live. They have to walk a line that most of us don’t yet, and maybe never will. Part of being alive—especially for this group—is thinking about the future, trying to figure out what’s next, planning another pursuit, but now they all admit that their horizon of looking out, say, 25 years is no longer a reality.

In the face of that tension, they’ve found a way to be optimistic about their future while at the same time savoring the present. None complained about getting older. None talked about their aches and pains. Yes, they faced growing limitations, but they all seemed to take them in stride.

“I do my best to love my life and try to have the same intensity and diligence that I had in my younger years,” said Leeds.

Langone said, “When I can no longer take care of myself and I’m totally dependent on someone else, then that’s the time to go,” adding that “God has his plan.”

And Patricof has his. He’s already publicly stated that he plans to live to 114, which would be 22 more years. I pointed out that the world’s oldest verified living person, Ethel Caterham in the UK, just turned 117, and I challenged Alan to break that record. He gladly told me that he accepts the challenge, and I don’t doubt he’ll make a real run at it. I have to admit I have an ulterior motive: I look forward to my lunch with centenarians. And supercentenarians.

#6972. A New Exhibition Celebrates the Mantilla in Fashion and Art

The garment finally gets its due credit at The Hispanic Society Museum & Library.

Few garments have been as imitated, reinterpreted, and misunderstood as the mantilla. The iconic lace veil with Spanish roots has endured for centuries. What started as an object traditionally worn for ceremonies has had a renaissance via Madonna, Rosalía, and Dolce & Gabbana, just to name a few. That’s just one of the reasons why The Hispanic Society Museum & Library in New York is opening its new exhibition “Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” beginning November 20. The museum calls the mantilla the “most distinctive garment in Spanish women’s fashion with origins dating to the 16th century.” Curated by Spanish-born scholar Carlos Usabiaga López, the show merges fashion, art, and archival material to shine a light on an iconic garment that has been donned on screen, on runways, and on red carpets for generations.

“The understanding of the mantilla in all its complexity is often limited, either by a superficial reading of the symbol itself or by its association with an old-fashioned, rather conservative image of Spain,” López tells ELLE. “This exhibition challenges the perception of the mantilla as an anachronistic relic of the past and reclaims its continued cultural relevance and subversive potential. From Spanish countercultural movements such as ‘La Movida Madrileña’ to drag performance, the mantilla has repeatedly been appropriated and reimagined in ways that reveal its capacity to carry new meanings.”

The exhibition is the second installment in the Hispanic Society’s multiyear fashion initiative, which focuses on reinterpreting its own renowned collection through a new lens. The museum was founded in 1903 by American philanthropist Archer Milton Huntington and has many visual examples of the garment, including Francisco Goya’s famed portrait of María del Pilar Teresa Cayetana de Silva y Álvarez de Toledo, the 13th Duchess of Alba (1762–1802). “By bringing together works of art, fashion, and material culture, the iconography of the mantilla activates a collection of such an extraordinary diversity, through unexpected dialogues between the museum’s different departments and international loans,” adds López.

The new exhibition pulls from many cultural moments new and old, including the “Spanish Craze,” which was a period of cultural fascination with Spain in the United States spanning 1890 to 1930. “During this period, the mantilla became part of a highly recognizable visual language through which Spain was imagined and represented in America,” says López. “Spanish performers who travelled to the United States achieved remarkable success: Raquel Meller, for instance, appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1926, while Concha Piquer starred in From Far Seville (1923).” He also points to the reinterpretation of the mantilla in The Devil Is a Woman (1935), starring Marlene Dietrich.

Fashion naturally plays a role in the show, especially through the imagination of emerging designers, like Arturo Obegero. “Throughout the history of fashion, major designers such as Cristóbal Balenciaga and Óscar de la Renta have drawn inspiration from this [Spanish] visual language, including the mantilla,” adds López. “However, it was a curatorial decision to bring contemporary designers, including emerging voices, into direct conversation with the masterpieces in order to make justice to the mantilla’s enduring influence on fashion to this day.”

The mantilla has been loved (and reinvented) in contemporary fashion at least since the 1940s, when it appeared in numerous fashion magazines. Today, the references are endless and people of all cultures and backgrounds are embracing it. “I think there is something particularly beautiful about the fact that those who have historically been excluded from the dominant narrative, who may have felt unrepresented or even oppressed by these national symbols, can now feel encouraged to revisit this folklore and expand its boundaries to make room for others to do the same,” says López. As the exhibition makes clear, the mantilla’s compelling story is still unfolding.

“Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” is on view at The Hispanic Society Museum & Library from Nov. 20, 2026, through March 28, 2027.

#6971. The Latest PlayStation Is 6 Years Old and Twice as Expensive as When It Debuted, What’s That About?

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

Thanks to AI and the RAMpocalypse, it’ll cost you to play the new ‘GTA VI’.

Grand Theft Auto VI is here, and it’s going to shock people heading into the holidays. No, we’re not moralizing about whatever violence, sex, or illegal activity is in the game. We’re talking about sticker shock.

Do you have any idea how much a new entry-level PlayStation 5 costs now? It costs $600, yet it was only $400 when it debuted in 2020. And the entry-level Xbox? It’ll also run you $600, though you can get a model with less storage for $500. Also having launched in 2020, the lower-storage Xbox Series S used to retail for $300. Both those consoles are more than half a decade old, and they have increased in price by 50 percent.

So what does a consumer do? I’m imagining a guy—perhaps this is you, reader. The last time he bought a console was a PlayStation 3 in 2013 for the previous Grand Theft Auto, or even a PS4 in 2018 for Red Dead Redemption 2. He thought about getting a PS5 for EA Sports College Football in 2024 but ultimately passed.This guy doesn’t pay attention to video games—or he only pays attention to the odd AAA release—and he is expecting to pay $400 for the key to experiencing this generation’s only game that matters. He might not even know that the game itself is going for $80, a rarity that only Nintendo has tried, with Mario Kart World. When he walks up to the counter, this hypothetical man will be paying nearly $900 to be able to play GTA VI. And the worst part? He’s not hypothetical. And according to JP Morgan Global Research, prices have risen more than 400 percent in the past two years.

As computer parts become scarcer and more expensive, the price of a console goes up. Things changed, audiences grew and moved elsewhere, and—somewhere between microtransactions and Xbox Game Pass—platform holders needed (or preferred) to make money on hardware again. So if a console is more expensive to manufacture, Sony and Microsoft aren’t going to eat that price. Consumers are seeing that increase directly, perhaps more directly and more acutely than we’ve ever seen any increase in technology prices.

Here’s our advice: Don’t wait. The AI push shows no signs of slowing down, and it’s likely that this RAM pipeline bottleneck won’t fix itself for a year or two. This holiday season is getting underway with a supply and demand squeeze similar to what we saw in 2020, when these consoles originally launched.

If you can swing it, you’ll probably want to buy a new console, but the PlayStation 5 Pro is already impossible to find brand-new. It might come down to shopping for renewed and refurbished consoles. These give you a very slight discount, but really they just give you a console that you can plug in and fire up GTA VI on. Welcome to the future of console gaming: refurbished six-year-old consoles.

#6966. 8 Popular Home Items From 20 Years Ago That Are Making a Surprising Comeback

They may be a blast from the past, but they’re about to be very now.

Trends come and go, but they almost always come back again. If there’s one thing we know about interior design, it’s that what feels old and outdated manages to become fresh and cool again in a few years’ time. Skeptics can easily be proved wrong with the resurgence of sunken living rooms, glass block walls, and even cushy sofas plucked right out of a Nora Ephron movie. And, as younger generations continue to be intrigued with the 1990s and early 2000s, they are turning to the era’s interiors for inspiration.

Well, sort of, as Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios points out. “When we look back at certain interiors from the 2000s, there was such a commitment to achieving a particular look that every element in the room was chosen to reinforce it,” the Georgia designer says. “A home should feel like a collection of things you genuinely love, not as though you’ve walked onto a movie set designed to represent a particular style.”

When you cherry-pick trends and design details from the past, you’re able to create a home that honors the past without appearing stuck in the past. “The goal isn’t to make a home look like it belongs to another decade,” Vollrath says. “You want to bring back the beauty and character of those decorative details while creating something that feels authentic to both the house and its owners.”

Below, designers share trends from the past that the next generation of design lovers adore.

For older generations, a swivel chair might conjure memories of sitting in your family’s den, but designers promise these movin’, groovin’ seats have been revived for the 21st century. “Swivel barrel chairs defined early-2000s transitional interiors, usually in a bright flat polyester-blend upholstery,” explains Hallie Malitsky, principal designer of Hallie Rose Design in Brooklyn. “The version coming back today is a different experience entirely.”

Today’s iterations, she insists, are subtler. Cheap polyester has yielded to rich, tactile fabrics like wool, linen, and velvet. For a timely twist, she also encourages trading the fire engine red and butter yellow popular in the 2000s for burgundy and ochre. “Surround it with natural materials like wood, linen, and stone, and [your chair] will stop being the loudest thing in the room,” she recommends.

When Malitsky thinks of early aughts design, she immediately envisions the gigantic walnut media center that plagued many of her friends’ family rooms. “Dark wood cabinetry is a notorious culprit of bad 2000s design, but the wood wasn’t the issue,” she says. “It was the fact that there was so much of it.”

Now that dark wood tones are making a comeback, Malitsky says to use them sparingly. Two to three walnut or ebony pieces per room will make enough of a statement. And, no: Unlike the living rooms of yore, your furniture doesn’t have to match.

“What I love about their return is that we’re incorporating these pieces in a much more collected way,” shares Vollrath. “Rather than purchasing an entire matching furniture collection, we’re mixing periods and bringing in individual pieces that have character and history.”

The early aughts had lots of elaborate cabinetry, replete with corbels, substantial cornices, and beautifully detailed range hoods. As everyone—yes, even younger generations—continue to favor more traditional interiors, we’re seeing a renewed interest in ornamented cabinetry.

“There’s something really special about a kitchen that feels as though it could have been there for generations, or even reminds you of your grandmother’s kitchen,” Vollrath says. “Those decorative flourishes bring a sense of history, craftsmanship, and permanence that can be difficult to achieve with a more streamlined approach.”

But why stop there when you can add character to other rooms of the house?

“We’re seeing a broader interest in architectural detailing throughout the home, from wainscoting and wall paneling to ceiling beams and substantial moldings,” she adds. “Homeowners are becoming more interested in understanding these elements, considering them during renovations, and making them a priority in new construction.”

If the rise of analog interiors proves anything, it’s that even the best chatbot can’t beat excellent design. Instead of having a home covered in screens and smart systems, many are favoring more primitive setups that harken back to dial-up and CD-ROMs. For example, Fargo-based designer Lindsey Christie has been noticing (and loving) the return of landlines. “There's something wonderfully nostalgic about a beautiful telephone sitting on a bedside table or tucked into a thoughtfully designed corner of a home,” she says.

Of course, there’s plenty of fun to be had in a “dumb home.” “We have had several adult clients wanting to incorporate their hobby of Legos into their design aesthetic,” reveals Allison Smith of Gem + Clay Interiors in St. Augustine, Florida. “I definitely think this speaks to a desire to return to the analog, and that [old-school] element.”

“We always joke about the Tuscan kitchens from 2005, but they had some good things going on,” says Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox. If you strip away the “bottles of preserved peppers and gaudy oil-rubbed bronze faucets,” the style was actually rocking a great color palette. Shades like brown, ochre, terracotta, and deep red bring a little bit of the great outdoors inside, plus they can pair nicely with today’s cozy interiors. But let’s keep the overly Italian influences in the past, shall we?

Today’s emerging design lovers are extra—just ask Scott, who has been noticing a considerable uptick in passementerie. “These intricate details were everywhere in the mid-2000s, before the world decided everything should be sleek and gray,” he says. “They’re becoming popular again, and I think they really evoke the attention to detail we saw at the turn of the century.”

Though yesteryear’s tassels, beading, and decorative trim are undeniably fun, using them requires a little bit of strategy. Since there’s a fine line between charming and kitschy, balance these louder, upholstered moments with pared-back wooden pieces.

Goodbye, panel-ready refrigerators and dishwashers: Katie Kiser says stainless steel appliances are so back. According to the Tennessee-based designer, the resurgence is a break from the picture-perfect kitchens older generations have pined over. “Gen-Z values practicality over perfection,” she explains. “I think it will show up in their interiors.” And why stop at appliances when you can bring that lived-in feel to the rest of the space? “They don’t hate clutter the way millennials do,” she adds. “We’ll see details like cookbooks in the kitchens and hall trees with tennis rackets on them.”

Though Scott hasn’t seen the Venetian Gold granite that ruled Y2K just yet, he’s noticing many homeowners are willing to get creative with their countertops.

“We've moved beyond using only Carrara marble in kitchens,” he explains.

Ditto for quartz, which some designers believe is on the outs. Instead, Scott says travertine is huge again and granite is poised to make the comeback. If you want to keep these materials from appearing dated, he encourages you to reach for stones with bolding veining and inclusions.

#6962. John Madden Deserved Better Than a Messy Amazon Biopic

A movie can have all the makings of a winner and still fumble the ball.

As a movie critic, I’m not in the business of telling professional filmmakers what they could or should do with their art. What an unproductive way to do my job. But sometimes I see something so egregious, and so boneheaded, I’m compelled to scream from my seat, forehead running hot and veins bulging out—like a coach forced to watch his wide receiver telegraph his route to the defense like a GPS in cleats. That’s how I felt watching MADDEN.

Streaming now on Prime Video, right on time for Thanksgiving football, is David O. Russell’s MADDEN. It’s a big streaming snooze where Nicolas Cage dons a fat suit and putzes around in a confused performance that flip-flops on which part of the legendary coach-turned-broadcaster-turned-foot-spray-spokesman’s interiority we’re meant to sympathize with. The real John Madden suffered cognitive decline until his death in 2021, but Cage’s performance is another exhausting exercise of actors playing undiagnosed dementia—and MADDEN has no calcium in its bones to even commit to that.

Devoid of suspense, stakes, or really any answer to the question “Why?” MADDEN is a plotless dud that falls short in its game plan of probing the unbearable weight of a massive legacy. Worst of all, the spine of a fantastic movie is all there. It’s just not in playing shape. To wit: Scrubbed from the trailers is the reason anyone under the age of 35 knows John Madden’s name: video games. (It is my personal conspiracy theory this was done to mitigate losing an audience of boomer granddads, who will fire up the movie on their Roku before passing out on tryptophan.) In 1988, the former Raiders coach who scribbled on screens lent his name to a project by a small tech company called Electronic Arts. Today it’s valued at $55 billion and owned by Saudis. But back then it was just a start-up full of nerds making a football-simulation video game called Madden NFL, which has since morphed into an industry juggernaut that rakes in the equivalent of a small country’s gross domestic product.

The men and women of Madden’s era are obviously unable to fathom computer games as a big deal. (Even now, on the eve of Grand Theft Auto VI, I have heard from people grossly underestimating its economic and cultural power.) In the film is John Mulaney as Trip Hawkins, the founder of EA, whose wiry physique, skinny ties, and plaid button-downs stand in contrast to the movie’s many beefcake extras who exude ’70s-flavored machismo. But Trip’s presence in the film exists to warn the chain-smoking dinosaurs of their coming extinction. Cementing a legacy is not about winning so much that they make a bronze bust and stick you in the Hall of Fame, with the other dead guys. It’s about making your name mean something. No one sees the future until it’s in front of them—you can only hope you can find your way to be part of it somehow. And somehow John Madden did just that. Unfortunately, even MADDEN fails to recognize the awesome potential of its own story.

Russell’s latest work spends the first half cosplaying as a sober sports biopic, in the vein of Moneyball, until it eventually and perhaps inevitably pivots into a low-stakes tech-business success story like BlackBerry or AIR. This alone isn’t a bad thing—frankly, it should be the entire movie—but MADDEN’s failure to synthesize its core themes about how men can actually live forever is what makes the movie such a frustrating experience to sit through.

MADDEN (the movie, all caps) is concerned with legacy. What does it mean? What does it really take to be remembered? For Cage’s Madden, it starts out as a straightforward idea: win Super Bowls. Football runs in his blood and runs his house; early on, Kathryn Hahn, as Madden’s wife, Virginia, scolds her son for rooting for the wrong team. #6959. Design Experts Reveal When It’s Actually OK to Start Decorating for the Holidays

There’s no hard rule—but your HOA may have one.

Now that the Halloween candy bowls are empty, you might find thoughts of tinsel, garland, and fairy lights dancing in your head, but is it really time to trade your pumpkins for holiday trimmings? Some have strong opinions on the topic, half believing the jolly spirit of the holiday season doesn't start until after Thanksgiving, the other rushing to unbox their silver bells as soon as the clock strikes 12 on November 1—but the choice isn’t always yours to make.

If you belong to a Homeowners’ Association (and over 78.1 million Americans do), the question of when to put up your outdoor décor may be dictated by their guidelines. HOAs tend to have rules about holiday decorations, restrictions covering their location (within the boundaries of your yard, of course), sound level (not too loud), safety (no open flames), light color, and when the decorations can be installed. In some communities, you can even be fined for bringing out the twinkle lights and nativity creche before Thanksgiving—this happened to one Florida man in 2002, when he was hit with a fine of $1,000 for putting his lights up on November 6.

If you don’t live in an HOA, you may not get fined for decorating too early, but you will get judged by neighbors who find pre-Thanksgiving holiday displays a little, well, tacky. To be fair, holding until after Thanksgiving does have its perks—why would you pass on the opportunity to decorate a gorgeous fall tablescape? In the spirit of peace on earth and goodwill toward man, we asked design experts what to do. Here’s what they said.

Additional copy by Meghan Shouse and Alyssa Khan.

You might follow a religious tradition that guides your holiday decorating, like placing the Baby Jesus in the nativity on Christmas Day or keeping the tree up until Epiphany. But when it comes to secular etiquette, there are no hard-and-fast rules about when to start (or stop) decking the halls.

“Most people find it tasteful to stick within the weeks or the month of the holiday itself,” points out Lizzie Post, co-president of The Emily Post Institute and author of Emily Post’s Etiquette—The Centennial Edition. “That said, there is no etiquette that dictates it. It’s the same way you can paint your house neon orange with blue stripes—there’s nothing that says you can’t. It’s really, do you want to be the person who does?”

For designer Tan France, the answer to that question is decidedly yes. For the past decade, he and his husband have been decorating their tree on Halloween and taking it down on Valentine’s Day. “I’m British, and we don’t really do Halloween,” he explains. “When I moved here 16 years ago, I was more obsessed with Christmas. So on Halloween day, when everyone else is crafting their costumes, I want to be decorating the tree. And a tree brings my husband and me a lot of joy when we come down and turn the lights on first thing in the morning. It’s just a lovely thing to perk your day up.”

Do people question France about his decision to put up decorations so early, and for so long? “Oh gosh, yeah, everyone asks that,” he says. “Online, the press, neighbors. And my answer is really simple: Why delay joy?”

If you are jonesing to do some holiday decorating, there's good news: While Christmas might be weeks away, there is one happening right now. It’s called Thanksgiving.

“Anytime before midnight of Thanksgiving is too early to decorate,” insists Wendy Kerrigan, founder of Atelier Design in New Orleans. “Filling the house with Christmas decorations too soon really takes away from the beauty of Thanksgiving, moving your mind into the bustle of Christmas.”

Kerrigan wishes more people would celebrate the fall season for longer; rushing to the tinsel takes away from the beauty of the autumn season, “Thanksgiving can be experienced in a special way too, filling the house with fall colors, fall leaves, gourds, and cornucopia,” says Kerrigan. “Instead of trying to hurry the year along, enjoy each family celebration for what it is.”

Even more, sprinting to the Yuletide season robs the holiday of its own magic. “What makes things special when they’re once a year is that they’re experiences you look forward to,” says Vermont-based interior designer Joshua Smith. “How many holidays can we take at one time? Are we doing Black Friday in July now? Let’s just enjoy the season that we’re in!”

When it comes to holiday decorating, many hands make light work. Several designers say they take advantage of the long holiday weekend and enforced family time to rope in some extra help making spirits bright. “In my parents' home, we start decorating right after Thanksgiving dinner. It’s a rare opportunity for everyone to be together, and my siblings and I stay to help out our parents,” says New York-based designer Mikel Welch. “Plus, it’s a much better tradition than terrorizing sales associates on Black Friday Eve!"

Smith has a plan for bringing the Christmas spirit to his home, without shouldering the burden of doing all the hall-decking himself. Last year, his family decorated their trees over Thanksgiving weekend and made it a tree-decorating contest. With his husband's family in town, “they’ll be divided into three teams, decorating three trees, and I’ll go around at the end judging, and everyone will win prizes. We’re not all going to be together at Christmas, so this activity is a fun way to celebrate the season and not do all the work myself,” he says.

Still, early decorators would argue that the amount of effort needed to set up holiday decor is another reason to put up decorations early—and keep them up late. “This is the most miserable time of the year; it gets dark so early, it’s so darn cold. I just think you should do everything you can to bring yourself joy,” says France. “Why put in all that effort for a few weeks?”

Even those who are normally on the side of waiting to decorate agree that desperate times call for dopamine decorating. Whether it’s the state of your inbox or the state of the world that is stressing you out, if you’re feeling a little blue, go right ahead and pull out the red and green.

“In a perfect calendar, the day after Thanksgiving would be my official start to the holiday season—it's traditionally the day when my family goes out into the woods of Tahoe National Forest to chop down our tree, drag it home, and decorate it,” says designer Noz Nozawa of Noz Design. “But this year, the day after Halloween is when I let the holidays start—there's plenty to worry about, and having my nutcrackers and string lights out a few weeks early can only boost spirits.”

Whenever you decorate, it can be a way to bring fun to a not-always joyful world. “We have no control over a lot of things in our lives, but when we walk through the door of our home, we choose to focus our energy and attention on whether that is on fear or on love and joy,” says Smith. “And this holiday season, I think more than ever we have to focus on ways to bring more light to ourselves and to each other.”

When it comes to early decor, “I can handle it from my neighbor; I cannot handle it from a corporation,” says Post. That may be because we know the Christmas displays popping up in a big-box store alongside the Halloween candy are trying to sell us something, while a neighbor decorating is spreading joy, spending their own time and money to brighten the dark winter nights. For a shop, holiday decorating is mercenary. For an individual, it’s a mitzvah.

If you appreciate the joy inspired by holiday displays and do want to start setting them up early or keep them up late, consider finding a middle ground by leaning toward decor that’s more about lighting up the night, rather than marking a particular occasion. “I see a lot of people in Vermont leaving the actual lights up, not so much like blow-up Santas or things like that, but the actual lights up for the entire winter,” says Post.

If you’re a minimalist (or live in a studio apartment), you don’t need a tree to decorate. “If you don’t have a tree, I’m going to invite you to place a hurricane lamp on your coffee table with a wreath of greenery around it and light a candle in the evening,” says Smith. “Because what we know from neuroscience and neuroaesthetics is, you’ve got to engage the senses to nourish the soul!” Light a candle or turn on some twinkle lights, and you’re liable to spark joy as well.

France would argue that even his trees aren’t occasion-specific. “Here’s the thing: I’m Muslim, and my tree and the lights on our house aren’t necessarily for Christmas,” he explains. “It’s to celebrate Thanksgiving, Christmas, and the New Year. This is the most miserable time of the year. It gets dark so early, it gets light so late, it’s so darn cold. I just think do everything you can to bring yourself joy.”

Though you’d never find them rushing to hang their stockings, the designer duo behind the Kansas City-based firm Kobel + Co, Elizabeth Bennett and Mallory Robins, say there’s one design scheme you can always tap into if you find yourself in the class of outliers running to get decor up before Christmas Eve. “Ralph Lauren Holiday isn't just having a moment; it's a classic holiday go-to for so many reasons,” Bennett explains. “Warm crimsons, rich browns, tactile fabrics, and blue-green plaids make a beautiful holiday palette. Layer in antique silver pieces and simple glass ornaments. A little patina and variation keep everything from feeling too matched. The goal is a warm, welcoming home with pieces that feel collected and personal.” After all, you want it to feel like home, even at Christmas.

#6942. How The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping Showcases Effie Trinket’s Fashion Evolution

Costume designer Bina Daigeler shares the inspiration behind the fashion in the new film.

If you follow red carpet fashion, these past few weeks, it’s been impossible to ignore Elle Fanning’s method dressing madness as Effie Trinket. From toy ballerina music box headpieces by Robert Wun fit for a fairy tale to makeup compact dresses (and matching headwear) from Givenchy, the fashion story is strong. All roads lead back to her character as young Effie Trinket from The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, which is making its theater debut on November 20. Based on Suzanne Collins’s 2025 novel of the same name, it is set 24 years before The Hunger Games and 40 years after The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes. It begins the morning of the 50th Hunger Games, with a 16-year-old Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) and Effie Trinket as a young stylist in the Capitol.

Bina Daigeler is the costume designer behind the new film, and with her team, she oversaw the creation of every single outfit from scratch, with a few helping accessories pulled from vintage specialists. She began with a mood board that focused on the aesthetic differences between the 13 districts while keeping in mind the personalities of the actors. Drusilla Sickle (Glenn Close) had a devoted board full of Alexander McQueen, John Galliano, and Vivienne Westwood, for instance. “It was really like old couture fashion,” Daigeler tells ELLE. “But pieces that, for me, would age very well, you know, that would keep up in time, because I think that is very important when you work on such a famous franchise; that you design for the future. That it’s still interesting in 10 years.”

“Working with Glenn Close, she really came to me and said, ‘I need to find a thread through all the costumes that are made for me, so that I can understand it,’” says Daigeler. “That was super important for her.” Daigeler wove everything together with “cool accessories to give her the strength,” and one very special finale look. “She wanted to have a big finale, you know, like a finale where she really feels like ‘I’m the winner. it’s nothing about Haymitch, it's all about me.’” Think: lots of red.

Daigeler also referenced Woodstock, Studio 54, the late ’60s and early ’70s. “I tried really to have a strong concept that has to do with the personality of each character,” she says. Unsurprisingly, given Effie Trinket’s predilection for fashion as a stylist, she was a big focus for Daigeler. “For example, young Effie Trinket, how did she become the Effie Trinket that Elizabeth Banks was? Here she is younger, she is fresh, she doesn’t have a lot of money or a fashion background. So it must be something that looked a little bit vintage that could be done at home, a mixture of fabrics, of textures, of color, something very young and fresh and fun.”

Early on in the film, we see Effie Trinket’s fashion as improvisational. But Daigeler intentionally showcases her ascension from fashion fan to fashion icon throughout the film. Daigeler says that Effie’s outfits all had to have fun elements. “The black kimono with the flowers and patchwork little waistcoat with a flowery blouse and all the color combinations were very strange, but it worked together,” she says. She also mentions her blue denim, Vivienne Westwood-inspired piece with huge sleeves as being the epitome of young Effie Trinket style.

“The most important thing was, for Effie, that she looks fun, but also free, because she is in the Capitol, but is still the freest and the most positive and optimistic character,” says Daigeler. “She doesn’t look like damaged and depressed and she had to come in and cheer them up and she definitely came with a solution. Our heroes somehow could trust her and they were so happy to see her. All the other characters from the Capitol, you really don’t know who you can trust.” Therefore, their aesthetic is darker while Effie is lighter and brighter.

Look out for the fashion Easter eggs throughout the film, like Effie Trinket’s butterfly crystal rings, which were just some of the pieces that Daigeler sourced vintage from one of her favorite specialists from around the world. Elsewhere, Haymitch has his own fashion revolution in a quieter way. “The way also how Haymitch wears the clothes and how they were aged and the colors, everything just comes together,” says Daigeler. “I think the audience will find, or I hope, that they find that there are things that resemble and they recognize it.”

Likewise for Caesar Flickerman (Kieran Culkin), the longtime Capitol television host, there’s an emphasis on fashionable suits. “It was very, very important that he can move because he is a very physical actor whom moves a lot, so he needed space in his suits,” says Daigeler. “Suits also had to give him power.”

And for Snow (Ralph Fiennes), too, suiting is used to convey power. “He’s very particular about fabrics, about the cut. So I went to a tailor in London, who I have known for many, many years,” she says. “Snow’s suits we developed really like a little bit, World War II military cuts, with a very strong shoulder and a very strong silhouette and these heavy fabrics, and the right colors. But then we gave him the fantasy through hand-embroidered lapels. It had also the hint to his rose that he always wears, and he has somehow always loved flowers.”

Fashion is both spectacle and a survival tool in The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping. The odds are forever in fashion’s favor.

Not the white box look popular with luxury new builds on the island, but instead something reminiscent of the “old St. Bart’s,” says East Hampton-based landscape designer Edwina von Gal, who, like Ries, has collaborated with the clients on other projects.

A Caribbean island is a challenging place to work under any circumstances. Everything has to be brought in from far away, including all the plants, which came by barge from Miami. St. Bart’s in particular requires anyone building to work with a local architect and engineer. In their initial proposal, this French team defaulted to that “slick and modernist” look, as von Gal describes it, with the oversized bedrooms and white color scheme that dominates much of the island’s luxury housing stock.

The design team insisted on cladding the buildings in Ipe, the durable tropical hardwood, which wears well. It mystified the local architects and set the complex apart from its neighbors. It also makes the buildings disappear into the greenery of the hillside.

But the architects quickly caught on to the new plans, and von Gal is eager to praise their technical accomplishments. #6916. This Unexpected French Antique Is Suddenly Making a Comeback, and You’ve Probably Seen It Before

The desire to preserve mementos from a wedding day transcends centuries and cultures. Today's brides still hang onto their wedding dresses, dry their bouquets, and spend a large portion of their budgets on photography. But in 19th- and 20th-century France, the art of preserving wedding-day memories took the form of globes de mariée, also known as marriage globes.

These ornate, shrine-like displays primarily showcased the bride's head crown and other keepsakes from the big day, and the additional pieces inside symbolized the couple's dreams for their life together. The cloche protected these special items from dust back then and still today.

Here's a closer look at the history and allure of globes de mariée, plus tips for sourcing and styling these gorgeous antiques.

Chris Featherston is the senior vice president of product acquisition at Austin Auction Gallery, and he and his family have been dealing in French antiques for more than 30 years. In the last couple of years alone, they've sold more than 150 globes de Mariée for an average of $400 to $600 each.

But this definitely wasn't always the case. Featherstone notes that the demand for these antiques has risen significantly in the past five years alone.

"In the early days of our business, most of what we sold came from house clearances in France," he recalls. "These globes were ubiquitous. At that time, we felt lucky to be able to sell them for $30 to $40 each."

Anyone with a strong appreciation for historic relics can see the appeal of these antiques. For instance, Mick and Paige Whitcomb have been collecting globes de mariée for nearly a decade, and they currently own 15. Their interest in these types of treasures is reflected in their careers. Mick creates sculptural lighting from architectural and industrial salvage dating back to the 1800s through his business, Artifacts by Nomad. Paige is a tintype photographer who captures staged scenes featuring antiques from the 1800s (including her globes de mariée) using a 19th-century large-format studio camera.

"While traveling in France for a wedding anniversary, we were first exposed to globes de mariée at the Marché aux Puces antique market in Paris," Mick says. "While we were initially caught by the beauty of the objects, after hearing their history and connection to French marriages, it felt like a beautiful way of commemorating our anniversary and trip to Europe."

Due to the highly custom nature of globes de marriée, each one is a unique find. However, Featherston notes that most contain a few similar components. For starters, the bride's headpiece often rests on a red cushion, and it typically shows off delicate beeswax orange blossoms that symbolize purity and virginity.

Most also contain small mirrors, which carried different meanings depending on the shape. For example, diamond-shaped mirrors symbolize how many children the couple wished to have, and rectangular mirrors stood for the length of time the couple knew each other before their engagement.

You'll also often find a wreath embellished with brass ornaments, usually flowers, birds and tree leaves. These symbolize love, a long-lasting marriage, and happiness.

"The globes de marriées integrate beautiful symbolism in every piece, and I find them fascinating," Paige says. "Each globe is so distinctive, and tells the story of the couple that created it. You can surmise the couple's hopes and dreams by minutiae tucked away behind the dome for safekeeping. I find very intimate details incorporated into the displays, such as a prayer card of a favorite saint, to photos of the bride and groom. I have even found hair and teeth, transforming the antique into a memento mori as well."

Mick has noticed that sourcing these antiques is considerably more difficult in the United States than in Europe. But with a little patience and determination, it is possible.

"Because of the glass domes, I think fewer importers are brave enough to bring them over," he says. "Despite this, we have found the occasional gem in the wild." In the States, he's had the most luck sourcing them from design-forward antique dealers, online auction sites and even prop houses that have just wrapped up a period film project.

Since there is a constant demand for these pieces, Featherston tries to feature several in every sale at Austin Auction Gallery.

Globes de mariée can add a formal and traditional flair to any space, and they even mix well with more modern accents.

"One of my favorite ways to showcase the globes de mariée is to mix them with a piece of contemporary art," Paige says. "I love to create a harmony between our collections of antiques and artwork from artists that are living and working today."

For example, Mick and Paige have styled two of their favorite globes de mariée with the piece "The Gardener" by working artist L.J. Eidolon. "I find collecting modern works that have a historic feeling to them helps create balance between the multiple centuries displayed together in one scene," Paige says.

#6879. One Martini to Rule Them All

The case for ordering a vodka martini, always.

At a dinner a few years ago, my fiancé’s mother, an elegant former Wall Street banker, ordered a vodka martini, dry, with three olives. I grew up in New Orleans, where I was weaned on sickeningly sweet, sticky cocktails, but I was always fascinated by the martini’s sophistication. I’d seen the drink in the hands of characters like Roger Sterling from Mad Men, who would expertly balance his V-shaped glass as he dropped a cynical witticism. So that night, I told the waiter to make it two. Ever since then, I have favored vodka martinis over gin, the base spirit for the by-the-books recipe.

I’m not the only one. Bartenders in New York have started to notice a trend away from gin. Chris Amirault of Bar Chimera in Manhattan says that he makes four vodka martinis for every one gin martini. “Vodka pays the bills, and it’s what a lot of people prefer,” says Brian Evans of the Lobby Bar at the Hotel Chelsea, also in Manhattan.

For those who insist that a true martini must only be made with gin, Tristan Stephenson, host of the Curious Bartender podcast, says, “History isn’t on the side of purists. The martini has been a moving target since the 1880s. It started sweeter, with maraschino, orange curaçao, and sugar syrup making appearances. Then it dried out over decades, then had the vermouth all but exorcised from it. Then came olive brine and everything since.”

Why do many martini drinkers prefer vodka? Ti Adelaide Martin, coproprietor of New Orleans staple Commander’s Palace, succinctly explains one reason: “Vodka is the more approachable spirit.” Unlike gin, whose botanicals impart noticeable flavors to a martini, vodka doesn’t overpower other ingredients. It’s more of a blank canvas, which is why it’s used for variations like the espresso martini, which is now the fifth-most-ordered drink at bars, according to Drinks International. Instead of strong flavor, vodka provides “texture, weight, and a kind of icy austerity that can be genuinely beautiful when the vodka is good and the drink is properly cold,” says Stephenson.

And, let’s be honest, we also drink vodka martinis for the booze. “Vodka tastes bracing,” says Esquire editor in chief Michael Sebastian. “I want to take a sip of my martini and get the feeling that it’s strong and it’s cold, and it goes through your whole body and you feel better and loose immediately.”

Now I order my vodka martini with a splash of vermouth, just dirty enough to make me thirsty, and shaken so that thin ice shards float on top. You might prefer yours vermouth forward, citrus focused, or dirty as swamp water. It’s all fair game. And that’s the beauty of a vodka martini: It’s a personal drink. My future mother-in-law has her order, and I have mine. What’s yours going to be?

So it’s hard to actually call 2026 a great year in gaming when it’s also been a year of heartbreak and uncertainty for the future.

But the games are still great. Even just a few hours into GTA VI, it’s obvious that it’s a massive achievement for Rockstar Games that’s well worth the wait. And just this past month, Nintendo dropped a remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time that features beautifully upgraded graphics from the original 1998 classic. Elsewhere, Resident Evil Requiem nailed the franchise’s decades-long identity split with a game that’s half third-person action and half first-person survival horror. Plus, action games like 007 First Light, Control Resonant, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword let loose the power fantasies we’ve only dreamed of.

But it’s not all blood and guts and guns and swords. Earlier this year, the coming-of-age Mixtape caused a ruckus among gaming circles, though anyone who’s played it and loved it will tell you just how much it’s a love letter to bygone summer days. So, unless you’re already dedicated to spending all of Thanksgiving and Christmas in GTA VI’s Vice City, some of the best games Esquire’s editors have played through in 2026 so far.

GTA VI hadn’t even been out for twenty-four hours yet and critics were already calling it a masterpiece. To be fair, video game critics call a lot of titles masterpieces, but it was clear from everyone’s immediate reaction to Vice City that Rockstar Games spent the last decade making something truly special. Sure, there are hundreds of hours of side quests and town-foolery to get lost in, but GTA VI also features a Bonnie & Clyde-esque love story between its two playable protagonists—Jason and Lucia—which breaks up the beloved smash-and-grab chaos of the Grand Theft Auto franchise and makes the gamer question just where the point between a video game and real life begins.

At its most basic, Nintendo’s The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake isn’t all that different from the classic, 1998 original. The game certainly looks a lot better on the Nintendo Switch 2 than it did on the Nintendo 64, now that the polygonal characters and sharp outdoors have been converted to more living and breathing graphics. But at the end of the day, you’re still playing the same game that you played in 1998. Don’t take that as a knock, though! Ocarina of Time still tops “Best Video Games of All Time” lists for a reason.

There are times playing Star Wars: Galactic Racer when you will feel like you are going too fast. And yes, the rate at which it will feel like that will shock you. But that’s pod racing! And better than any racing sim, let alone a Star Wars video game with voice acting as good as Galactic Racer, it truly feels like you’re speeding around in a hunk-a-junk that you just cobbled together with space garbage and jet engines.

Following up Fire Emblem: Three Houses was already a tall task, let alone having to follow Fire Emblem: Engage. Both titles combined the best of Japanese RPGs with the new slice-of-life school relationship activities introduced in Three Houses. And now Fortune’s Weave, something of a trilogy-capper for the series, is the most maximalist entry yet. The Blood of Dawnwalker is the debut game from Rebel Wolves, a new studio started by former creatives from The Witcher 3: Blood Hunt. So if Dawnwalker feels eerily like a Witcher game, you’re not crazy. That being said, the vampire game’s controversial timer—which forces players to make quick decisions with big impacts on gameplay—fully separated the RPG and proved that the new studio was ready to try out exciting ideas all its own.

Remedy’s Alan Wake 2 topped Esquire’s Best Video Games of 2023 list three years ago, so you can bet we were excited for the studio’s next game, Control Resonant. Rock out with your claws out. Insomniac applies its winning formula for triple-A superhero games to flesh out the most popular X-Men member of all time. The five-foot-four berserker stars in his own solo adventure in a universe where mutants live in secret. While piecing together his own memories, Wolverine meets a telepath named Jean Grey. A hyper-violent tour through some of its title antihero’s classic comics, Wolverine doesn’t quite reach the same heights as Spider-Man, but the way it lets players truly feel like “The Best There Is” is gold.

If you could rewind to your last day of school, what would it look like? Who are you with? And most importantly, what’s on the stereo? Essentially a reboot of the series (whose last title was released all the way back in 2006 for the PlayStation 2), Way of the Sword follows legendary swordsman Miyamoto Musashi as he comes into possession of the Oni Gauntlet and must cleanse the world of a supernatural evil. While not quite a Souls-like, the game offers an adequately complex combat system that ensures sword slashing never gets stale. And slicing demons in half feels oh so satisfying.

With the release of No More Heroes III in 2021, Grasshopper Manufacture finally put the saga of Travis Touchdown to rest and kicked off its next era with Romeo Is a Dead Man. Set in an island town overrun with monsters and mysterious creatures, Reanimal follows a nameless brother and sister who must survive the island’s horrors if they want to save their friends. With a story as cryptic as some of its puzzles, Reanimal carries the baton of other memorable platformers like Limbo and Little Nightmares to deliver an otherworldly experience. Although Reanimal can be played alone, it is a journey best taken with someone by your side.

Video games are all about rewarding the player for progression, and there may be no better equivalent to this feeling in the real world than rock climbing. If you thought Doom had too many pixelated demons and not enough rubber-hose whimsy, Mouse: P.I. for Hire is your game. #6857. Take Note: This Is How Cool People Are Decorating for Thanksgiving This Year

These tastemakers are bringing their personal style to the holiday.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pumpkin centerpieces, rustic garlands, dried-fruit wreaths, and cornucopias are classic Thanksgiving decorations. They’re timeless and beloved, but they can also feel predictable. Traditional decor has its place, of course, but Thanksgiving tablescapes—and dinner parties in general—are evolving, much like interiors, to feel more personal, lived-in, and layered. So we asked nine tastemakers and experts how they’re decorating for this family-centric holiday.

From traditional touches to wholly unexpected looks, read on to see how they’re making their homes feel festive this autumn, and maybe you’ll be inspired to try something new this year.

Ever since designer Mikel Welch moved to sunny California, his family has never hesitated to go to his for Thanksgiving to escape the chilly Michigan weather. It’s become a tradition to gather at his house, and similarly to how he grew up, Welch likes to keep his decorations for the holiday understated and warm.

“I love getting the exterior of my house into full fall mode with mums, pumpkins, and seasonal greenery,” Welch tells House Beautiful. “The beauty of fall decorating is that it can carry you from October straight through Thanksgiving without having to change much.”

The designer’s house is filled with dark, moody colors and earthy textures year-round, so he focuses mainly on bringing natural elements inside to do the work for him. “I also have a little tradition with my dad,” he adds. “For the past couple of years, we've made trips to the nursery together to pick out flowers, greenery, and sometimes bales of hay for the front of the house. It's become something I really enjoy doing with him.”

“Thanksgiving has always been a big family affair,” real estate expert and maximalism extraordinaire Harvin Greene says. She’s happy to keep that tradition going as an adult, just in her own way. In the past, Greene’s aunt and uncle would have everyone at their house for the big meal, but last year, they passed the hosting baton to Greene for the first time. “I may have gotten a little carried away...in the best possible way.”

Getting to decorate two tables—one for the “adults,” another for the “kids,” even though they’re technically all grown-ups, too—Greene used this opportunity to design two completely different tablescapes. “I grew up surrounded by women who understood that a beautifully set table and a genuinely good time should go hand in hand,” she adds.

The one in the formal dining space (i.e., the “adults’” table) was theatrical and dramatic, and nearly everything on it had some sort of familial connection, from her parents’ engraved goblets to her husband’s grandmother’s sterling flatware. The kids’ table, on the other hand, was whimsical and vibrant, complete with a yellow patterned tablecloth, colorful glassware, and playful animal candlesticks. “I don't feel the need to follow the traditional Thanksgiving color palette,” Greene says. “Who decided Thanksgiving had to be orange and brown? Yellow, pink, turquoise, and green can be just as festive.”

The holiday isn’t over until they bring out the Bingo cards, a spirited Thanksgiving tradition that Greene’s Aunt Jean started—which involves cash prizes. “It's one of those family traditions that makes the holiday feel like ours, no matter whose house we're celebrating in.”

Talia Hubble is no stranger to a good dinner party—much of her content actually centers around her love of hosting. But Thanksgiving is a bit extra special for the Rhode Islander, especially since it’s being held at her cottage this year. “Growing up, it was just me and my mom, and she was too busy to decorate,” Hubble says. “I think that’s part of why I’ve fallen in love with seasonal decorating as an adult, I get to bring all my cozy childhood decorating dreams to life!”

As soon as the calendar switches from October to November, Hubble starts decorating for fall, opting for an eclectic mix of subtle Christmas decor (think bows, garlands, and wreaths), an excessive amount of taper candles, and a traditional Thanksgiving palette of oranges, browns, greens, and reds. Every year looks a little bit different, but there are pieces in her collection that are simply too cute not to bring out every holiday season.

“Half the fun of pulling everything out for Thanksgiving is remembering where it came from,” she says. “I think the best decor is a culmination of pieces you’ve been collecting for years, and the memories behind those pieces are what make the table feel special and uniquely yours.”

By the time she’s done decorating, her home feels like a warm, welcoming escape, somewhere her guests can exhale. “My philosophy is that you can’t go wrong when you aim for cozy, colorful, and embrace a little organized mess,” Hubble adds. “My favorite Thanksgiving tables are the ones that look even better halfway through dinner, when the napkins are rumpled and everyone’s reaching for seconds.”

Chef and food stylist Romilly Newman can’t always count on being in the same place as her family for Thanksgiving since everyone’s scattered about, but she always makes a point to throw an autumnal dinner with friends around the holiday time, complete with beautiful fall decor.

Her mom never decorated their home while she was growing up with specific Thanksgiving decorations—instead, she would decorate with deep orange and red flowers to capture the fall vibe. Newman’s style as an adult is similar. “I am fall-obsessed, so as soon as the temperature drops from summer, I'm setting up branches, candles, and darker accents,” she says. “I love the atmosphere inside and outside around Thanksgiving time, and I try my best to bottle that up in my home.”

Natural thicker linen, pops of silver, dark florals, and moody lighting envelop her space as the chef relies mostly on candlelight and organic elements—like figs, sage, chestnuts, and mandarins—as the decorative accents.

“Thanksgiving is great because it brings family together and offers time to enjoy food, friends, and being at home,” Newman adds. “Whatever your style is, try to create an environment that allows for great conversation and a festive evening. You want everyone to be comfortable and feel the holiday spirit.”

When writer Olivia Noceda was a child, she would stay up late the night before Thanksgiving and design custom menus for everyone on Microsoft Paint, which she would then print out, frame, and place on each table setting in preparation for the big meal. The holiday was a big deal for Noceda’s mother, who would always put on the most beautiful Thanksgiving gatherings. “I think that's where my love for hosting and setting a beautiful table really started,” she explains.

However, for the last six years, Noceda has been having Thanksgiving with her husband’s side of the family, so when she does host around the holiday, it’s typically for Friendsgiving. “I don't necessarily pick a theme, but I do like everything to feel cohesive,” she explains of her decorating process. “I usually start incorporating little autumnal touches sometime in October and let them carry me through Thanksgiving.”

The writer and recipe developer lets the season guide her, building her tablescape and decor around a beautiful branch from the farmers’ market, a bowl of pears or persimmons, a collection of little pumpkins and squash, or a few taper candles on the dining room table. Ingredients play a big part in her decor—she can even take a fresh loaf of bread and make it feel special on her table.

For the rest of her decor, she likes to incorporate vintage serving pieces, everyday elements, and elevated details like antique silver and linen napkins with things she already owns to lean into that lived-in, warm feeling. The last thing she wants is her home to feel like a department store display. “Nothing needs to match perfectly, and I actually prefer when it doesn't,” Noceda adds.

What entrepreneur and Real Housewives of New York star Erin Lichy remembers best about Thanksgivings growing up is the smell. “We always spent Thanksgiving out east, and the house was full of pumpkins, local hay, and lanterns,” she explains. The scent of that fresh produce, mixed with her grandmother’s homemade turkey t’bit, has shaped the way she decorates for and hosts Thanksgiving. “That coveted and highly personal (and incredibly delicious) recipe is in my cookbook, She’s a Host, and it’s part of every Thanksgiving I host.”

Decorating starts in the kitchen for her family. Lichy wants the home to smell like fall, so she focuses on that sense, making seasonal recipes with squash and pumpkin and incorporating autumnal flowers in her interiors. The melding of those scents gives her townhouse a warm feeling, especially when mixed with layered fall colors and the crackling of the fireplace.

Ever since Lichy and her family have moved into their townhouse, she’s been obsessed with decorating her stoop too. “The stoop is the first thing everyone sees, so this year I decided to bring in Simon Wallace Design to make it really stand out!” Lichy adds. “They completely transformed it with layers of seasonal color and texture, and it feels so warm and welcoming.” She specifically loves using the faux garlands and pinecones, as well as lanterns, from Balsam Hill and Frontgate because they’re so beautifully made that they look real, and she’s able to reuse them year after year.

No matter what she does to decorate though, she just wants her home to feel warm and lived-in, and this is the season and holiday to really lean into that.

Decorating for the holidays is Gabby Amato’s version of a self-care day. “It's truly one of my favorite days of the year,” she says. “I carve out a weekend afternoon when my husband and son are out of the house, light my favorite fall candles, turn on some music, and have the house entirely to myself.”

Amato, the founder of Table Two Studio, a paper plate brand which specializes in beautiful, artistic designs that you definitely won’t want to throw away, credits her love of Thanksgiving and the fall season in general to her mother. While growing up in the ‘90s, she saw her mom put so much care and effort into the holiday decorations, always making their home feel warm, nostalgic, and full of spirit. “For Thanksgiving, she decorated our mantel with gourds, greenery, and ceramic pilgrim statues that were a gift from my grandmother,” Amato says. “We had oversized cornstalks and pumpkins at the front door. Nothing ever felt overly styled or precious.”

It’s this feeling that has stuck with her throughout her life, and it’s what she hopes she captures in her own home every year. She begins decorating for the fall early, right after Labor Day, pulling out old pieces and unboxing anything new and arranging it all in fresh ways so nothing feels stale. “I love beautiful decor, of course, but I'm much more interested in creating an atmosphere than achieving a particular aesthetic,” she adds. “I want my home to feel warm, lived-in, a little nostalgic, and festive.”

Donna Lennard’s family was never one for holiday dressing—most of the attention at Thanksgiving fell on the delicious food shared with family. As a restauranteur and cofounder of beloved NYC establishment il Buco, it makes sense why the meal remains to be the most important thing to Lennard even today.

“Clearly, the table is always festive, whether it’s the tablecloth, the napkins, or linens that reflect the season in burnt orange and the like,” she explains. “I love to decorate a table with assorted colorful gourds and seasonal florals, beautiful candlelight (our beeswax candles already have an orange glow), orange ranunculus, and the occasional dried floral.” Colorful Pienza glassware dots the table and her plethora of beautiful tableware gives it more character, but she keeps everything else relatively simple to ensure the food remains the center of attention.

Though Lennard doesn’t often host her family and friends for the Thanksgiving meal, she’s already looking forward to next year when everyone will finally gather at her beach house.

Being from Australia, Kendall Knox—the cofounder of Olive Ateliers along with her husband—didn’t have a personal reference of what the holiday “should” be or look like. This gives her the freedom of deciding what it is for her family. “What it has become is an intimate table: my husband Ben, our son Rhodes, and Ben's mum,” she says. “No rotation, no kids' table, no forty-pound bird. Just the four of us and a dinner that runs long because nobody is in a hurry to leave it.”

Since Knox isn’t beholden to the classic Thanksgiving decor, she tends to keep her home and her table rather simple, “shopping the house,” as she puts it to decorate instead of buying something new. She keeps a rather neutral palette of rich browns, creamy whites, terracotta, and silver, then uses antique linens, mismatched silverware, and candlesticks that have collected wax to capture the true lived-in feel.

Her focus tends to be on the table and the meal, letting the food add more color. If a tablecloth has a wine ring on it from the year before, all the better—it complements the stacked plates at the end of the meal.

“The objects that come out every year, that get a little more marked each time, are the ones that end up meaning the most,” Knox adds. “Thanksgiving is just a very good excuse to use them.”
