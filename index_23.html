#6923. Claude Intros Sonnet 5.5 with Model-Switching Cybersecurity Safeguards

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

#6913. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6908. SailPoint Intros New Capabilities to Address Agentic AI Identity Security Challenge

AI agents are moving from experiments into everyday enterprise environments, but the security controls around them are not keeping pace, according to enterprise identity security company SailPoint. In its latest Horizons of Identity Security report, SailPoint found that 79% of enterprises already have AI agents running in production, while just 2% have deployed identity security tools specifically designed to govern them.

That gap could become harder to ignore as organizations give agents more autonomy. Agents can increasingly access applications, sensitive data, and credentials, and in some cases take actions on a user's behalf, effectively creating a new class of identity that IT and security teams need to manage.

SailPoint, which develops identity security and access governance technology for enterprises, is responding with a collection of new identity security capabilities announced at its Navigate 2026 conference. The company is expanding its Agentic Fabric to discover "shadow AI" agents and provide runtime authorization and controls capable of immediately disabling an agent.

The company also introduced what it calls SailPoint Autonomous Agents, specialized AI agents designed to govern other agents. The security agents can monitor runtime activity, intercept threats, and adjust permissions while allowing legitimate agents to continue operating, SailPoint said.

The idea reflects one of the challenges created by increasingly autonomous AI systems: Traditional human approval processes may not be fast enough to govern software capable of taking large numbers of actions on its own.

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. Organizations can then revoke access, disable an identity or AI agent, assign an owner or automate enforcement.

Just-in-Time provisioning is another part of the strategy. Rather than giving people, service accounts, and AI agents permanent access, permissions can be granted when needed and subjected to conditions such as reauthentication or a business justification.

Capabilities across SailPoint's Agentic Fabric, Human Fabric, and Atlas platforms are beginning to roll out globally. A-ISPM and several other announced features are expected in the company's fourth quarter of fiscal 2027.

"Autonomous agents are no longer just experimental copilots; they are becoming the primary execution layer of modern enterprises," SailPoint President Matt Mills said.

#6902. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

Cybersecurity concerns were more common among organizations with mature AI programs.

Half of those reporting established AI returns identified cybersecurity as a barrier, compared with roughly one-third of those still experimenting.

Overall, 86% of respondents said their organizations are adapting their cybersecurity operating models to address AI-accelerated threats.

The report identifies accountability, operational resilience, and cost management as priorities as AI becomes more embedded in business operations.

"Many organizations have made AI models widely available and track their use," said Swaminathan Chandrasekaran, head of the US AI Center of Excellence at KPMG. "But consumption alone is not a strategy."

#6898. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

#6790. Is KATSEYE Following the Rules of K-Pop?

Fans are worried by Megan’s decision to perform in the face of a concussion, but maybe the debate is about more than one group, but about an entire industry (or two).

Online debates about celebrity work culture sparked when KATSEYE’s Megan Skindiel, who is recovering from a concussion, recently performed seated and in sunglasses during a show in Austin on the group’s WILDWORLD Tour. The next night in Dallas, video surfaced of the artist taking part in the group’s intense dance choreography, while wearing what many speculate to be a heart monitor.

Fans expressed grave concern for her well-being, questioning whether she should be performing at all. While it is important to note that we don’t actually know whether it was Megan’s own decision to perform, many still accused KATSEYE’s label, HYBE x Geffen Records, of potentially overworking their artists at the expense of their health. The label is a partnership between the South Korean entertainment company HYBE (home to groups like BTS, Illit, TXT, and Seventeen) and the American label Geffen Records (home to artists such as Olivia Rodrigo, Selena Gomez, Lady Gaga, and Lana Del Rey).

And there’s another relevant context: With Manon and Sophia already on hiatus, Megan’s absence would leave the group with just three members, only half their original formation of six, and for that reason the group had already postponed three shows. As of now, neither the label nor the group has publicly addressed the criticism.

For longtime K-pop fans, none of this is entirely shocking. Those who follow the industry have most likely already witnessed their idols push through panic attacks, fainting spells, extreme sleep deprivation, injuries from falls, and many other mental and physical struggles in order to give their fans the concert experience they were promised. But for many Western fans, this may be the first look at the grueling expectations that the K-pop world has for its artists, which helps explain the intense concern in their reactions.

KATSEYE is an especially interesting case for us to explore this friction. The group was formed through Dream Academy, a survival competition run by HYBE and Geffen Records, where the finalists trained mainly in Los Angeles, with trips to HYBE’s headquarters in Seoul. While KATSEYE’s music is mostly in English, the group promotes in both the U.S. and South Korea, exemplifying the group’s unique positioning between the two music industries, each with its own set of rules and ideas of what a pop career demands.

Commercially, that meeting point keeps getting closer, with the dramatic rise of K-pop’s popularity and its growing presence in mainstream Western media. And that cross-cultural exchange is reflected in the music itself: English words increasingly make up the majority of the lyrics in K-pop songs, and many labels are now intentionally debuting members who are proficient in English.

It also helps to compare how music careers get built, starting with how the artists are even first discovered by the world. Many of the West’s biggest stars began as those who were organically noticed: Justin Bieber was discovered through cover videos on YouTube, and Taylor Swift was spotted as a teenager playing Nashville’s Bluebird Cafe. K-pop runs on a completely different model: one where the company trains the individual into being an artist through a strict, meticulous formula within a larger system of strategic control.

Those who make it through the company’s insanely competitive rounds of auditions will then spend years as trainees with no guarantee of debuting. Those years typically involve regular evaluations, private lessons, as well as sometimes group living for management and surveillance purposes. Trainees often agree to be prescribed diet plans and sometimes even cosmetic surgery, as well as close monitoring of their private lives including harsh restrictions on dating and phone use to ensure that their status as trainees remains a secret. And what may be even more jarring for those new to this culture is that many of these trainees are often as young as 13, with more and more labels debuting their idols before they are even close to being legal adults.

In exchange comes a carefully engineered shot at success, with executives and experts calibrating the exact combination of members, visual concept, songs, and more to optimize audience appeal. Audiences got a glimpse of this process during Dream Academy, though in a more Westernized version. Whatever ethical questions this process raises, it works, at least commercially. All the biggest K-pop groups have come out of this exact system, and the labels continue to stay closely involved throughout their careers.

But the label isn’t the only relationship that heavily shapes a K-pop artist’s life. There’s also the one with the fans. Western audiences are no strangers to fan culture, from Beatlemania to Swifties, and plenty of domestic K-pop fandoms have a U.S. counterpart. However, one notable cultural difference is one of degree. Take whatever you’ve seen from American fandoms and multiply it by a hundred, and you start to approach the level of devotion an average K-pop stan extends to their bias, or favorite member. They follow idols to concert after concert and every public appearance in between, and they also work together, using step-by-step guides to stream songs in ways that send them up the charts, sometimes even outperforming many mainstream Western artists on global charts like the Billboard Hot 100.

And that kind of devotion comes with expectations. Many groups have dedicated fan-chatting apps, such as HYBE’s Weverse and the subscription-based Bubble, where artists can post and chat with fans almost directly, and fans come to expect regular contact through this service. Idols are also expected to keep up a cheerful image, to avoid canceling a show that fans paid for at all costs, and to immediately apologize when they fall short, even when the reason is an injury.

Public dating, meanwhile, has long been treated as a career-threatening betrayal for these artists. In 2024, after aespa’s Karina was revealed to be dating an actor, she faced a wave of backlash, causing her to post a handwritten apology to fans. Norms have loosened for established stars, but newcomers, or what the industry calls shin-ins, are discouraged, or often barred from it, by both their companies and their fans.

Because they’re still largely construed as Western artists, KATSEYE is, to a large degree, exempt from these rules. For example, members have been comparatively open about their dating lives—a luxury a Korea-based group would most likely not be afforded. But KATSEYE is still managed by a K-pop company, trained by a K-pop system. It wouldn’t be surprising that some of that ethos of always putting the fans first is internalized within the group’s own decision-making, shaping how much they feel they owe their fans in ways a Western artist might not.

From a Western perspective, it makes sense why all of this might feel like an oppressive system that needs reform, or even dismantling. And the criticism exists internally as well: On November 16, South Korea’s Culture Ministry officially announced the launch of a comprehensive survey to review concerns over young performers’ working conditions following the NewJeans controversy. However, one could argue that Western criticism can often be translated by domestic fans as a kind of white-savior complex, with “outsiders” interfering in the methods and systems that have existed without their attention for decades, while deciding it must assimilate to their terms and standards. Especially when many of these critics lack the necessary understanding of the complicated nuances of how K-pop’s cultural specificity largely reflects South Korea’s distinct historical, political, and socioeconomic landscape—one that dramatically differs from the U.S.

These aspects of K-pop are deeply normalized within the industry itself: Trainee systems are shown openly in documentaries and TV series, including ones the companies themselves help produce, and artists often talk about those years candidly, suggesting that many of them may not see these practices as the problem outsiders do. And maybe there is also a truth in the idea that K-pop’s impressive commercial success is partly a product of the very controls critics object to, including the symbiotic relationships between artists and their labels, and between artists and their fans. Without that culture, we would probably not have what we now know to be BLACKPINK or BTS.

None of this makes the overworking of artists acceptable, nor does it erase much of the valid concern so many people feel. We are allowed to worry when our favorite artist seems to be pushed too far, whether it’s by their own drive, their label’s instructions, or by the weight of fans’ expectations. And we’re allowed to question the very systems that make that possible—in K-pop, in Hollywood, or anywhere else.

But it’s also worth asking what it might mean for a system with decades of independent history—one that grew largely outside Western norms—to navigate a global spotlight (and the equally globalized standards that come with it). Where does cultural context end and basic safety begin? And who gets to decide what “better” looks like, if not the artists living it?

Stephanie Wenk, creative director of Sauer.

The anniversary collection is a testament to that philosophy. Drawing on an extraordinary archive of gemstones, some of which have been tucked away for decades, Wenk brings new life to Brazilian imperial topaz, aquamarines, and other precious stones through unexpected combinations. It’s an approach that feels true to the house’s history while pushing its creative boundaries. But beyond the jewelry itself, Sauer’s story is one of family, discovery, and a deep connection to Brazil. From Jules’s early adventures to the house’s growing international presence, each chapter has been shaped by a love of experimentation and embracing the unexpected. As the brand celebrates this milestone, Wenk and Sauer share how they’re staying true to the house’s original spirit.

The cover of Sauer: Dialogues Between Worlds from Assouline.

#6733. The Story Behind Peggy Scott’s Wedding Dress

The Gilded Age threw the wedding of the year for the fan-favorite character, played by Denée Benton

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

It’s 88 degrees on a humid June day in Troy, New York, and Denée Benton stands in a local church’s outbuilding, posing in Peggy Scott’s wedding dress, not breaking a sweat.

It has been a long, hot day of filming, and the cast and crew are scheduled to keep going for many more hours. To get to this photo shoot, I had to walk past a cramped church hallway where Cynthia Nixon, Audra McDonald, and John Douglas Thompson sat together in various stages of Gilded Age undress, trading inside jokes. Everyone on set is in a state of elation, both because of the long-planned-for episode featuring the wedding of Peggy and Dr. William Kirkland and because it’s the day of Game 4 of the Knicks’s historic NBA run. A few hours after I leave set, Nixon will post a reel of the cast reacting to the Knicks win in their Gilded Age finery. But even amidst all of this excitement, Benton in her wedding dress steals the show.

Writers, production assistants, costume designers, and even Jordan Donica, who plays Dr. Kirkland, gather to watch the shoot. Benton, in the middle of it all, is poised in the gown of white lace, a corset underneath, dress lines crisp, makeup unsmudged, a picture of bridal serenity. Later, Kasia Walicka Maimone, the show’s costume designer, will tell me she spied Benton doing breathing exercises in the church pews, just to maintain her stamina.

Peggy has been a fan favorite since her introduction in the show’s first episode. An ambitious, upper-class Black woman in Gilded Age America, she is an anomaly in period television. Her storyline has introduced viewers to the world of the Black elite, a burgeoning class that emerged during Reconstruction and would dominate Black political and cultural life to the present day. Popular nonfiction books like Our Kind of People have documented the history of this group, but they have been largely absent from television. Even Black weddings, of any time period, are rarely portrayed on American TV. So how do you design a dress for a wedding that hasn’t been seen before?

“The Gilded Age,” as the period between the 1870s and 1890s is known, “was famous for its excitement [around fashion],” says Walicka Maimone. “How do we represent this excitement? How do we pull out from the historical research things that we, the modern audience, are going to be excited about? We have this giant library of about 50 to 60,000 images that we’ve collected since season 1 … but there’s always manipulation that I apply to it.” Peggy’s wedding dress “resonates with a lot of elements from the period, but it’s not a direct quote of any one specific dress.…What was really important to both Denée and me was the composite of the silhouette [and] the texture of the lace,” a detail that was “referred to in the script directly by her mother-in-law.”

“The speed that we had to accomplish the design and execution and put it on the screen was astronomical,” Walicka Maimone says. “Hundreds of hands altogether contribute to the making. Each dress takes about six to eight weeks to be built.” Those working on the final bridal look included the in-house costume-design team, milliners for the veil and hat, a jeweler in Rome who designed Peggy’s earrings, and on-set tailors who maintained the costume during filming.

“My wedding dress had over 75 buttons going down the back. Amber, my head dresser, buttoned each one by hand. The craftsmanship was really unbelievable,” Benton tells me a few months later. “It felt powerful to me, spiritually. It felt like we were embodying a moment for a lot of people. I have never seen a luxury Black wedding of the 1800s on any screen, on a stage, in any popular media. And so it really feels like we’re getting to be a part of a pop-culture history moment.”

Peggy is a child of post-Reconstruction America. Her father, Arthur, experienced enslavement, but she is part of the first generation of Black people born into freedom and citizenship. The thrill of watching her on screen is seeing a character who relishes pushing against the boundaries of what that might mean. She is both an avatar of modern viewers’ desires and a fantasy of what Black womanhood might have looked like in a time of dynamic social change and fabulous dressing. For Peggy, the question of a wedding and marriage takes on additional significance.

“Because the law of the land during enslavement recognized enslaved people as property, [and] therefore not protected by the law, nor were there unions, whether they were chosen unions or forced unions … they could be separated at any moment. The law did not protect the sanctity of marriage for Black people, for enslaved people,” explains historian Erica Armstrong Dunbar, who is chair of the African-American Studies department at Emory University, in addition to a consultant on The Gilded Age.

Marriage for the Gilded Age generation of Black people was an assertion of agency and an act of claiming Black humanity. The ability to choose partners and form Black families—and have those family bonds legally protected—was a radical act in the aftermath of slavery. All of this history can inform the context of Peggy Scott’s marriage, but what that wedding might look like on screen is another question.

“When we first started talking about the season and the wedding, Sonja Warfield [the episode’s writer] and Julian Fellowes [The Gilded Age’s creator] were very invested in the notion of the jumping of the broom and that being a part of African American wedding tradition,” Armstrong Dunbar explains. Jumping the broom was a wedding tradition practiced by some enslaved people, a ceremonial act to mark a bond that by definition was not legally recognized.

“I immediately was like, but that’s not really happening in the 1880s,” Armstrong Dunbar explains. “People were actually distancing themselves from many of the traditional experiences connected to the weddings of enslaved people.”

Fellowes, Warfield, and Gilded Age executive producer Salli Richardson-Whitfield and producer Davita Scarlett, however, were all adamant about including this practice in the wedding episode. Many of them had participated in broom-jumping ceremonies in their own weddings; the practice became popular again in the 1970s, ’80s, and ’90s as post–Civil Rights era couples looked to include specifically African American rituals in their wedding ceremonies.

But part of the pleasure of The Gilded Age is watching how the show plays with historical conventions and anachronisms to further draw viewers into characters’ lives. In this case, the writers and producers decided to give Peggy’s father, Arthur, who was once enslaved, the task of handing the broom he used in his wedding ceremony to his daughter, a moment that recognizes the family’s beginnings and its triumph of seeing a freeborn daughter legally wed. “It really encapsulated the way in which people try to meld traditions when they’re bringing their families together,” says Scarlett. “It just felt too delicious and too interesting and too thematically rich to pass up.”

“I knew that we were going to be doing Peggy getting married, and I claimed it. I said, ‘No one else is allowed to direct this wedding but me,’ ” says director Richardson-Whitfield. “I just wanted to make sure that it was as lush and gorgeous and special as anything else that we’ve done on the show.”

This included the decision to feature a gospel choir. “We specifically chose also to do it a cappella and not have any instruments with the music because I felt like those beautiful Black voices were enough to fill the church,” she says. An a cappella chorus singing spirituals would have been, like the broom jumping, frowned upon by many upper-class Black people in the 1880s, but that tension provided another opportunity to deepen the wedding scenes. “Just because we are African American people doesn’t mean we all think the same thing,” Richardson-Whitfield says. “It’s nice to be able to explore different kinds of Black people.”

The show’s commitment to nuance and its willingness to acknowledge the historical existence of different kinds of marginalized people means its fan base stretches far and wide. Recently, the creative agency Little Big World hosted a Gilded Age drag ball and panel on queer black history with Benton, activist and Gilded Age fan Brittany Packnett Cunningham, and queer historian Hugh Ryan. The event was packed.

“I think that people see Peggy as this sort of doll princess,” Benton tells me, “but as the actor playing her … bell hooks has this quote that’s like, ‘Queer not as in who I sleep with, but queer as in at odds with everything around me.’ That is Peggy. Period. There was not really a class of professional women [during the Gilded Age]; you were either aristocracy or you were working class. And then to be a Black professional, she really is one of one moving through this space in her time. And I find that to be very queer.”

“That’s one of the two powers of history work for me,” says Ryan. “One is seeing … ‘Oh, things actually were really different back then. So that means they could be really different in the future.’ And I think that's liberatory. But the other part, and this is where I think The Gilded Age shines … is getting people excited. I was in a panel with 60 Black queer folk in a Black queer space in Bed-Stuy talking about the Gilded Age and Black drag kings in vaudeville. That conversation only happens because beautiful art sparks our interest and allows us to see a place in the past.”

“As an artist, you can’t really ask for more than to do something that is popular and meaningful at the same time,” Benton says. “The sweet spot.”

Another artist who didn’t land a nod was pop princess and Cosmo cover girl Kim Petras, who made history as the first openly transgender woman to win a Grammy back in 2023. Her career took a tumultuous turn when she signed a major-label deal after riding the success of her award-winning track with Sam Smith, “Unholy.”

Years into her deal, she publicly accused her label of gatekeeping funds and resources, and pleaded to be released from her contract. She ultimately returned to her independent roots for her latest record, Detour, funding most of the studio sessions, visuals, and live performances herself. When the album didn’t earn a nod, fans claimed she was “snubbed,” since her hard work deserved recognition.

“I wouldn’t say so … I just left the industry tbh and I made the music to make the music cause I love it so much,” she wrote in response, claiming that her “nomination days [are] over” as she roots for Tinashe and Underscores.

The self-proclaimed “Greatest Band in the World,” MUNA, also started gaining momentum since trading a larger label for Phoebe Bridgers’ independent Saddest Factory Records in 2022. Their latest album, Dancing on the Wall, drew massive crowds at festivals like Lollapalooza and All Things Go last summer. The Academy has yet to acknowledge them, despite continued success.

The band’s lead singer, Katie Gavin, released a short video addressing fans after nominations dropped, saying, “I wasn’t expecting it, and I understand it. And it’s tough for a band like us because we make pop music, but we’re not as big as other pop artists.”

She added that they attempted to submit for alternative categories, but they ultimately didn’t make the cut because of their inherent pop sound. In the end, she kept things candid.

To put it plainly, there isn’t a definitive right or wrong way to go about being slept on. If artists decide to publicly address their snubs in the future, they should grab their pens and papers and take notes from Katie’s final sentiment.

“I’m really proud of all the people that did get nominated. There was so much amazing music this year,” she shared. “I hate being disappointed, but I’m not giving up hope.”
