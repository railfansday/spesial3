#7052. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

Censuswide surveyed 1,200 information technology and security professionals across nine countries and eight industry sectors in the first half of 2026.

The sample has an important limit: This year's study included only organizations where at least one ransomware attack caused business disruption. Semperis sells identity security, recovery and crisis management products.

The report says 84% of respondents experienced attacks that exploited AI in some way. Its breakdown distinguishes AI-based attacks, reported by 40%, from attacks that manipulated victims' own AI agents or systems, reported by 44%. Defensive use of AI also produced mixed responses. Those are survey responses about victimized organizations, rather than a controlled comparison of security products.

Chris Inglis, former U.S. national cyber director and a Semperis strategic adviser, described the difficulty of recognizing AI's involvement. "Many attacks are not perceived as being aided and abetted by AI because they're just too slick, too sophisticated," he said in the report.

Data erasure, destruction, or release remained the most frequently reported threat, at 56%. Blackmail, extortion or public shaming followed at 41%, with system lockouts or operational sabotage at 40%. The report also recorded psychological intimidation at 23% and physical threats at 17%.

#6916. 7 Window Treatment Fixes Designers Swear By

Smart solutions for windows that are anything but standard.

You’d be hard-pressed to find a homeowner who complains about having too many windows, but not all windows are created equal. Maybe you’re saddled with a skinny one tucked in the corner of a bedroom, or an unusually shaped one in a bathroom. The right treatment can make even the most awkward window feel intentional—but the trick is knowing which solution works best for each situation.

Here, interior designers Paige Kelly and Lindsey Walsh share the window treatment fixes they turn to again and again, along with a few clever ideas you may not have considered.

If a room is short on vertical space, Kelly recommends mounting your curtain rod a few inches from the top of the wall, rather than right above the window. “Getting the drapery high and wide is going to make a room feel bigger,” she says.

Tall panels draw the eye up, but make sure they are long enough to skim the floor. “Otherwise, they look like high-water pants, and it just looks wrong,” warns Walsh.

TWOPAGES Liz Linen Pleated Drapes are a full-length option with a tailored pleat that gives the finished look a little extra polish.

Walsh points to “pattern drenching” as a way to make a space feel more cohesive and help soften the edges of an oddly placed window or render an awkward proportion less noticeable. When you carry the same pattern across the walls and window treatments, the window becomes part of a larger visual composition rather than a feature that stands out for the wrong reasons.

Walsh notes that TWOPAGES can work with paper-backed fabric to allow the same material to cover both the walls and windows. A wallpaper installer can apply the fabric to the walls, and a window treatment workroom will install it as drapery or shades, carefully aligning the pattern repeat so the two applications meet seamlessly.

Increasing the fullness of drapery panels can trick the eye so that windows appear more substantial. “You can make yours look wider by using the stack back required for a wider window,” says Walsh. “The panel fabric may just be covering a lot of the wall, but it will look like there’s a window there.”

If the window placement is part of the problem, Kelly suggests extending the curtain rod beyond the window and using a one-way draw. “Gathering a drapery stack to one side feels intentional, and can help balance an off-center window,” she says.

In rooms where you don’t need full blackout, like kitchens and bathrooms, cafe curtains—which hang in the middle of a window along the check rail—are a great solution. “They cover your neighbor’s dirty yard but you still get to see the sky,” says Kelly. TWOPAGES also sells a variety of ready-to-hang cafe curtains, along with the necessary hardware in a gold or black finish.

Long panels aren’t always practical, particularly when a window sits above a radiator, cabinetry, or a piece of furniture. In those situations, Walsh often turns to roman shades. They’re a streamlined way to introduce fabric and softness without taking up wall space.

For a more decorative look, Walsh loves adding a lambrequin, a fixed fabric-covered frame around the window, with a functional shade tucked beneath it. “It’s a great fix when the interior architecture gets in the way of having the kind of window treatment you want,” she says.

TWOPAGES carries a wide range of shade materials—from linen blends and embroidered cottons to bamboo and woven wood—and offers every fold and lift type possible, so you can find the exact form and function to best fit your space.

Walsh likes to use drapery beyond traditional windows, especially if a room feels stark or echoey because of finishes such as marble, glass, or tile. A portière—a curtain hung in a doorway or cased opening—can create a sense of separation between spaces, soften an entryway, or help buffer cold air near an exterior door.

“Even if they’re purely decorative, drapery and window treatments absorb sound and provide texture and visual interest,” adds Kelly.

The right fabric reinforces that effect. A lighter fabric like TWOPAGES Patti Belgian Flax Linen Curtain adds softness without feeling too heavy, while a drape with a more substantial texture, such as the brand’s Birkin Velvet Curtain, adds visual warmth and a richer feel, perfect for the colder months.

The smallest details can make the biggest difference, even if custom treatments are not in your budget. Instead of a traditional curtain rod with finials on each end, which can look dated, Walsh advises using a French return rod, which is mounted on the wall and curves outward. This allows the end of the panels to sit flush against the wall while still creating space for a stack back.

“A French return rod is by far the most elegant way to fake it and make your drapes look professional,” says Walsh. But stay away from grommets—“they are a dead giveaway you did it yourself,” she says—and use rings with clips instead.

Tollett finds a thrill in uncovering her clients’ design styles and endless joy in creating their dream homes that reflect who they are.

#6890. Take Note: This Is How Cool People Are Decorating for Thanksgiving This Year

These tastemakers are bringing their personal style to the holiday.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pumpkin centerpieces, rustic garlands, dried-fruit wreaths, and cornucopias are classic Thanksgiving decorations. They’re timeless and beloved, but they can also feel predictable. Traditional decor has its place, of course, but Thanksgiving tablescapes—and dinner parties in general—are evolving, much like interiors, to feel more personal, lived-in, and layered. So we asked nine tastemakers and experts how they’re decorating for this family-centric holiday.

From traditional touches to wholly unexpected looks, read on to see how they’re making their homes feel festive this autumn, and maybe you’ll be inspired to try something new this year.

Ever since designer Mikel Welch moved to sunny California, his family has never hesitated to go to his for Thanksgiving to escape the chilly Michigan weather. It’s become a tradition to gather at his house, and similarly to how he grew up, Welch likes to keep his decorations for the holiday understated and warm.

“I love getting the exterior of my house into full fall mode with mums, pumpkins, and seasonal greenery,” Welch tells House Beautiful. “The beauty of fall decorating is that it can carry you from October straight through Thanksgiving without having to change much.”

The designer’s house is filled with dark, moody colors and earthy textures year-round, so he focuses mainly on bringing natural elements inside to do the work for him. “I also have a little tradition with my dad,” he adds. “For the past couple of years, we've made trips to the nursery together to pick out flowers, greenery, and sometimes bales of hay for the front of the house. It's become something I really enjoy doing with him.”

“Thanksgiving has always been a big family affair,” real estate expert and maximalism extraordinaire Harvin Greene says. She’s happy to keep that tradition going as an adult, just in her own way. In the past, Greene’s aunt and uncle would have everyone at their house for the big meal, but last year, they passed the hosting baton to Greene for the first time. “I may have gotten a little carried away...in the best possible way.”

Getting to decorate two tables—one for the “adults,” another for the “kids,” even though they’re technically all grown-ups, too—Greene used this opportunity to design two completely different tablescapes. “I grew up surrounded by women who understood that a beautifully set table and a genuinely good time should go hand in hand,” she adds.

The one in the formal dining space (i.e., the “adults’” table) was theatrical and dramatic, and nearly everything on it had some sort of familial connection, from her parents’ engraved goblets to her husband’s grandmother’s sterling flatware. The kids’ table, on the other hand, was whimsical and vibrant, complete with a yellow patterned tablecloth, colorful glassware, and playful animal candlesticks. “I don't feel the need to follow the traditional Thanksgiving color palette,” Greene says. “Who decided Thanksgiving had to be orange and brown? Yellow, pink, turquoise, and green can be just as festive.”

The holiday isn’t over until they bring out the Bingo cards, a spirited Thanksgiving tradition that Greene’s Aunt Jean started—which involves cash prizes. “It's one of those family traditions that makes the holiday feel like ours, no matter whose house we're celebrating in.”

Talia Hubble is no stranger to a good dinner party—much of her content actually centers around her love of hosting. But Thanksgiving is a bit extra special for the Rhode Islander, especially since it’s being held at her cottage this year. “Growing up, it was just me and my mom, and she was too busy to decorate,” Hubble says. “I think that’s part of why I’ve fallen in love with seasonal decorating as an adult, I get to bring all my cozy childhood decorating dreams to life!”

As soon as the calendar switches from October to November, Hubble starts decorating for fall, opting for an eclectic mix of subtle Christmas decor (think bows, garlands, and wreaths), an excessive amount of taper candles, and a traditional Thanksgiving palette of oranges, browns, greens, and reds. Every year looks a little bit different, but there are pieces in her collection that are simply too cute not to bring out every holiday season.

“Half the fun of pulling everything out for Thanksgiving is remembering where it came from,” she says. “I think the best decor is a culmination of pieces you’ve been collecting for years, and the memories behind those pieces are what make the table feel special and uniquely yours.”

By the time she’s done decorating, her home feels like a warm, welcoming escape, somewhere her guests can exhale. “My philosophy is that you can’t go wrong when you aim for cozy, colorful, and embrace a little organized mess,” Hubble adds. “My favorite Thanksgiving tables are the ones that look even better halfway through dinner, when the napkins are rumpled and everyone’s reaching for seconds.”

Chef and food stylist Romilly Newman can’t always count on being in the same place as her family for Thanksgiving since everyone’s scattered about, but she always makes a point to throw an autumnal dinner with friends around the holiday time, complete with beautiful fall decor.

Her mom never decorated their home while she was growing up with specific Thanksgiving decorations—instead, she would decorate with deep orange and red flowers to capture the fall vibe. Newman’s style as an adult is similar. “I am fall-obsessed, so as soon as the temperature drops from summer, I'm setting up branches, candles, and darker accents,” she says. “I love the atmosphere inside and outside around Thanksgiving time, and I try my best to bottle that up in my home.”

Natural thicker linen, pops of silver, dark florals, and moody lighting envelop her space as the chef relies mostly on candlelight and organic elements—like figs, sage, chestnuts, and mandarins—as the decorative accents.

“Thanksgiving is great because it brings family together and offers time to enjoy food, friends, and being at home,” Newman adds. “Whatever your style is, try to create an environment that allows for great conversation and a festive evening. You want everyone to be comfortable and feel the holiday spirit.”

When writer Olivia Noceda was a child, she would stay up late the night before Thanksgiving and design custom menus for everyone on Microsoft Paint, which she would then print out, frame, and place on each table setting in preparation for the big meal. The holiday was a big deal for Noceda’s mother, who would always put on the most beautiful Thanksgiving gatherings. “I think that's where my love for hosting and setting a beautiful table really started,” she explains.

However, for the last six years, Noceda has been having Thanksgiving with her husband’s side of the family, so when she does host around the holiday, it’s typically for Friendsgiving. “I don't necessarily pick a theme, but I do like everything to feel cohesive,” she explains of her decorating process. “I usually start incorporating little autumnal touches sometime in October and let them carry me through Thanksgiving.”

The writer and recipe developer lets the season guide her, building her tablescape and decor around a beautiful branch from the farmers’ market, a bowl of pears or persimmons, a collection of little pumpkins and squash, or a few taper candles on the dining room table. Ingredients play a big part in her decor—she can even take a fresh loaf of bread and make it feel special on her table.

For the rest of her decor, she likes to incorporate vintage serving pieces, everyday elements, and elevated details like antique silver and linen napkins with things she already owns to lean into that lived-in, warm feeling. The last thing she wants is her home to feel like a department store display. “Nothing needs to match perfectly, and I actually prefer when it doesn't,” Noceda adds.

What entrepreneur and Real Housewives of New York star Erin Lichy remembers best about Thanksgivings growing up is the smell. “We always spent Thanksgiving out east, and the house was full of pumpkins, local hay, and lanterns,” she explains. The scent of that fresh produce, mixed with her grandmother’s homemade turkey t’bit, has shaped the way she decorates for and hosts Thanksgiving. “That coveted and highly personal (and incredibly delicious) recipe is in my cookbook, She’s a Host, and it’s part of every Thanksgiving I host.”

Decorating starts in the kitchen for her family. Lichy wants the home to smell like fall, so she focuses on that sense, making seasonal recipes with squash and pumpkin and incorporating autumnal flowers in her interiors. The melding of those scents gives her townhouse a warm feeling, especially when mixed with layered fall colors and the crackling of the fireplace.

Ever since Lichy and her family have moved into their townhouse, she’s been obsessed with decorating her stoop too. “The stoop is the first thing everyone sees, so this year I decided to bring in Simon Wallace Design to make it really stand out!” Lichy adds. “They completely transformed it with layers of seasonal color and texture, and it feels so warm and welcoming.” She specifically loves using the faux garlands and pinecones, as well as lanterns, from Balsam Hill and Frontgate because they’re so beautifully made that they look real, and she’s able to reuse them year after year.

No matter what she does to decorate though, she just wants her home to feel warm and lived-in, and this is the season and holiday to really lean into that.

Decorating for the holidays is Gabby Amato’s version of a self-care day. “It's truly one of my favorite days of the year,” she says. “I carve out a weekend afternoon when my husband and son are out of the house, light my favorite fall candles, turn on some music, and have the house entirely to myself.”

Amato, the founder of Table Two Studio, a paper plate brand which specializes in beautiful, artistic designs that you definitely won’t want to throw away, credits her love of Thanksgiving and the fall season in general to her mother. While growing up in the ‘90s, she saw her mom put so much care and effort into the holiday decorations, always making their home feel warm, nostalgic, and full of spirit. “For Thanksgiving, she decorated our mantel with gourds, greenery, and ceramic pilgrim statues that were a gift from my grandmother,” Amato says. “We had oversized cornstalks and pumpkins at the front door. Nothing ever felt overly styled or precious.”

It’s this feeling that has stuck with her throughout her life, and it’s what she hopes she captures in her own home every year. She begins decorating for the fall early, right after Labor Day, pulling out old pieces and unboxing anything new and arranging it all in fresh ways so nothing feels stale. “I love beautiful decor, of course, but I'm much more interested in creating an atmosphere than achieving a particular aesthetic,” she adds. “I want my home to feel warm, lived-in, a little nostalgic, and festive.”

Donna Lennard’s family was never one for holiday dressing—most of the attention at Thanksgiving fell on the delicious food shared with family. As a restauranteur and cofounder of beloved NYC establishment il Buco, it makes sense why the meal remains to be the most important thing to Lennard even today.

“Clearly, the table is always festive, whether it’s the tablecloth, the napkins, or linens that reflect the season in burnt orange and the like,” she explains. “I love to decorate a table with assorted colorful gourds and seasonal florals, beautiful candlelight (our beeswax candles already have an orange glow), orange ranunculus, and the occasional dried floral.” Colorful Pienza glassware dots the table and her plethora of beautiful tableware gives it more character, but she keeps everything else relatively simple to ensure the food remains the center of attention.

Though Lennard doesn’t often host her family and friends for the Thanksgiving meal, she’s already looking forward to next year when everyone will finally gather at her beach house.

Being from Australia, Kendall Knox—the cofounder of Olive Ateliers along with her husband—didn’t have a personal reference of what the holiday “should” be or look like. This gives her the freedom of deciding what it is for her family. “What it has become is an intimate table: my husband Ben, our son Rhodes, and Ben's mum,” she says. “No rotation, no kids' table, no forty-pound bird. Just the four of us and a dinner that runs long because nobody is in a hurry to leave it.”

Since Knox isn’t beholden to the classic Thanksgiving decor, she tends to keep her home and her table rather simple, “shopping the house,” as she puts it to decorate instead of buying something new. She keeps a rather neutral palette of rich browns, creamy whites, terracotta, and silver, then uses antique linens, mismatched silverware, and candlesticks that have collected wax to capture the true lived-in feel.

Her focus tends to be on the table and the meal, letting the food add more color. If a tablecloth has a wine ring on it from the year before, all the better—it complements the stacked plates at the end of the meal.

“The objects that come out every year, that get a little more marked each time, are the ones that end up meaning the most,” Knox adds. “Thanksgiving is just a very good excuse to use them.”

#6879. Dreaming of Buying a Home Abroad? These 10 Countries Make It Shockingly Simple

If you’ve always wanted to live out your expat fantasies, start here.

Over the last few years, many Americans have daydreamed about life abroad. Moving internationally can often feel like a pipe dream, but with the right planning, it’s possible. However, lengthy visa processes, daunting language barriers, and a difficult-to-navigate legal system can make the dream feel more like a nightmare.

Certain countries, like Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, have bans on foreign buyers. Others, like Thailand, Cuba, and Vietnam, don’t allow outsiders to own property at all. On the other end of the spectrum are countries actively trying to attract foreign buyers, offering enviable incentives like affordable home prices, a smoother customs process, and an easier-to-navigate work-visa approval process.

Choosing the right location can make moving abroad much less intimidating. Ahead, we’re breaking down the ten best countries for Americans looking to buy property abroad. Whether you’re dreaming of relocating or searching for your perfect vacation home, these countries make buying as easy as possible and offer gorgeous landscapes too.

If you’ve ever dreamed of living among medieval architecture and rolling mountain ranges, Georgia might be the key to unlocking your fantasy. A bridge between Eastern Europe and Western Asia, the transcontinental country borders Russia, Turkey, Armenia, and the Black Sea, offering both subtropical and high-altitude climates. Thankfully, the real estate landscape is much less complex, with few legal or bureaucratic hurdles for American buyers, no residency or visa required (for up to a year), and remarkably fast processing times. In fact, the only major restriction in the Georgia market is that foreigners cannot purchase or own agricultural land, a nod to the importance of the local, small-scale farming cultures peppered across the country’s regions.

Uruguay’s buying process could not be more straightforward. South America’s second-smallest sovereign country requires no citizenship, visa, or prior approvals. Under Uruguayan law, foreign buyers are given the same rights as citizens and enjoy the benefits of free capital flow. Locals are just as, if not more, tourist-friendly. The country is known for its hospitality, laid-back café culture, and stunning coastal landscapes.

Instead of retiring to Florida, consider this balmy, beautiful Caribbean country—just a four-hour direct flight from the New York area. Like everything else on the island, the real estate laws are relatively relaxed, making buying a house easy-breezy; and you can buy and own property without obtaining residency status or special permissions, as is the case in many other Caribbean islands. “Foreigners can own property outright in the Dominican Republic, with legal protections for foreign investors,” says Mauricio Umansky, CEO and founder of The Agency. One important consideration is financing: You can certainly get a mortgage in the DR, but it may not be quite as cushy as what you can get in the U.S.—so pay cash if possible.

Great news if you’ve been wanting a more provincial life: Americans face zero restrictions when buying homes in France. The open market allows for statesiders to buy houses, apartments, or plots of land in the city, countryside, and coastal towns in person or remotely without needing citizenship, residency, or even a local visa. However, a purchase does not grant residency, so if you’re hoping to make France your permanent home, you’ll want to apply for a separate long-stay visa and note the Schengen 90-out-of-180-day rule. While buying may be simple, the finances are anything but. Closing fees, including notary fees, typically add seven to ten percent to your purchase. Mortgage rates are also incredibly hard to secure for U.S.-based buyers, thanks to strict debt-to-income caps and FATCA compliance. To simplify the process, many buyers opt to put 20 percent to 40 percent down in cash.

Perhaps the most American-friendly market, Panama boasts a large English-speaking market, virtually no restrictions on foreign property ownership, and uses the U.S. dollar as legal tender in a vastly affordable market. The Central American nation also offers residency programs like the Friendly Nations Visa, clearing the way legally for residency by investment. In addition to an efficient legal system and reliable market, Panama boasts breathtaking beaches, lush rainforests, and exceptional wildlife.

If you’re hoping for a home in the coffee valleys of Colombia, you’ll need to hire a real estate lawyer before you start scouting for mountain chalets, coffee triangle treehouses, and rainforest retreats. The Caribbean country opts out of standardized agent licensing systems, making legal aid essential to verify title and freedom certificates. While citizenship isn’t required, you will need to secure a tourist visa and Colombian Tax ID to complete your purchase and register for property taxes.

The buying process in Greece is lengthy, though it is streamlined, with a clear checklist for American buyers to find success in the market. Hiring a local lawyer or notary is a great first step after scouting properties. Not only are they familiar with title checks and contracts, but they can also navigate the intricacies that come with buying in designated border areas or near military zones, which require special clearance for non–EU buyers. While the most popular areas for American buyers, like Athens and Crete, don’t come with heavy legal intricacies, buying any property in Greece will mean securing a Greek Tax Number—a small price to pay for cities with such rich histories and breathtaking seaside views.

The people in this popular Western European coastal country are famously friendly and inviting, and that extends to real estate. According to Samira Easton, a certified international property specialist with Premier Sotheby’s International Realty, Portugal offers a Golden Visa Program, which makes it simple to buy property and gain residency; clear real estate laws; and a well-established network of English-speaking real estate agents, lawyers, and notaries to help you during the process.

Belize is home to one of the most vibrant, tourist-friendly cultures in Central America, mixing Mayan, Hispanic, Asian, and Creole cultures in its dense rainforests and rolling low plains. Buying here is as easy as it can get, as outright ownership applies to foreigners, and a British-inspired legal system abolishes language barriers and makes contracts easier to understand. Even more, there is no capital gains tax in Belize, and property taxes trend low. The country also boasts vibrant marine life and is home to the world’s second-largest barrier reef.

Spain may have ended its investor visa program in 2025, but the real estate market is still remarkably easy for Americans to navigate. The country currently imposes no restrictions on foreign or nonresident property ownership, but there are a few things you’ll need to procure before you buy, like a Foreigner Identification Number, which is mandatory for taxes. Despite the minor hurdles, the market is booming both on the ground and online. “There are also numerous online resources and property portals available, so it’s easy to find listings and information,” Easton says. This is a boon for those who aren’t able to make the trip over to Europe to house shop in person.Note that buying property does not grant you residency or bypass the country’s 90-day Schengen limit. If you’re hoping to turn your Spanish holiday into your everyday, you’ll have to apply for a separate visa.

#6857. Sam Heughan and Stephanie Bullock Make Their Red Carpet Debut in Coordinating Looks

Sam Heughan is making things red carpet official with his girlfriend, model Stephanie Bullock. On Sunday, November 15, the Outlander actor brought his partner to the BAFTA Scotland Awards in Glasgow for their first joint appearance.

For the occasion, Heughan wore a Siobhan Mackenzie navy jacket and waistcoat over a white shirt, paired with a blue-and-green tartan kilt and black lace-up boots. Bullock complemented his look, wearing a matching tartan shawl with a floor-length black sequined gown.

Although they have kept their relationship largely out of the public eye, Bullock was seen with Heughan at New York City’s Tartan Day Parade in April.

The couple’s red carpet debut coincided with a significant moment in Heughan’s career. At Sunday’s ceremony, he accepted the Outstanding Contribution to Television award on behalf of Outlander.

Heughan keeps a low profile outside of work. In a November 2024 interview with InStyle, the actor discussed how public recognition can affect his ability to fly under the radar.

“I think actors before social media were pretty elusive,” he said. “I love to people watch. I don’t like standing out in the crowd…I love sitting in a café or a bar and just watching people.”

He prefers not to share much about his private life because “I just think it’s who I am.” And keeping a little mystery helps with work, too. “I think an actor should disappear behind the character,” he said. “You shouldn’t be like, ‘Oh, there’s Sam Heughan onstage playing a character.’”
