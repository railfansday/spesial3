#7019. For His 40th Birthday, Drake Flexed a Custom Rolex and a $250,000 Watch in the Space of 24 Hours

The rapper marked his milestone birthday with two extravagant timepieces, including a one-of-a-kind Daytona worth millions.

Turning 40 is a milestone worth celebrating in style. And to nobody's surprise, Drake did just that.

As you'd expect, the Canadian rapper's birthday outfit was not exactly understated. In fact, his watch rotation featured not one, but two rather extraordinary Rolexes. Why settle for one when you can have two?

The first was a custom, sapphire-covered Daytona, while the second was a colorful Day-Date 36 with a reported six-figure price tag. There are certainly worse ways to welcome a new decade.

First up was the Rolex Cosmograph Daytona, a watch that makes even the most extravagant diamond-set timepieces look positively modest. The one-of-a-kind creation features a diamond-pavé dial, brilliant blue sapphire hour markers, and a bezel set with baguette-cut blue sapphires. But the real showstopper is the bracelet, which is covered in a gradient of blue sapphires that gradually fade into diamonds.

And if all those precious stones weren't enough to make your eyes water, the estimated price tag certainly might. The one-of-a-kind creation has reportedly been valued at anywhere between $2 million and $5 million. Although, given that Rolex made just one, it's difficult to put an exact figure on it.

While you might assume the rapper had commissioned the watch especially for his big birthday, he actually debuted it back in August. Still, when you've got something quite this spectacular sitting in your watch collection, it would be a shame not to give it another outing.

The second Rolex was a rather different kind of extravagance. Drake also showed off a rose-gold Day-Date 36 featuring the brand's famously playful puzzle dial. Instead of traditional hour markers, baguette-cut sapphires surround a dial made up of brightly colored jigsaw pieces. And rather than displaying the usual day and date, the apertures reveal inspirational words and emojis, including a little red heart. It's about as far from your grandfather's Rolex as you can get.

The watch has been valued at around $250,000 which, rather remarkably, makes it the considerably cheaper of Drake's two birthday watches.

Of course, Drake has never been one for doing things by halves, particularly when it comes to watches. And while most people would be more than happy to receive a single Rolex for their 40th, it seems the rapper couldn't quite settle on one.

#7018. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#7008. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#7002. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6966. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6962. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#6916. 4 Weekend Decluttering Habits That Make Small Spaces Feel Bigger

A few smart systems can keep your counters, closets, and junk drawers in check.

The easiest clutter to manage is clutter that never has a chance to form. And in small spaces like galley kitchens and studio apartments, mess piles up naturally on any flat surface. Keeping it under control is less about “magic bullet” tips and more about systems you can actually keep up over time. So when we asked the professional organizers about how to declutter small spaces, the answer was in mindset and habits. If you can limit what comes into your space in the first place, you can better control its organization.

“I could say, ‘Grab a garbage bag and pick up all of the detritus left in the wake of a busy life and then put everything else where it belongs.’ That’s obvious,” Gayle M. Gruenberg of Let’s Get Organized says. “The hard part is actually DOING it. It requires motivation, presence of mind, initiative, and the necessary tools.”

Below, find the pro-approved habits that make a difference when you have more stuff than your space can hold. You can start these routines in just one weekend, and it’s far more attainable than tackling the massive pile in the garage.

Everything needs a home in your house. That’s the power of a well-placed “drop zone.” Even in small spaces, a catchall tray, bowl, or mini basket belongs at entryways or places of transition. A hook for your keys and bags when you come through the door, a bin for loose throw blankets, or a basket for remote controls on the mantel.

Throughout the day, it can take quite a bit of mental load to always put everything back when you use it. That’s where Cathy Orr, cofounder of The Uncluttered Life, recommends having a “collection basket.” It’s just one catchall container, where you can drop miscellaneous misplaced items that you pick up throughout the day. At the end of the night, take about 10 minutes to go through the collection basket and return everything to where it belongs. It’s just one small habit, rather than something to keep up with all day.

Impulse shopping lends itself to clutter very easily. The “One In, One Out” rule can curb this. It works like this: for every new thing you want to bring into your home, one thing has to go. Greenhalgh suggests checking your closet, fridge, and pantry before you shop—going through your back stock will save you both space and money.

“In small spaces, items accumulate very quickly,” Macie Kreutzer, founder of For the Love of Simple, says. “Before shopping for new items (home goods, clothing) clear out at least one item from this category. Don't bring in more than your space can physically contain.”

Once you have everything you’re ready to get rid of, make the donation process as seamless as possible. Fill your donation bags and boxes in the hallway or in the car. This encourages you to actually finish getting rid of the clutter, rather than letting them collect dust in the garage.

“Nothing gets to wander back inside just because it once lived there,” Carroll Cartwright, professional organizer and owner of Neatly & Co., says. “And this is why I love moving everything out of the space and into the hall or a separate area first. The things you decide not to keep are already halfway out the door. Keep them moving.”

It is so tempting to take advantage of the freebies in life. But this is how linen closets and kitchen drawers get quickly overrun. Unless there’s a specific and immediate need for them, you’re better off getting rid of them.

“They are usually cheap and break easily anyway,” Diane Greenhalgh, owner of Tiny to the Max, says. “That includes airplane and hotel freebies, special event or conference swag, and extra takeout plastic utensils and condiments. Your junk drawer will thank you.”

If you already have a drawer overflowing with freebies, it’s time to start culling and evaluating. You might part with two or 10 things, or you might choose to keep it all. “The point is that you’ve looked at every single item and made a decision about it, instead of continuing to store things simply because they were already there,” Cartwright says. “More likely than not, you’ll find something that makes you say, ‘Why on earth do I still have this?’”

The advice your grandma gave you was spot-on: It’s easier if you wash the dishes as you cook. That same principle applies to keeping your small space tidy. Mess feels larger with less square footage to work with, so once you’re finished with something, put it away.

“Put away your makeup after you've applied it in the morning, stack dishes in the dishwasher and wipe down surfaces after each meal, and tuck away work or homework into a drawer once you're done for the evening,” Greenhalgh says.

For the days you can’t clean as you go, Cartwright suggests spending 10 to 15 minutes a day “closing your circles.” It’s what helps your home stay clean over time, instead of just for a few weeks. This period is when you put the scissors back in the drawer or hang up your jacket.

“Most clutter is just a collection of unfinished tasks,” Cartwright says. “This small commitment allows you to wake up to order instead of yesterday’s chaos, and that is a much better way to start the day!”

#6895. This Colorful California Mountain Home Gives Classic Ski House Style a Fresh New Look

Using the surrounding landscape as palette inspiration, designer Alden Fletcher brought 70s retro flair to this family retreat.

When most people think of a classic ski chalet, they picture log-lined walls, dark corners, a raging hearth, plenty of plaid, and maybe even a taxidermied deer or two. But a certain kind of magic strikes when high-altitude architecture hits the right notes, and this Truckee, California, retreat is a masterclass in making the mountains sing.

Built almost 20 years ago, this family holiday home boasted all the 90s mountain classics: an abundance of knotty pine, traditional slate floors, and a minimalist beige palette. Undoubtedly stylish for the time, the retreat now felt out of step with the Bay Area family looking to make it into a personalized winter escape. The owners knew updates were imminent (necessary, even), but they feared losing the home’s classic California soul. Fighting modernism’s white-box aesthetic, they called in Alden Fletcher of the eponymous San Francisco-based design firm to meld the home’s retro architecture with a fresh, forest-inspired brief.

Noting the owner’s concerns and dedication to sustainability, the project’s goal was not to remodel for grandeur, but to restore the home’s charm. Fletcher’s task was to design around storied memories, the family’s obvious love for winter sports, and their passion for entertaining—all while combining the crisp aesthetic of the alpine wilderness with cozy textiles and natural details to create the ultimate mountain sanctuary. By pulling saturated hues from the surrounding landscape and sourcing perky patterns inspired by the local culture, the redesign breathes new life into the slope-side abode without sacrificing sentiment.

FAST FACTSDesigner: Alden Fletcher Interiors Stylist: Beth Protass Location: Truckee, CAThe Space: A five-bedroom, four-bathroom home across 3,000 square feet

The home's hallways set the tone for the entire design scheme. On the first floor, just beyond the main door, wall-mounted ski runners double as decor. Upstairs, Fletcher redefined the boundaries of the primary suite to carve out a nook for a vignette displaying the home’s most bespoke decor piece: a vintage cherry-red chairlift. “The home’s location is unique,” adds Fletcher. “It sits on a ski slope, and you can view chairlifts out the back windows!”

A true gathering space, the living room was designed around the original stone-faced fireplace, though Fletcher did replace the former pine mantel with white oak, connecting the floor details with the exposed oak beams running along the ceiling.

Swiveling burnt orange armchairs accompany an inky indigo sectional. Both gather around a button-tufted ottoman, which doubles as a coffee perch or game table. Shaded sconces flank the fireplace to give the room a glow, even when the hearth is unlit, while a pair of vintage ski shoes hang on the wall, crowning the design.

In the playroom, the deep green hue of the wallpaper paired with the natural texture of knotty pine borrows inspiration from the forest-like landscape just beyond the window. Vertical lines carried through both the wallpaper and the wainscoting create the illusion of height, while playful ski motifs bring the local sport’s culture into the play space.

“The clients are sustainably minded and didn't want to get rid of what was working,” says Fletcher. With conservation in mind, she opted to keep the original perimeter cabinets, refreshing them with paint, new hardware, and Virginia Black Satin Granite countertops, a sturdier alternative to show-stopping soapstone.

To optimize the space, she nixed the two existing islands, reimagining the room to fit a larger iteration that boasts a sink and easily seats five. The design team then added upper cabinets, transforming the space into a storage haven. Later, she placed a library ladder and golden hardware for easy access and a touch of whimsy. Then, she turned her attention to making sure the lighting was up to par. Fletcher firmly believes a beautiful space should be equally as functional. To ensure no detail fell short, she installed discreet recessed lights alongside under-cabinet lighting, and two large glass pendants to adorn the space. All dimmable for ambiance—of course. “There’s nothing worse than a home kitchen that is just flooded with bright light everywhere. Fletcher compensated for this former powder room’s lack of windows with vibrant wallpaper to energize the space and make the most of interior lighting. For the primary bath, the team capitalized on the luxury of the sloped-side views, framing a classic 400-pound slipper tub with a nearly floor-to-ceiling window featuring vintage pine trim. Truly the focal point of the design, Fletcher finished the window treatment with woven shades to tie in the natural wood details of the home and stand up to moisture. Natural grain runs through the room’s doors and vanity, topped with soft white Calacatta marble, channeling the classic ski chalet aesthetic. Gold fixtures extend from the vanity to the lighting, towel rack, and tub, for a touch of glamour.

Boasting the project’s biggest splurge, a custom bunk-trundle bed, the kid’s room combines playful hues with forest-inspired details and maximum sleepover potential. “We wanted to maximize sleeping spots in this home,” Fletcher explains. “The Casa Kids bunk bed in the kid's room was a splurge, but it can sleep 5!”

#6889. Hublot Just Relaunched Its First Watch. Founder Carlo Crocco Explains How It Changed Everything.

In Milan, celebrating the release of the Classic Fusion Tributo, I got to catch up with the guy who altered the aesthetics of modern watchmaking.

If you spend a lot of time following the world of watches, Hublot’s origin story might feel as familiar as one of the fairytales you heard at bedtime as a child. If you don’t, though, it might feel just as fantastical as some yarn about a princess who feels a pea under a stack of mattresses or a prince who slays a fire-breathing dragon.

You start with an unlikely hero. This time around, it’s an Italian entrepreneur with a big, bold, brazen idea. He wants to make a watch, something unlike anyone has ever seen. It’ll be gold, with an angular case shaped like the porthole on a ship. Not so crazy yet. But wait. Because, breaking entirely with the conventions of the day, the strap won’t be made of exotic leather or precious metal but rubber. Humble, industrial rubber.

This is exactly what Hublot founder Carlo Crocco envisioned nearly half a century ago, and what he brought to the Basel Watch Fair—pretty much the place to be if you wanted to be a part of the business of timekeeping—in 1980. He spent three years developing the idea, all without doing a bit of market research. He just believed in his vision. And then the fair began.

“I was very anxious, because for the first two days, no one came into our booth,” he says. But the anxiety didn’t have long to fester. Word was spreading. And then? People galore. “On the third day, we realized that everyone was speaking about us because it was really something different from all the other watches.”

That first watch—36mm in diameter, with a quartz movement and a black rubber strap—quickly became a hit. Industry buzz gave way to the kind of clientele most brands can only imagine. The King of Greece bought one in Gstaad for himself and another for his brother-in-law, the King of Spain. More royals followed. (“By the way, they’re all related,” Crocco quips.)

But the biggest thing wasn’t getting insiders to a booth at a trade fair or convincing kings to strap Hublot watches—named, if you’re curious, for the French word for porthole—on their wrists. It was changing how the industry thought about the way watches are made.

“At that time, the mentality of watchmaking was very classical,” Crocco says. “They couldn’t think, normal people, of putting together rubber and gold. I personally thought it was a really good idea because it was against the current. It was very risky, but if you don’t risk, you have no result.”

The result was a watch industry that completely recalibrated itself. Crocco says that within four or five years of the original Hublot’s release, the brand was already dealing with imitators. Now, the idea of combining disparate materials is firmly embedded in the minds of both consumers and designers. You can see it on watches from brands that sell at the local department store and brands that only sell to folks who haven’t seen the inside of one of those since the first time their private jet took off. Crocco’s big idea changed the whole game.

Which means it’s a pretty good time to go back to the beginning of this thing. Last month in Milan, Hublot debuted a new take on that first, groundbreaking watch. Dubbed the Hublot Classic Fusion Tributo, it’s an homage but not a nuts-and-bolts recreation. Instead of the original 36mm case, it’s been upsized to a more 2026-appropriate 38mm. And instead of a quartz movement—Crocco thought it was “more practical” at the time but has come around to the enduring appeal of mechanical construction—a self-winding HUB1711 movement with a 50-hour power reserve keeps things ticking along. Oh, and now you can get it in white gold, too.

Then there’s that rubber strap. When Crocco designed the original Hublot, he was insistent that the strap was entirely straight; he felt it was crucial to the aesthetic proposition. On the Tributo, it’s ever-so-slightly, almost imperceptibly tapered, a necessary concession to keep the proportions intact after increasing the case size (which, handily, lets that automatic movement sit comfortably inside).

But, as with the first Hublot, it’s not all about tech specs and watch-nerd particulars. It’s about the spirit of the thing. And the spirit of this watch stays true to that pioneering design. There are twelve screws around the bezel, so you can keep an eye on the hour despite the dial’s lack of indices—something Crocco says was a non-negotiable when he started consulting on the new release. The yellow gold version keeps the abyss-like black lacquer dial and the white gold throws us a tiny (and welcome) curveball by using onyx. The combo of precious metal and rubber still hits, 46 years later.

If you prod him how he feels about the way that he shifted the watch industry, Crocco won’t brag. He left Hublot nearly 20 years ago, when it sold to LVMH. He knows his idea changed things; he’s happy about it. But this isn’t his day job anymore. Still, when he’s asked if he could be considered the godfather of fusing high and low in watchmaking—and you give him a minute to ponder it—he’ll tell you the truth.

#6877. Here’s What It Takes to Keep a Pool Running During the Winter, According to Experts

Before you dive in, it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved.

When most people think of backyard swimming pools, relaxing outdoors in the summertime immediately comes to mind. But some homeowners don’t want the swimming season to end when temperatures drop. Fans of cold-water swimming are starting to consider ways to enjoy their pools throughout the winter as a part of an at-home wellness experience.

“Ten years ago, most people didn’t think winter swims were possible, especially in colder climates,” says Karen Larson, cofounder of Soake Pools. “Times have changed because these days, they’re seeing neighbors, friends, and others do it and post the proof on social media.”

The increasing interest in cold-water experiences is part of a broader wellness trend. In American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, cold therapies, including cold plunges, cryotherapy, and cold showers, earned the 17th spot among fitness trends for the year, up from number 20 in its 2025 report.

If you’ve previously closed your swimming pool around Labor Day but are interested in making cold-water swimming a part of your daily wellness routine, there’s a lot to keep in mind. Not every existing pool is a good candidate for keeping open for winter swimming, and it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved. But if you’re curious, here’s what to consider about keeping a pool open year-round.

Why is cold-water swimming becoming more common? There are numerous purported mental and wellness benefits, from athletic recovery to stress relief and better sleep. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS ONE found that cold-water immersion led to a reduction in stress for 12 hours afterward as well as improvements in sleep quality and overall quality of life.

“Winter swimming often creates a time where friends and family can come together without distractions,” adds Larson.

That said, while swimming during the colder months has its perks, there are also several risks to keep in mind. A 2020 review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that cold-water immersion can trigger an immediate shock response, leading to involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, and a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure. These reactions can make it difficult to control your breathing and increase your risk of drowning. Cold temps can also impair your muscle strength and nerve function, making it challenging to swim safely.

It’s a good idea to acclimate to the cold water gradually and avoid prolonged exposure. You’ll also want to make sure there’s someone else home while you take your cold-water plunge in the pool.

In addition, if you have underlying conditions like heart disease, consult your doctor before all else, as the cold water may place additional strain on your heart.

If you’re trying to consider whether your pool is a good candidate to keep open during the colder months, these are the things to look into.

The cost of keeping a swimming pool open for winter swimming will depend on your location, size, desired water temperature, and the type of heater you use.

“Routine pool maintenance generally remains about the same year-round, although chemical consumption may decrease depending on how frequently the pool is used,” explains Jim Eisch, Jr., vice president of operations at Pinch A Penny Pool Patio Spa. “The biggest expense to consider in the winter is heating the pool.”

For this reason, prospective winter swimmers may want to consider an upgrade to their heating system. Instead of using pricey natural gas heating or a traditional heat pump, Eisch says that inverter technology is one of the most affordable ways to maintain a swimmable pool temperature year-round.

For example, if you live in Florida and have a 10,000-gallon swimming pool, Eisch estimates your annual heating costs will be $2,467 with a natural gas heater, $741 with a traditional heat pump, and $434 for a heat/cool pump that uses inverter technology.

As aforementioned, the goal with wintertime swimming is not to be soaking in bathwater-like temperatures while it’s freezing outside. For safety purposes, winter swims are quick, and the heating system is to keep your chlorinated pool water well above the 19-degree Fahrenheit freezing point.

Unsurprisingly, a standard 14-by-28-foot swimming pool is going to be one of the hardest to use effectively year-round, unless you live in a region with a mild wintertime climate.

“You can technically use a large pool year-round, but it’s difficult to cover all its surface area well enough to hold in the heat, so heating bills can climb fast,” says Larson.

Therefore, smaller sizes like a 10-by-16-foot cocktail pool or plunge pool are better candidates for keeping open. Dedicated winter swimmers should also invest in an easy on-and-off cover—or a motorized auto cover—for added protection.

Regular pool cleaning and maintenance are essential, and this upkeep over the winter is no exception.

“Check the filtration system regularly to make sure it’s circulating smoothly, monitor the water chemistry, and plan for power outages—because there’s a good chance they will happen,” Larson explains. For this reason, you should also invest in a generator that can keep the pump running if the power goes out.

Safe pool access is just as important as any other aspect of the pool’s design. That’s why a nonslip surface, excellent drainage, and ample lighting are key.

“Your design should also provide a clear path from the house to the water,” says Neha Gupta, interior designer and founder at NU Curations.

Especially in the wintertime when ice and snow are a factor in your region, make sure you have a safe and smooth exit plan for getting out of the water, bundled up, and into your warm home without any obstacles. And then, of course, remember to cover the pool back up to keep it protected and ready for your next plunge.

Three-time Emmy Award winner and Oscar nominee John Malkovich joins as a new antagonist, Spencer. The second season of Bad Monkey breaks from Hiaasen’s books (with the author’s blessing) to tell a new story that, in a spiritual sense, takes place between the original Bad Monkey novel and its 2016 sequel, Razor Girl. “We took massive liberties expanding certain characters, making the world bigger,” Lawrence says about collaborating with Hiaasen. “My nightmare would be to do anything he didn’t feel was true to Florida or his voice. I’m not overstepping when I tell you he helped come up with the main issue that Rosa’s chasing down [in season 2], because it was something he had written about when he was a journalist.”

Knowing the cast will never get too big, it’s fun to see the smoke clear and [find out] who’s left.”

This is serious business. But it’s still a blast.

#6851. What You Should Know About Exercising When Bone Density Is a Concern

Osteopenia or osteoporosis doesn’t have to sideline your workouts. Here’s what to do, how to modify, and when to back off.

You might have heard that being active is a good idea when you have osteopenia or osteoporosis, conditions marked by lower-than-normal bone density. But when you’re exercising specifically to improve bone health, some types of exercise may be more helpful than others. Before lacing up your sneakers, there are a few things to know.

“When patients ask me what they can do to preserve and protect their bone health, consistent weight bearing and resistance exercises are the first things I recommend,” says Nicole Leung, MD, director of the Osteoporosis and Bone Health Program at NYU Langone Health in New York City. “Bone is a living tissue, so it adapts to mechanical load through remodeling.”

When bone tissue is stressed via physical activity, your body responds by making more bone, helping strengthen the tissue. Exercise also lowers the activity of cells that dissolve and break down old bone. That combo helps preserve or even improve your bone mineral density, regardless of your health status. Exercise may slow or even prevent the progression of osteoporosis; if you have osteoporosis already, it can help you maintain the bone you have and reduce your risk for falls and fractures.

A bone-density diagnosis doesn’t mean you have to give up exercise—but your routine should reflect your fracture risk and health history. Here’s what Dr. Leung recommends for choosing activities, adapting your routine, and knowing when to check with your doctor before making major changes.

As for activities like swimming and cycling, they’re great for getting your heart pumping—but because they’re low-impact, they won’t do much for your bones. That said, feel free to do them in addition to your weight-bearing exercises if you enjoy them.

Lifting (relatively) heavy things works your muscles and helps build bone density. This includes free weights, machines, resistance bands, or body weight exercises like squats or push-ups. Aim for two to three strength sessions per week, and don’t be afraid to gradually go heavier (with your doctor’s okay). Pushing yourself to 70 to 90 percent of your max effort may deliver a bigger bone boost than lifting lighter weights, research suggests.

Having osteoporosis makes you more prone to fractures after falls, but you can strengthen your balance—and reduce your chances for a broken bone—with exercises like yoga, tai chi, or Pilates. “Any exercise where you have to shift your body weight side to side and forward and back can be very helpful for your stabilizing muscles,” Dr. Leung says. She recommends working balance exercises into your schedule a few times per week. Find safe modifications if you’re worried about falling, like holding onto a sturdy chair or touching a wall.

Whether you’ve been exercising for years, are just starting out, or are returning to a routine after a long hiatus, Dr. Leung advises paying attention to your posture, avoiding exercise when you’re overly exhausted, and increasing intensity gradually. Older women who are new to exercise can still do resistance training, she says, but may need to start at a slower pace and under supervision.

Consistency counts. People with low bone density who stay active over several months tend to see the greatest bone-health benefits, studies show.
