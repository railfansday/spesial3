#7004. Has Zegna Cracked the Code to Modern Tailoring?

With its latest jacket, the Falcon, the brand brings some sporty utility to its collection of mix-and-matchable clothing. Artistic director Alessandro Sartori explains how it’s all part of a bigger project to help men dress their best.

Pay attention to the world of menswear long enough, and you’ll notice that certain quandaries come up time and again. A favorite among traditionalists and avant-garde types alike is the fate of the suit. It’s been incorrectly declared dead enough times that the handwringing over its demise has largely shifted to questions of its evolving role in the modern guy’s wardrobe: Who wears a suit, and when, and why?

According to Alessandro Sartori, the artistic director of Zegna for a little more than a decade now, there’s another question we should all be asking ourselves: What exactly is a suit these days?

“Everything we do here is grounded in tailoring techniques and in tailoring fabrics,” Sartori tells me over Zoom from his home in Milan. “But I'm a big believer that a suit doesn't necessarily have to be a combination of the classic top and the classic bottom in the same fabric and color.” It can be comprised of those things, he stresses. But the “modern suit,” as he calls it, can also be an intentional pairing of a different kind of top—a chore coat, an overshirt—with a trouser with a different fabrication. The materials and colors should feel complementary and cohesive but needn’t be perfectly matched.

“This is not only a way to give a new life to suiting,” he explains, “but to dress men differently—and, at the same time, keep the quality, the craft, and the beautiful fit that a tailored piece has.”

The idea is to adapt classic techniques to the realities of getting dressed today. “Many fashion brands offer a nice quality, but is not the handmade quality that we do,” Sartori says. “And there are a lot of brands that are offering beautiful, beautiful quality, but they are quite classic.” Since he started in 2016, Sartori’s overarching project has been to populate the gulf between those two modes of production, making clothes that feel contemporary but are rooted in the top-tier fabrics and handwork that helped Zegna become the powerhouse of Italian fashion that it is today.

The latest addition to Sartori’s lineup is a new jacket, the Falcon. It pays homage to the sort of utilitarian jackets Zegna founder Ermenegildo Zegna might have worn while walking the grounds of what is now the Oasi Zegna nature reserve in northern Italy, at the base of the Biellese Alps. (According to company lore, Ermenegildo imagined those walks as being accompanied by the flight of a falcon.)

Sartori acknowledges that the use case, in 2026, might be a little different. “I don't think that people are necessarily using the jacket walking up in the mountains,” he says. The Falcon is more about embracing a “sporty-chic attitude,” whether you’re stomping around the country or the city, and working within the modular framework that the designer has created at Zegna. You can mix and match it with other pieces—both old and new—from the brand, and you can wear it as part of a “modern suit” or on its own.

The new design is cut from Zegna’s Oasi Cashmere for lightweight warmth, and Sartori calls it “a great addition to our vocabulary.” Buttoned patch pockets give you somewhere to stash your stuff, a bowling collar can be flipped up to shield against stiff winds, and an internal drawstring can be cinched at the midsection for a little extra protection against the cold. Ultimately, it’s a familiar-looking piece. But it’s exceptional in its construction and materials and its design—a streamlined take on the hunting jackets of yore—is as considered as how it’s made.

“Long-lasting life is a key factor for what we're doing, and I don't mean only quality-wise,” Sartori says. “I also mean design-wise.”

The designer, for his part, hopes that the Falcon—alongside other modern classics from the brand—will be the sort of thing that guys wear and reinterpret for years before passing it on to the next generation. And as the brand continues to grow and evolve alongside its customer base, one of his biggest aims is to ensure that each item he introduces remains relevant throughout its (ideally long) lifespan.

“It’s like writing a book, and every season I add a chapter,” he says. “Because the story is going on, is moving with our clients, our community of people, our brand, our family. So many layers are … I don’t want to say ready, but cooking. I see a bright future for the brand, following this principle of walking with our customers and building an indelible aesthetic. I like this approach.”

#7002. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6996. 6 Antique Trends Designers Are Watching for 2027

Above: The kitchen in designer Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat.

Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? In many ways, a great antique shop feels like the antidote to trend fatigue: a space where the true must-haves are hidden in dusty corners, with part of the fun in the thrill of the hunt. Yet, as we round out 2026, there’s no denying the current design cycle is casting off perfection for patina. Antiques are en vogue, and top designers are keeping a close eye on the periods, pieces, and yes, trends, that are poised to find new life in 2027.

“The return of interest in antiques reflects a trend towards more personal, unique interiors, and away from strict modern rooms which can often be cool and generic,” says ELLE Decor A-List designer Jamie Drake. This shift paves the way for more playful interactions between pieces, unexpected layering, and unabashed personality.

Other experts second the motion for more character. “I’m seeing clients really drawn to antiques with more to them than meets the eye: pieces that have a bit of humor or whimsy, or pieces with great provenance,” says ELLE Decor Rising Star William Cullum. Luckily for the amateur antiquer, not all trending vintage styles require a substantial space commitment or glamorous provenance—although an interesting backstory helps. “Antiques are always about a story: where you found them, who owned them, and how that story will continue on after us,” Cullum says.

Ahead, we asked top designers and experts to share the most inspiring antique trends of the moment. From neoclassic mirrors to hand-painted chests paired with modern art, their takes are sure to inform your next estate sale rummage, or inspire you to restyle an old heirloom.

We called mirrored surfaces a major trend to watch for fall 2026, and antique trends have followed suit. Unexpected (or better yet, artfully manipulated) reflections can add rich dimension and movement to any space, but in this category, antiques create a window to the past that’s tough to beat.

“One of the easiest ways to add a sense of history to a room is to include an antique mirror,” says Drake. “Whether you choose a sober and strict neo-classic piece or a more flamboyant baroque one, reflections of the past add depth and sparkle.”

“One trend I’m observing is an uptick in antique dowry chests,” antiques dealer Ali Mahon says. From Pennsylvania Dutch to Swedish Folk and Spanish carved, she’s noticed clients gravitating toward more delicately adorned storage over spare. With thoughtfully mixed furniture (from style to period) becoming more desirable than matching sets, Mahon sees more hand-painted antique surfaces in our future: “With their decorative tulips, leaves, birds, and hearts, dowry chests create a colorful splash in any room.”

ELLE Decor Rising Star Reagan Gail Struble agrees—although delicate designs are not a requirement of the trend, in her book: “Something about a playful wash of color on an awesome old piece of furniture is very alluring, especially when you can see the age and texture of the wood peeking through,” she says. “Hand-painted case pieces add a little punch of charm to any room.”

Luckily, these antiques can be just as functional as they are fashionable. Mahon recommends using a painted chest as a coffee table, at the foot of the bed, or in a space that could benefit from a bit more romance (and a few extra drawers). When styling your find in a modern space, Struble says that contrast is key: try a “chippy Swedish chest of drawers” with an unexpected photograph or painting above it.

According to ELLE Decor A-List designer Nick Olsen, Art Deco pieces are setting the tone for 2027 antique trends—although, if you ask him, they were never out. “I'm particularly inspired by the [art deco influence in the] Gujarat palace in India,” he says. “I'm fascinated by how every element seems to have a wide border: marble floors, carpets, walls of veneer and mohair velvet upholstery.”

The summer of 2014, I was a college student working at a small-town lingerie store that lined its walls exclusively with neutrals. Undressing in front of a guy that same summer, he voiced genuine disbelief over my nude, industrial-strength strapless bra. Pop princess Adela’s exposed bras have become as central to her identity as her electric pink hair. Once left to jiggle freely beneath semisheer cotton, Charli XCX’s chest is now more often displayed in intricate lingerie. Several months back, Saint Laurent’s Spring 2027 front row was littered with boudoir-inspired looks while Prada and Fendi literally sent bras-as-tops down the runway. Bras have come out of hiding.

Somewhere between the “free the nipple” movement in 2012 and the impending #MeToo revolution of 2017, boobs became less of a sexualized spectacle and more of a political battleground. Victoria’s Secret canceled its fashion show as basics-driven intimates brands like Cuup and Arq gained traction for destigmatizing natural shapes. Then, breasts bounced back into cultural focus. The shift might be credited to the climate—conservatism generally propels women to take shelter in traditional femininity, or it is thrust upon them—as well as the twin forces of Sydney Sweeney and semaglutide weight loss. Either way, women got thinner while their chests grew larger—reflecting a beauty standard we hadn’t seen in a generation. Implants entered a new era of demand and dialogue, as celebrities like Kylie Jenner and Megan Fox transparently joined the big-breasted brigade.

But boobs aren’t just bigger—they’re bolder. Gone are the days of basics and bralettes. It’s all about va-va-voom underwire, satin, and lace—even push-ups have returned to the conversation. Still, “there’s a difference between showing your bra and showing off your bra,” discerns Tara Ocello, director of brand marketing at intimates brand Skarlett Blue.

Last month, the recently revived Victoria’s Secret Fashion Show broadcast Jenner strutting down the runway, her 445-cc-moderate-profile silicones outfitted in a record-breaking $50 million bra. Bella Hadid, Candice Swanepoel, and Adriana Lima showed off the iconic mall brand’s finest embroidered balconettes, lacy push-ups, and satin plunge bras. Angels aside, the reintroduction of these runway collections is also indicative of a shift in consumer product demand—a spokesperson for Victoria’s Secret claims there’s “strong interest” in fashion-forward details like lace, satin, logo hardware, and the brand’s statement shine straps. The Very Sexy Push-Up Bra remains the number one of the three mainline bras in its “Very Sexy” category. “Today’s customer wants a bra that performs beautifully and deserves to be seen,” says a Victoria’s Secret spokesperson. “It’s expanded how customers engage with [our bras] as both foundation pieces and intentional styling elements.”

The luxury London retailer Agent Provocateur has been positioning its designs as statement pieces since it launched in the mid-’90s—the last time exposed bras enjoyed prevalence. Creative director Sarah Shotton noticed the trend bubbling several years back on the Glastonbury grounds and immediately returned to the design studio. “We predicted this and designed [for it] about a year and a half in advance. I am constantly thinking, could it be worn as an outerwear bra?” she says. “I think if customers are going to invest in it, they want it to work harder than just in the bedroom. Lingerie as outerwear is just getting bigger and bigger.”

Category transcension is, of course, good for business. Victoria’s Secret reported sales up 10 percent in its second quarter, and Agent Provocateur has also seen revenue growth this year. Eager to capitalize on the movement, contemporary brands like Lioness are now engineering built-in underwear to imitate the effect of an exposed bra. This week, Charlize Theron wore a similar style by Tom Ford to the Governors Awards.

These are bras I’ve never gravitated toward, let alone purchased, because they seemed like impractical additions to my wardrobe. I’ve had to ask myself: Can you wear leopard-print under a white tee? What if the lace breaches my neckline?

Somewhere between Erin Brockovich and Blake Lively’s character in The Town, an exposed bra—particularly a colorful one—became a media shorthand for unsophistication, and more often than not, sexual availability. In simpler terms: tacky. Now, tacky is trending. Along with diamanté-encrusted snapbacks and sky-high peep-toes, straps in dynamic hues are stretched across the décolleté of It girls en masse. Printed cups are peeking out of the necklines. Some fashionistas are forgoing the shirt altogether. “There's a real cultural shift from polished perfection toward something more expressive, unpredictable, and a little undone,” claims Ocello. Or, perhaps a little more human.

This certainly isn’t a trend for everyone. According to Shotton, Agent Provocateur’s biggest sellers are corsets—a style that was once also considered strictly lingerie, but has since sashayed into the light. One wonders if their popularity is a gateway to full bra-baring, especially as GLP drugs become more readily available, and women more comfortable in their bodies. But with a Blue Wave washing in Woke 3.0, how long do boobs—and their bold accessories—have left? In all seriousness, might we look back on this moment with the same disdain we once reflected on early-aughts raunch culture? Only social media will tell. In the meantime, I plan for my collection to keep growing.

#6938. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

Short answer: Yes. There’s a truly pink hair color for everyone. “I have clients asking for all shades of pink, from blush and rose gold to hot pink to magenta,” says hairstylist Alyssa Chapa.

Pink is surprisingly so versatile, so there are plenty of ways to make it work for you. “In my opinion, pink is a neutral. I think of fashion colors as cousins to classic shades,” says Craig. “So if you are a blonde, consider baby pink. If you are a brunette you’ll want to move to darker pinks and fuchsias. Wondering about how your skin tone will look with pink hair? “I’m personally of the opinion that there is no right or wrong shade for your skin tone,” says Chapa, but if it’s really important to you, here are her tips for finding the best hair color for your skin tone. “Start by figuring out your undertones,” she says. “I like using the veins on the inside of the wrist; if you have blue or purple veins then you usually have cool undertones, green veins mean warm undertones, and a mixture of both is a neutral undertone.” Then, it’s pretty easy from there: Match your undertones to the shade of pink, which your colorist will be able to help out with (think baby or icy pink for cool tones, peach or salmon pink for warm tones, that kind of thing).

First things first: Bring reference photos to your colorist—we’ve handily included some pics throughout this story for ya. After all, Adéla’s vivid pink and Stewart’s subtle rose gold hair require very different approaches, so you’ll want to make sure you’re specific about what shade you’re after.

For a more natural-looking grow-out or to keep your hair color easy, “your alternatives are to choose an already muted color like a dusty rose or mauve and let it fade to blonde or brunette, or to invest in an at-home color depositing treatment to maintain your pink until your next salon visit,” Chapa says.

You can help extend your color by washing less frequently, using color-safe shampoo and conditioner, limiting heat styling, and incorporating a color-depositing mask or at-home hair gloss. “I always recommend my clients use a pink-tinted shampoo or conditioner to keep up with the color between appointments at home,” says Claire.

#6883. “It Was Never About Fashion”: Emily in Paris’s Costume Designer Reveals the Method to Her Madness

“Emily in Paris: Fashion Without Rules,” a new show at SCAD Fashion in Atlanta, is devoted to the series’s unique style

Emily in Paris premiered when audiences needed it most. The Netflix original, created by Sex and the City producer Darren Star, offered an escapist fantasy: a young girl trying to find love and success in Paris in October 2020. The show functioned as a reprieve from the pandemic, a dreamy alternative in which we could vicariously live through Emily (Lily Collins) as she gallivanted around the City of Light. It was and still is delectably digestible television.

The love for Emily in Paris was anything but unanimous. Critics wasted no time tearing the show apart, with some targeting its depictions of French culture or claiming the script lacked the bite of Star’s previous television shows. The criticism extended to Emily’s idiosyncratic wardrobe, with some writing that the clothing leaned hard on American stereotypes of French fashion or looked cartoonish, with clashing patterns and bright colors. Costume designer Marylin Fitoussi took these criticisms head-on and doubled down. Emily’s outfits weren’t meant to be couture or designer; they were meant to dress a girl finding her sense of self, expressing her desire to explore the unknown.

For six seasons, Fitoussi curated a wardrobe fit for Emily, a bright-eyed young woman unafraid to try something new, to break the rules. This mirrors Fitoussi’s personal philosophy on clothing: that fashion is meant to break rules, to push boundaries, to provide a space of unrelenting experimentation. Ahead of the sixth season, SCAD FASH Museum of Fashion + Film compiles six seasons’ worth of outfits in “Emily in Paris: Fashion Without Rules,” on view through March 14, 2027. The exhibition lives up to its name; for the designer, clothing is a mode of expression that lets anyone feel free, wherever they are. It offers a character study, tracing Emily’s evolution from a doe-eyed expat to a well-versed Parisian resident. (And worry not, there are also looks from Emily’s chic boss Sylvie—played with devastating elegance by Philippine Leroy-Beaulieu.)

Ahead of season 6, Bazaar sat down with Fitoussi at SCAD Fashion in Atlanta to discuss working with Lily Collins, her swan song for Emily in Paris, and how everyone has the potential for creativity.

Marylin Fitoussi: I don’t recall a contemporary TV show being in the spotlight in this way. Generally, when you think of an exhibition, you think of period pieces or couture pieces. The show is really part of pop culture now!

MF: I was surprised when fashion magazines [criticized the show] at the beginning. I’m not a fashion designer, [but] I love fashion and I love clothes. This show was not about fashion at the beginning. [Emily] is a bubbly character with a very strong personality from Chicago; she couldn’t have come dressed in Comme des Garçons or in Yamamoto. I needed to have this cultural shock. She’s a fish-out-of-water character, and we needed something to catch the audience’s eyes. When you want to show people that you love the culture but you can’t express it because you don’t know the codes, you don’t speak the language, the only way you find it is through clothes. It was never about “fashion.” Sometimes, it’s about creativity, individuality, making something not trendy at all. The audience got that, and fashion magazines didn’t get it, so they killed me.

MF: Many times, I’ve asked myself, “Why does this little gingham suit have so much influence?” It’s quite simple. It was the most duplicated, copied, reposted, fan-arted, and used as a Halloween costume. It touched people because it was for them. The French can have their own version of it, their own concept, but for the entire world, it was this one little girly, timeless outfit with the red beret, the red lips, the red soul.

MF: She read the bad press after the release of season 1, and we had a talk. I said they hated it in France; they find it hideous. [I asked her] “Do you want me to change [it]?” She was smiling and said, “No, we can’t, and we won’t please everybody. We need to keep this strong DNA. We need to continue to have this unexpected silhouette because it’s what catches the audience’s eye.” It’s a guilty pleasure. We can say, I love it.

It opens a way for the audience to see fashion in a different way, where you can mix in an awkward way and free people from this law where you shouldn’t [do that]. Coco Chanel used to say, “Before leaving a room, look at yourself in the mirror and take something out.” I always say, “Put something more on.”

MF: I wanted to present a more elevated version, more mature, more evolved, but still true to what she was and what she is, with an eclectic combination and a little bit awkward. She knows the French rules, but as I do, she finds French fashion so boring. In general, I find that fashion is less and less interesting. Why are fashion and celebrity stylists sourcing more and more from archives? What it’s showing is that now everything is the same. Everything is boring, and I understand why. They need to sell the show! We don’t have to sell anything. We have to sell dreams, illusions, craziness, boldness. People watch the show in groups. Some people say, “I love it,” and some say, “I hate it,” and they have a dialogue and fight and have a good [time]. We love that.

MF: A young designer wants to be famous right away. Fame doesn’t happen, and it doesn’t matter. You need to be happy with what you’re doing. With costume design versus fashion design, you can be much freer. You don’t have anything to sell, and you don’t have to design the “It bag.” If your show is successful, it’s a win-win for everybody. To see that, you need to be true to your vision. You need to be true to your gut feeling, to your vision, to your DNA. Talent is not enough; you need to be at the right place at the right time. Being discovered by Darren Star … he changed my life. At some point, you find the right project for you.

You need to work a lot to take care of every detail. You need to be strong and push the boundaries. You need to try to show a different way to see costumes and clothes, to see that everything is possible…. Style begins when fear ends. You don’t want to please everybody, and I’m always attracted to people who dress in a different way, and I’m never judgmental. To create something is to expose yourself. It’s very vulnerable. It’s brave.

We’ve collectively accepted that a long-term relationship somehow means the end of passion. You spend months or even years learning everything you can about your partner. You’ve gained trust, a strong foundation, and yet, the passion seems to have disappeared with the uncertainty.

“The comfort you worked so hard to build may be what’s subtly killing your desire for your partner,” certified sex therapist Dr. Mindy DeSeta, PhD tells Cosmopolitan.

She explains that over time, we unknowingly become complacent about romancing our partner. All of the exciting unknowns of the beginning of a relationship (Do they want to kiss you? Do they want to come home with you?) eventually fade away.

“You may be intimately familiar with your partner's moves after a number of years, and that's a beautiful thing,” Dr. DeSeta says. “But repetition can dull the excitement of physical intimacy.”

But contrary to what we’ve all learned to accept, this isn’t an inevitable diagnosis for your long-term relationship. It is possible to have both emotional intimacy and sexual passion. You just have to work for it.

“Your sex life will suffer if you do not actively continue to learn your partner’s desires,” Dr. DeSeta says.

You might think that you know everything there is to know already about your partner. But as we get older, our desires change—even if our partner doesn’t.

“Your pleasure is constantly evolving and if you want to continue to have a thriving and exciting sex life, a couple must be able to talk about most recent desires,” she says. “What turned you on at 25 may not still turn you on at 40.”

This is why communicating your desires and feelings to your partner is so important. Sure, you and your partner may be pros at working through disagreements and emotionally supporting each other through life’s changes—but does that energy translate into your sex life? First, in order to feel sexy with your partner, you need to feel sexy yourself. Do something every day that makes you feel sexy and confident,” Dr. DeSeta says. “The foundation of a great sex life starts with each person having a thriving libido.”

This can look like caressing their back as you pass them in the kitchen or holding hands while you watch your favorite show on the couch. Instead of maneuvering around each other, lean into the touch. It signals to your partner that you see them and want them throughout the day. And just the feeling of being wanted can ignite some of that passion from the early days of your relationship.

Most importantly, you should date your partner every day. Dr. DeSeta recommends “Daily Dates” to couples she sees in therapy. This is when you set aside 20 minutes everyday to make your partner a priority.

“All too often long-term couples put their relationship second, so this tune up is always needed,” she says. “During these 20 minutes the phones are silenced, logistical conversations are off the table, and the time is dedicated to emotionally connecting with their partner.”

By doing this, she explains that you slow down the daily hustle and really learn how to reconnect with your partner. You might even learn a few new things about them in the process.

“If we do not make the intentional effort to keep the passion alive, then you are at risk of falling into autopilot,” Dr.Deseta says. We often think passion and emotional intimacy are at odds with each other, but that’s because we think it’s acceptable to simply fall into our relationship without making an active effort to keep our relationship exciting. And no, you don’t have to be constantly ripping each other’s clothes off or feel nervous in order to have a passionate relationship. But you should aim to feel the same deep desire and attraction to your partner that you did when you first started off—if not more.

#6853. What to Eat for Healthier Skin and Slower Aging

A dietitian breaks down the foods, nutrients, and habits that may support skin from the inside out.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

On the quest to age smarter and slower, there’s a lot that your skin care can do. But perhaps one of the greatest contributions lies in the foods you consistently eat. Of course, diet and exercise are the pillars of good health, proper body function, and longevity, but a recent study done by scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) also reveals that 10 specific diets are linked to slower aging. Experts unpack the findings for ELLE and break down which foods you should incorporate or ditch from your diet to help improve skin health.

To understand the connection between diet and skin health, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry, says it’s important to define the notion of slowing down aging. She points out that this particular DZNE study didn’t look at wrinkles or how old people feel. “It looked at biological aging, measured with epigenetic clocks. These read chemical marks on our DNA (methylation) that shift as our cells age. In about 7,500 adults, the researchers found that following any of 10 healthy eating patterns more closely was associated with slower biological aging,” Hosmer explains. The 10 eating patterns or diets included in the study results are Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based. Hosmer says that while the effect was modest, this kind of study shows an association, not causation—proof that diet is the reason for the positive outcome—so it is still a meaningful signal.

Additionally, Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Plan, tells ELLE that no single food has been satisfactorily studied and shown to be likely, on its own, to slow aging. “Of all the predictors of a long, healthy life, two of the best, which can be improved with diet, are muscle mass and strength, but this leads to a controversial conclusion,” says Gilbert. “The best nutritional intervention to increase muscle and strength is eating a high-protein diet. However, many scientists argue that high-protein diets have the opposite effect on longevity. So, if we can’t even agree on that, you can see that science has a long way to go to try and figure this out.”

One stat Hosmer found particularly striking was that over 70 percent of the biological pathways affected were the same across all ten diets. “I interpret that to mean that no single superfood is doing the work, and it affirms that [a combination of] food, diet, and nutrition impacts us on the cellular level. The foods these patterns share are what matter,” says Hosmer, who, along with Gilbert, breaks down the standouts:

Alongside individual foods, Hosmer says certain diet components have the greatest impact on aging. For starters, protein is a must. “As we age, our muscles respond less efficiently to the protein we eat. This is called anabolic resistance, and in women, the drop in estrogen makes it worse,” she explains. Building and retaining muscle isn’t only about strength; Hosmer says it’s our largest site for handling blood sugar and a strong predictor of independence later in life. “Many women eat less protein just when they need more.”

Gilbert adds that while protein is an important part of a balanced, longevity diet, there are some considerations to keep in mind before adding it to your meal. “I believe that because muscle and strength are so strongly associated with longevity and that protein, particularly later in life, supports them,” he says. The caveat, however, is that there is a potential mechanism (which has to do with the amino acid methionine) whereby too much protein could have negative effects. “Many scientists argue that, because in insects and some other animals, less protein along with calorie restriction increases lifespan, humans would similarly benefit. Also, some cite the so-called ‘Blue Zones’, alleged to show that plant-based, low-protein diets are superior. In my opinion, all of these have been shown to be invalid,” says Gilbert.

Fiber is another essential nutrient that you’ll reap from plant foods and mushrooms. Meat, fish, dairy, and eggs contain no fiber, and because our enzymes can’t break them down, Hosmer says that fiber travels intact to the large intestine to assist. Once there, fermentable fibers become food for the gut microbiome; other fibers mainly add bulk and keep things moving. “As gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the cells lining the gut, help keep that barrier strong, and keep inflammation in check. Fiber also steadies blood sugar and helps the body excrete estrogen the liver has already processed, supporting healthy hormone balance. Yet more than 90 percent of American women don’t reach the recommended 21 to 25 grams a day,” Hosmer explains.

Plant diversity matters, too, and not just quantity. Hosmer points to research from the American Gut Project that found people who ate 30 or more different kinds of plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. “That’s because each plant brings its own mix of fibers and polyphenols, and some bacteria have preferences, so a varied diet feeds a broader community of microbes. A broader community does more jobs. It makes short-chain fatty acids that protect the gut lining, helps regulate immunity and inflammation, turns plant compounds into forms the body can use, and even helps process estrogen.” The variety of plants Hosmer adds seems to matter more than any single “perfect” plant.

You’ve likely heard of healthy fats, meaning omega-3s from fish and the polyphenol-rich fat in olive oil. Adding these to your diet can support cell membranes and help settle inflammation.

Also on the list for Gilbert is vitamin D, which he says studies have shown to be inversely related to the risk of mortality. “This does not prove that low vitamin D is the cause, but it is compelling evidence. Although some results are controversial, low [vitamin D] levels have been linked to all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and infection-related mortality,” he adds. Vitamin D is intimately involved in immunity, too. It protects T-cell immune response.

Although Gilbert says, “The only thing all good nutritionists agree on is that there is no one ‘best’ diet or ‘best’ food,” he warns that for most people, minimizing the intake of sugar and refined, starchy grains and increasing protein intake is, in my opinion and experience, the best approach, but, again, different people can thrive on different diets. “As in the examples I gave, many of these foods contain phytochemicals—plant chemicals like polyphenols, flavanols, cyanidins, and others – which affect disease risk or support systems in the body that are likely to serve you well in old age.”

Hosmer notes several other red flags as contributing to unhealthy skin: Diets dominated by refined carbohydrates and added sugars, heavily processed meat, and regular alcohol. “Each of these drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation researchers call “inflammaging,” and blood-sugar instability.”

It’s made to help close the protein, fiber, and plant-variety gaps that so many women are carrying,” she adds.

You don’t need to pick the “right” diet and follow it perfectly.”

She highlights that the DZNE study found few people scored well on all ten diets at once, yet better adherence to any of them helped. What matters is the common ground. Increase your familiarity with, and use of, plant-based proteins. Plant-based proteins offer an excellent package of beneficial nutrients, including fiber (not found in animal products). If you aren’t already familiar, learn how to include whole soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame) in your diet; add beans and peas and lentils to dishes here and there. Nuts and seeds and nut and seed butters count here also.

Build meals around vegetables and fruit. Fill half your plate with them, and choose deep colors like leafy greens, berries, beets, and cabbage-family vegetables. Those colors come from polyphenols, plant compounds that act as antioxidants and feed beneficial gut bacteria.

Make your grains whole and varied. Oats, rye, barley, brown rice, and whole-wheat bread are staples of the Nordic and DASH patterns, the two most consistently linked to slower aging in this study. Try a grain you haven’t eaten before—quinoa? Farro? Millet or teff? They are all cooked the same way—in boiling water, just with different grain-to-water ratios. Count your plants, not your calories. Aim toward 30 different kinds of plant foods a week. Veggies, fruits, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans, peas, herbs, and spices all count. A homemade trail mix, an interesting salad, or a smoothie with a handful of fruit and veggies can add three or four at once.

According to Gilbert, collagen supplements contain very high levels of the amino acid glycine, which he says can balance the potential harms of proteins that are high in methionine.

Hosmer further explains that collagen peptides break down during digestion into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then decides where to use them. “To actually build collagen, it needs enough total protein, plus vitamin C. Vitamin C is a required helper for the enzymes that stabilize the collagen structure. It also needs minerals like zinc and copper. So a collagen supplement taken on top of a protein-poor, micronutrient-poor diet is not going to be optimized,” Hosmer says. “Collagen is also an incomplete protein: it’s low in leucine and lacks tryptophan entirely. It can add to your protein intake, but it isn’t a substitute for complete proteins when it comes to muscle.”

The gut matters here, too. “Researchers call the connection the gut–skin axis. When the gut lining is healthy, and the microbiome is well fed, inflammation throughout the body tends to stay lower, and the skin reflects that. External products can only do so much if we aren’t supporting our skin from the inside,” Hosmer says.

Diet plays a major role in overall skin health and slower aging, but Hosmer argues that it’s not the full picture. To optimize more youthful-looking skin, consider other factors that may be equally as important as diet.

Few garments have been as imitated, reinterpreted, and misunderstood as the mantilla. The iconic lace veil with Spanish roots has endured for centuries. That’s just one of the reasons why The Hispanic Society Museum & Library in New York is opening its new exhibition “Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” beginning November 20. “The understanding of the mantilla in all its complexity is often limited, either by a superficial reading of the symbol itself or by its association with an old-fashioned, rather conservative image of Spain,” López tells ELLE. “This exhibition challenges the perception of the mantilla as an anachronistic relic of the past and reclaims its continued cultural relevance and subversive potential. The exhibition is the second installment in the Hispanic Society’s multiyear fashion initiative, which focuses on reinterpreting its own renowned collection through a new lens. The museum was founded in 1903 by American philanthropist Archer Milton Huntington and has many visual examples of the garment, including Francisco Goya’s famed portrait of María del Pilar Teresa Cayetana de Silva y Álvarez de Toledo, the 13th Duchess of Alba (1762–1802). “By bringing together works of art, fashion, and material culture, the iconography of the mantilla activates a collection of such an extraordinary diversity, through unexpected dialogues between the museum’s different departments and international loans,” adds López.

The new exhibition pulls from many cultural moments new and old, including the “Spanish Craze,” which was a period of cultural fascination with Spain in the United States spanning 1890 to 1930. “During this period, the mantilla became part of a highly recognizable visual language through which Spain was imagined and represented in America,” says López. “Spanish performers who travelled to the United States achieved remarkable success: Raquel Meller, for instance, appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1926, while Concha Piquer starred in From Far Seville (1923).” He also points to the reinterpretation of the mantilla in The Devil Is a Woman (1935), starring Marlene Dietrich.

Fashion naturally plays a role in the show, especially through the imagination of emerging designers, like Arturo Obegero. “Throughout the history of fashion, major designers such as Cristóbal Balenciaga and Óscar de la Renta have drawn inspiration from this [Spanish] visual language, including the mantilla,” adds López. “However, it was a curatorial decision to bring contemporary designers, including emerging voices, into direct conversation with the masterpieces in order to make justice to the mantilla’s enduring influence on fashion to this day.”

The mantilla has been loved (and reinvented) in contemporary fashion at least since the 1940s, when it appeared in numerous fashion magazines. Today, the references are endless and people of all cultures and backgrounds are embracing it. “I think there is something particularly beautiful about the fact that those who have historically been excluded from the dominant narrative, who may have felt unrepresented or even oppressed by these national symbols, can now feel encouraged to revisit this folklore and expand its boundaries to make room for others to do the same,” says López. As the exhibition makes clear, the mantilla’s compelling story is still unfolding.

“Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” is on view at The Hispanic Society Museum & Library from Nov. 20, 2026, through March 28, 2027.

#6814. The 5 Types of Coats Every Guy Should Own

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

A great winter outfit starts with the right outerwear.

Years ago, I was talking to a very menswear-y buddy at a very menswear-y event, and he proposed a theory of outerwear that’s stuck with me: Your coat is your car. Sounds pretty … menswear-y, right? But bear with me here, because I think it actually makes perfect sense, so long as you’re willing to make a couple (major) concessions.

No, your coat cannot help you travel at 75 miles per hour down an interstate. You cannot fill it up with gasoline or top it up at a charging station. It contains, ideally, zero percent steel.

But if you live in a place where you spend more time walking or taking public transit than you do behind the wheel, your coat does indeed become quite car-like. You ensconce yourself in it when you move about the world. It’s the first thing people see when you arrive and the last thing they see when you depart. It shields you from the elements while simultaneously signaling to friends and strangers alike your combined senses of utility and aesthetics. So, yeah, if you squint and look at the situation sideways, your coat is your car. Even if you live somewhere that demands personal automotive transportation, your coat is your second car—unless you’re one of those kooky types who just dart from the interior of a sedan to the interior of a restaurant in nothing but a sweater—for all the reasons stated above.

The point is: Your coat is the most visible and functional part of your winter wardrobe, and you should approach the buying and wearing of said coat with all due respect for its position. Which is exactly why I created this guide. We’ll run through the key styles that every guy should know, where to get a great example (or three!) of each, and how to wear them with style all season long. You won’t find truckers or bombers here—those are jackets—but you will find parkas, topcoats, trenches, and more. All of them will serve you well until the spring thaw, so let’s get into it.

If you’ve been keeping up with Esquire style guides, you’ll know that we’ve covered a lot of ground over the past few years, breaking down everything from the shoe styles you should have in your rotation to the winter essentials you shouldn’t go without. One story that really resonated with readers was about sweaters. In it, we talked about the fact that you can score very good knitwear from a huge range of lifestyle brands these days. We also mentioned the prudence of keeping a few category specialists on your radar. The same advice holds true for coats.

On the lifestyle side, the brands will look familiar to longtime readers. You’ve got Todd Synder and Buck Mason. J. Press and J. Crew. Ralph Lauren and Drake’s and Alpha Industries and Madewell and Billy Reid and Filson ... the list goes on. You’ve also got fashion behemoths like Burberry, which famously started out with the trench coat. And then you’ve got brands that may not just do outerwear but are truly steeped in it. That could be a heritage player like Mackintosh or Schott or Gloverall, a parka expert like Canada Goose, or an upstart label like Wythe, which makes a wonderful range of products but has a cult following for its exceptional overcoats.

As we’ve warned before: This list is not exhaustive. But it’s a good place to start. Now, on to the coats themselves.

This is the dressiest of the bunch, though it wasn’t always. If you’re a real traditionalist, you might have a velvet-collared Chesterfield in your closet to wear with your fanciest togs. Most of us, though, don’t really need something that formal. The topcoat is similar to and derives from the Chesterfield, but it’s less stuffy.

Still, it looks plenty dressed up to modern eyes. It’ll have lapels, which can be notch or peak (for a little extra personality), and can be single- or double-breasted. If you suit up with any regularity, that means it’s likely the most essential for your wardrobe. Look for one made of wool or a wool blend; this is not the time to overspend on synthetics.

Colors are usually pretty conservative—think camel, navy, gray, and black. That might seem limiting, but remember that this thing should be able to play nicely with your whole wardrobe, so opting for a more subdued hue is usually the right move. As for length: Some folks will swear something that hits above the knee looks more modern. We just think it looks off. Go for at or below the knee.

(Oh, and if you’re wondering if a classic polo coat really counts as a topcoat as opposed to an overcoat, well, you might be right to question us. Technically. But considering its cultural associations—rich New Englanders, Wall Street power brokers—we’re giving it a home here.)

Eschewed by many as a bit too fusty and old-fashioned as recently as a decade ago, the raglan overcoat has come roaring back into the spotlight in recent years. We enthusiastically endorse its return.

The style is defined by its raglan shoulder construction, in which a diagonal seam runs from around the collarbone to the underarm, allowing for an uninterrupted panel over the top of the shoulder. Don’t worry too much about the technical details, though. The main thing to remember is that this shape is more relaxed and tends to layer over other garments (a chunky sweater, a sport coat) more easily and comfortably than a traditional “set in” shoulder seam.

The raglan overcoat also tends to be cut wide and long, so you can bundle up underneath it and never worry about chilly knees. It leans more casual than a topcoat, for sure, but you can still wear it with a suit. And it lends polish to something as simple as jeans and a sweatshirt. One of our favorite subgenres of raglan overcoat is the balmacaan, which features a Prussian collar (which looks like a shirt collar instead of a lapel), a “fly front” with a placket that covers the buttons, and buttoned fasteners at the wrists so you can cinch up to keep out the cold.

If you’re into the warm and wooly vibes of the two styles above but simply can’t stand a long coat, the peacoat is for you. Double-breasted and hip length, it’s a shorter—but no less capable—take on the woolen overcoat that dates back about three centuries.

Originally created for sailors of European (and, eventually, American) navies, the peacoat is tough enough to defend from open-ocean winds and sea spray, which means it’s definitely up to the task of keeping you toasty on your way to work. There are a lot of versions out there these days that are done up in frankly wimpy wool blends that might feel nice at first but don’t do the job.

Go for a heavyweight wool like melton, but make sure there’s enough room to layer up underneath it—and keep an eye on the lapels. They should be big. Big enough that you can pop that collar and shield yourself from an incoming gust without feeling even a tickle of wind touch your neck. Plus, it looks cool.

J. Crew Dock Peacoat in Wool Blend with PrimaLoft

Thanks to a quirky little phenomenon we like to call “global climate change,” there’s a very good chance that some of the winter days ahead will feel a lot more like fall or spring. On those days—especially if it’s raining—you should reach for a trench coat or a mac. The two styles aren’t synonymous, but they’re related in both vibe and history, so we’re coupling them here.

Maximalists will prefer the trench, with its storm flap and epaulettes and double-breasted closure. Minimalists will reach for the mac, with its clean lines and streamlined shape. It’s really a matter of preference, because both fulfill the same roles in your outerwear lineup.

They’ll keep you warmish, especially when combined with additional layers. They’ll help block the wind when it kicks up. And although we can’t guarantee you’ll walk away dry if you stand around for an hour during a deluge, they’ll fend off a shower—or a sprint through a thunderstorm—quite capably.

If it’s really fucking frigid, you need a parka. Make sure it’s properly insulated, whether with a synthetic fill or down. And unless you like freezing your literal ass off, get one that’s hip length or longer. You could get something technical and bright that looks like it just stepped off the ski slopes, but we’re more inclined toward rugged-looking styles that take their cues from the military and outdoor exploration.

The N-3B, or “snorkel parka,” is a burly beast that looks absolutely fantastic with a casual outfit and, if you ask us, can be fun with a suit too. (Though if you’re worried about raising eyebrows or breaking a dress code, maybe opt for a topcoat instead.) Something like Canada Goose’s Langdon parka is a little more pared back in looks but overdelivers on warmth, meaning you’ll be cozy in even the worst weather. The trick is finding one that balances form and function so that it looks as though you intended to wear it on the way to the bar—not like you wandered off some snowcapped mountain and forgot to change.

Like the peacoat, the duffle coat is a part of maritime history. It was adopted by the British Royal Navy in the late 19th century and widely issued to British troops during World War II. Thanks to the sheer number produced and postwar surplus, it quickly became a civilian staple and eventually hopped across the pond to become a fixture of the American preppy uniform. Made of thick wool and equipped with a big hood, it’s very warm. And thanks to the large toggle closures, you don’t even need to take your gloves off to fasten it. Is it a little odd? Perhaps. But that’s what makes it so charming.

#6813. The Tutu Is the Tactile Miracle Fashion Needs Right Now

The tutu’s fashion legacy just keeps going.

Distinctly fluffy, feminine, and over-the-top, the tutu has an unrivaled fashion legacy. At the center of it is the tutu: Nicole Kidman recently wore a Matthieu Blazy Chanel dress with a perfectly layered tutu tulle confection of a skirt that would give Carrie Bradshaw a run for her money. Elsewhere, Rosalía, Tinashe, and Olivia Rodrigo have all recently donned the tutu. The look right now reads as more punky Y2K or ’80s couture.

The spring/summer 2027 runways were littered with echoes of the tutu, too. Both Chanel and Dior showcased voluminous skirts with tutu-like shapes; Dior’s were made of cut fabric petals. Rising label Agro Studio integrated ethereal watercolor tutus into its recent collection. And it’s impossible to forget Cecilie Bahnsen’s undying dedication to the garment. Her fall/winter 2026 collection was a tribute to movement and dance and repositioned the tutu as an everyday item versus occasionwear, pairing it with fleeces and down jackets as live dancers wove throughout the venue wearing tutus. “They brought a beautiful femininity and softness to the more utilitarian pieces, creating an interesting contrast,” Bahnsen tells ELLE. “And as the dancers moved through the show, the tutus came to life, adding a wonderful sense of volume, lightness, and movement to the silhouettes.”

In 2026, wearing a tutu is an undeniable endorphin booster. Perhaps it’s deeply and satisfyingly impractical, the exact opposite of wearing a pair of denim jeans and a T-shirt or button-down, the kinds of items that typically dominate capsule wardrobes. “I love the femininity, the sculptural quality, and the beautiful bounce and movement of a tutu,” adds Bahnsen. “There is something so joyful, light, and full of positive energy about it.”

Given the tutu’s broad history, it’s no wonder vintage tutus are experiencing a bit of a revival too. According to Haile Lidow, founder of Lidow Archive, there’s been a surge in demand. “The tutus I can’t keep in stock right now are lighter and more ballerina-focused,” she tells ELLE. “I have this David Dalrymple for Patricia Field white tutu skirt that was made to be like the famous Carrie Bradshaw title sequence look, and that piece has been renting out constantly. Like Bahnsen, she sees an inherent joy in the silhouette and also links the current wide appeal to the extra textural feeling. “There’s something very tactile about tutus, and in an age of digital-everything, I think we’re craving pieces that seem to reach out and ask us to interact with them IRL,” she adds. In a season of pared-back dressing, the tutu’s frothy excess feels like a joyful rebellion.
