#6975. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6970. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6906. When to Arrive at (and Leave) a Party, According to the Pros

Once again, we find ourselves on the verge of party season, that most wonderful (and exhausting) time of year. Fear not; we’ve gathered the best and brightest party-goers to answer all those questions: how to be late enough to be fashionable and early enough to be polite, courtesy of those who know best.

ELLE Decor Rising Star Romilly Newman, the chef and food stylist who towers her chocolate cakes as high as her well-decked greenery, thinks “30 minutes is the sweet spot,” she says. “As a frequent host, I'm always running behind and secretly wishing everyone would arrive late. Whether it's running to buy bags of ice, ironing the tablecloth, or an extra braise in the oven.” She warns, however, “An hour late is when it starts to get into dangerous territory—as the host is most likely waiting to serve a hot meal.” But, when it comes to dinner, when it’s time to sit down, it’s also time to stay down: “Cocktail parties are come and go, dinner parties are a nightlong commitment.”

Ivy Getty, a stalwart of guest lists from Milan to Manhattan, suggests arriving a bit earlier: “The best window is 15-20 minutes, so as not to rush the host. I don’t really think of these as rules: they feel like common sense.” She adds: “Obviously, if you are going to a funeral or graduation you arrive early—or on time at the latest.”

Ryan Nordheimer, a New Yorker who plans elaborate monthly party potlucks, suggests that “the sweet spot” for those headed to dinner parties is 5 to 15 minutes after the host tells you to arrive. He adds, “please never be early. Those 5 extra minutes always come in handy as the host.” If it’s a cocktail party, however, particularly one on the longer side, “you can arrive right on time to 30 minutes late.” Plus, he says, “You can always text the host the day before and ask—just don’t ask them the night of.”

Then, when every interesting topic has been exhausted, and any talk about the weather has completely dried up, there’s the question of departures. Newman says, “I like to think of a dinner party like a play in three acts. There's the mingling, the meal, and the lingering. When the host starts clearing the table and blowing out candles, it's time to pack up.”

Nordheimer has a more scientific approach to closing time: on “a weeknight, I like to have people leave around 10pm,” he says. He’ll go an hour later on weekends. “But every host is different. The host reserves the right to kick people out; just start yawning a lot to make it obvious,” he notes. Clear signals—and choosing guests who can pick up on them—is key.

As for how a host should plan, “You should have most of your food done by the time guests arrive,” says Newman. At the end of the day, though, as long as a guest arrives ready to keep the energy high and be gracious to their host, they can’t go wrong.

The size of your sofa cover will depend not only on the size of the sofa or couch you want to protect, but also on the properties of the fabric itself. Leigh says that with a stretchy fabric, you may want a cover with measurements slightly smaller than the dimensions of your sofa for a tighter, more fitted look. For sofa covers with corner ties that allow you to tighten or tie bows, the fabric may have a bit less give, so you'll want to allow for a little extra room in the cover size.

Some styles lend themselves to being covered more effectively than others. Leigh says that sofas with thinner or rolled arms tend to look nicer with a slipcover than those without. You can also choose between having a skirt on your sofa cover or showing off the legs of your furniture, depending on your aesthetic preference. “I like when they go right to the floor because a skirt cover is a much more traditional, covered look,” Leigh says. “If you have a little bit more of a modern style, having a slipcover that stops at the legs can be a fresher, kind of more updated take.”

Natural fabrics, such as chenille or linen, are typically a great choice when selecting a sofa cover fabric. For family rooms or homes with pets, indoor/outdoor fabrics like Crypton that have been treated to repel dirt and debris are safer bets for those wanting something stain-resistant. The stretchiness of the fabric also plays a role in selecting a sofa cover. "If you wanted something a little bit more durable, canvas or cotton would be a really great material on there," Leigh says.

It's always a good idea to pay attention to the care instructions for whatever cover you buy, as fabric care really depends on the individual material. For example, a heavier cotton material can likely withstand more washing. “I would say only wash when you really need to, just like with a really nice sweater,” Leigh says. “Don't wash it every time you wear it because washing breaks down fibers. So, it could reduce the longevity of your sofa cover. Most are designed to be removed and washed. So if and when you feel like 'oh, you know what, it's looking a little bit dingy, it's time,' that's the time to do it.” Additionally, it's essential to consider the size of your cover, as larger ones may necessitate the use of a commercial washing machine.

Additionally, the cover may wrinkle over time, especially with linen, which softens with each wash. “Linen does get a little bit wrinkly, but I think that's kind of the charm of a slipcovered sofa, having that kind of rumpled, you know, 'someone just took a nap here' look,” Leigh says.

“An oatmeal linen slipcover is that very quintessential, relaxed coastal style that you see a lot of slipcovers used in, and I think it's a classic choice that you'll never go wrong with,” Leigh says. Don't run away from patterns either, though—Leigh is seeing a rise in patterned upholstery in the design industry, and it's a chance to push the envelope and have fun with your design choices. “A cover can be a great way to, you know, try out a pattern without necessarily committing to it forever, because if in five years you say, ‘okay, this pattern was fun, but I'm ready for something different,’ you can just change the slipcover as opposed to having to get an all-new sofa,” Leigh says.

#6894. 5 Fall Trends Designers Think Will Still Look Good Next Spring

Above: A hallway view of Eric Egan’s Milan apartment.

If any season has the power to inspire a complete aesthetic overhaul, it’s fall. Nature’s changing palette inspires us to follow suit, as each year our mood boards make new space for jewel tones, cozy textures, and a bottomless candle budget. Autumnal trends are so alluring, it almost feels like a risky time to make long-term design decisions. But with the right consideration, that seasonal inspiration can translate into choices that look good year-round.

This fall in particular, designers are urging clients to trust their guts—making sure they see themselves in the spaces they create, rather than blindly following the latest fads. “For me, a trend has to be authentic or it’s just noise,” says designer Chad Dorsey, who’s eager to ditch the spare minimalism of years past for looks with more personality. ELLE Decor A-Lister Kerry Joyce seconds the motion: “By prioritizing personalized storytelling, a space becomes evergreen, evolving as smaller objects are swapped out over time.”

But an expert nudge in the right direction never hurts. This fall, top designers are gravitating toward creative color-drenching, cleverly concealed storage, tactile upholstery, and rooms built around art. Read on for their takes on the fall 2026 trends worth investing in, plus tips on building (as Dorsey puts it) “spaces designed to be lived in, not looked at.”

Fabric and upholstery are trending ultra plush and textured for fall, but when thoughtfully integrated, they feel more timeless than seasonal. “Design is shifting toward how a space feels, not just how it looks,” says Joyce, who’s seeing an increased popularity in fabrics like mohair, velvet, cashmere, and alpaca hair. “This focus on texture does more than elevate a room's aesthetic,” he explains. “It creates a sensory experience that comforts the soul.”

Start your bedroom redesign this fall and your space will strike the perfect balance between fresh and lived-in by spring. Between client requests and a personal home build, Amber Lewis has bedrooms on the brain right now. One trend takes the lead in her eyes: “I love matching the walls to the bed upholstery and bedding so the whole room feels wrapped in one warm color family. It’s calm and peaceful, and that enveloping feeling works in any season.”

Luckily, this cozy trend doesn’t require a complete room overhaul: “The easiest place to start is the bed itself. Reupholster your headboard in a pattern you love, or drape a vintage suzani across the foot of the bed,” Lewis says. “One move like that changes the whole mood of the room without starting over.” To bring in a little dimension and pop, she recommends adding a singular statement, like a lone striped pillow.

If your primary suite needs a more holistic update, Lewis recommends creating a small sitting area. Sourcing vintage chairs and small sofas for primary suites this fall has given Lewis a new perspective on where bedroom design is headed: “True sanctuaries that feel layered, personal, and built for actually living in.”

Color drenching isn’t going anywhere for fall 2026—or long after, according to Dorsey. When choosing a shade for a room, he finds that any color works harder when it’s layered and intentional. Color capping—a term coined by Benjamin Moore referring to painting a room in multiple tones from one color family—can help keep a space from feeling flat or overwhelmed beneath a statement hue.

According to Dorsey, the staying power of color drenching goes hand in hand with the shift away from spareness and toward “collected” spaces: “Neither is really a ‘look;’ they’re about living with things you love, which is why they’ll carry well into spring.”

Designer Regan Baker says that cleverly hidden space solutions are also on the rise. “It could be a TV that slides behind a commissioned art panel, a Murphy bed tucked into a wall of millwork or that serves as a desk when it's stored, or a peg wall that’s a place to hang a jacket one minute and a standing desk the next,” she says. These solutions are tailored to her clients’ spaces (rather than tied to a season or palette), which gives them staying power.

This concept is an easy way to turn the corner you’ve never known how to style into your favorite part of the room. “In an existing home, look at the awkward spots, like an alcove or the wall around a fireplace, and treat built-ins there as a design moment instead of an afterthought,” Baker advises.

If there’s one built-to-last fall trend most designers can agree on, it’s added eccentricity. “Homes are steering away from the sterile, overnight look of a showroom delivery,” Joyce says. “The defining philosophy now focuses on layering modern staples with antiques, secondhand finds, travel mementos, and new pieces that carry a sense of patina.”

Dorsey describes this style as “collected rather than prescribed,” where a high-low mix of vintage and custom elements gel together. And while it may feel loftier than a trending, sold-out piece, Baker says that most clients start with an artistic anchor, then build around it: “We’re designing more homes around a piece of art than around a color trend. That might be wallpaper by local designers, a commissioned piece, or even a palette pulled from a lichen-covered rock found on the property.”

One key takeaway? Curate like a collector. Your reward may be a space that never goes out of style.

#6884. This Breezy Los Angeles House Has “a Little Funk to It”

Designer Mike Moser creates a Santa Monica home that spans decades, styles, and influences—without sacrificing fun.

Above: The living room features a custom red marble coffee table, sofa, and side table by Mike Moser Studio. The wicker chairs are from Matahari; the vintage mirror is from A. Tyner Antiques, and the carpet is from Lawrence of La Brea.

When designer Mike Moser first saw the Santa Monica house, it hadn’t been touched since the ’90s, and no one had lived in it for over a decade. He knew it would require a full gut renovation. But Moser saw that as an opportunity for his clients—UTA’s chief communications officer Cassandra Bujarski, media executive Jason Bergsman, and their daughter—to get exactly the house they wanted. A place that’s at once a little bit Spanish, a tad preppy, and very Californian.

Moser, a Los Angeles-based interior designer who opened his eponymous firm in 2018, met the clients through their real estate broker. The agent, a friend of Moser’s, called him in while the couple was in escrow, asking him to help them imagine what this crumbling house could become with the right eye and guiding hand. They ended up moving forward with the house and hiring Moser to lead the renovation.

Bujarski’s parents were antique and art collectors and she had a vision for the home as a place that would honor that past and help her pass that legacy down to her own child. “Whatever we do,” Moser remembers her saying, “I want it to really feel collected and eclectic.” Other than that guideline, Bujarski and Bergsman gave Moser free rein—almost.

“That was really her only directive,” he says. “She was like, just have fun and play and be colorful and try new things.”

Moser leaned fully into this eclecticism, pulling references that span eras, regions, and styles. He cites 1920s and ’30s Los Angeles houses, Mexican architect Luis Barragán, Milan’s Villa Necchi, and hacienda style all as influences in the house’s design. In the dressing room, Moser added a “menswear, haberdashery, Ralph Lauren feeling,” he says—fitting, as Moser once worked for the brand. He calls this potpourri of inspiration an “amalgamation”: “It’s easy breezy California and we wanted to add a little funk to it.”

Los Angeles is the place to mix and match styles. It’s a city where modern mansions share blocks with Tudors and Craftsman; Spanish Colonials bump up against French Normandy houses. “The beauty of California, especially L.A. architecture, is it's a melting pot,” Moser says. “It's all really about these weird artists and creatives back in the ’30s, ’40s, ’50s that just were marching to the beat of their own drum. So I was like, let's do the same.”

Moser and his team gutted the Spanish-style house and stripped it down to the studs. They had to modernize all the internal systems, bring in new steel and windows, open up the layout to create more “breathing room,” as Moser calls it, and replace the laughing plaster walls. Despite the major work required to make the house livable, Moser still wanted to honor what it used to be. He added Saltillo replica floors and was able to keep the original wooden beams in the living room. In the dining room, he added new wooden beams, calling back to those in the living area, but had artist Nicke Valle paint them with swirling yellow and red spirals.

If the structural elements pay homage to the space’s origins, the furniture is tailored precisely to its new residents. Moser needed to find stylish places to store Bujarski’s collections. “I had an itch to scratch with the casegoods on this project. I was really obsessed with armoires,” the designer says.

There’s a baroque cabinet from Giannetti Home in the entryway, an antique credenza in the dining room, and two vintage Deco Swedish dressers that act as nightstands. “Each one of these cabinets got to be a curio,” Moser says. The dining sideboard houses her china; a vintage Swedish armoire in the family room holds her mother’s Murano candlesticks. In the dining room, the dishware mounted on the wall is vintage Majolica, all with a seafood theme, that Moser and Bujarski hunted down—at auction, on eBay, in antiques stores—in the six months before the install. Throughout the home, “there’s a sense of reveal,” Moser notes.

“I always want to be a chameleon,” Moser adds. “I live as a joyful person. The first thing I say to a client when I take on a project is ‘If this isn't fun, we're doing it wrong.’”

If you want to make a statement in your kitchen using the budget-friendly quality of paint, a high-gloss finish could be the right move. Here’s how the pros recommend pulling it off.

When you’re considering a high-gloss finish, think of how it can be used to complement the rest of your kitchen. If you’re leaning toward trending darker palettes—like chocolate browns, emerald greens, and deep plums—then a high gloss will help bounce light around the room and make undertones more noticeable throughout the day. But if you’re doing just fine with a neutral backdrop, then a high-gloss will only make that look brighter. Either way, “high gloss” is paint speak for highlighter.

“The way that light reflects off a high-gloss finish adds a brightness that can make the space feel more expansive, even in moodier tones,” says Ariel Fischer, who is also a principal at Noa Blake Design.

Fischer and Goldberg would likely pick a darker shade on kitchen cabinetry for this effect, which Goldberg notes works on ultramodern panels and more ornate fronts. But Jen Levy, interior designer at Surrounded by Color, says it’s OK to go lighter—while looking up. “I love a high gloss ceiling in a kitchen,” Levy says. “My team and I see it used either way, but my preference is to have this paint treatment punctuate a lighter, brighter space.”

Paint is often regarded as DIY-friendly, but choosing this finish probably shouldn’t involve you rolling up your sleeves. “Hire a professional,” Levy says. “High gloss involves a lot of prep work to look right.” Since the results should almost resemble a mirror, Davis echoes this advice. “If a surface isn't perfectly sanded or smooth, high-gloss will show those imperfections,” she says. “It will ultimately take away from that perfect finish you’re after.”

As far as the best location for high-gloss paint, Davis has one strong preference. “High-gloss cabinets, from a practical standpoint, are forgiving in terms of wear,” she says. “You can easily wipe fingerprints, spills, and food right off the cabinet with a microfiber cloth.” Keep in mind, though, that chips will be noticeable, so this probably isn’t the right finish for busy households with young children.

As you determine the remaining details of your kitchen with a high-gloss paint in mind, remember to keep it front and center. Everything else in this space has to complement that sheen in order for the effect to be successful. “In a rustic kitchen, or one where natural wood is prevalent, high-gloss paint may clash with the overall vibe,” Davis says. The opposite is also true: A modern kitchen with too many shiny surfaces will feel like a carnival fun house.

To pull your kitchen together, seek out countertops and flooring with a matte finish alongside lighting and hardware that has a slight sheen. The goal is to find ways to ground the room, even as you center its high-gloss boldness. “I would love to have a client who’s daring enough to do a high-gloss kitchen,” Davis says. “Nevertheless, high-gloss paint can still have a timeless quality, while still having a big impact on the room.”

#6837. These 8 Common Heating Mistakes Could Be Costing You More Than You Realize, Experts Warn

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Staying cozy is a top priority in winter—whether you're curling up on the sofa or getting snug under the covers. But turning up the heating isn’t always the answer: some everyday habits can leave your home colder while pushing your bills higher.

From setting the thermostat too high to neglecting to bleed radiators, small heating mistakes can affect how warm your home feels and how much energy you use.

To help you stay comfortable without wasting energy, we asked heating experts to share the most common mistakes—and the simple fixes that can make your home warmer and your bills easier to manage.

When the chill sets in, it’s tempting to whack the thermostat up a few notches for a quick blast of warmth. But this common habit does more harm than good. “While it won’t heat your home any faster, what it will do is waste energy and therefore waste money,” say the heating specialist experts at Heatforce. “Each degree above 66 degrees Fahrenheit can add a whopping 10 percent to your annual heating costs.”

The smarter approach is to keep your thermostat at around 66 degrees when you’re at home and awake, then drop it by four to six degrees overnight. That way, you’ll stay comfortable without sending your bills soaring.

When arranging furniture, it’s easy to prioritize style over function—but placing bulky pieces too close to your radiator can drastically reduce its efficiency.

“Make sure that furniture and curtains aren’t obstructing this area of your home, as heat needs to be able to circulate freely,” says Amthal Karim, head of design at Furniture & Choice. “Where space allows, move sofas, armchairs, and other large pieces of furniture away from radiators to help warm air circulate more effectively around the room.”

Scott Byrom, a home energy expert at The Energy Shop, adds: “A large piece of furniture placed against a radiator can absorb a significant amount of the heat it emits, meaning you're paying to warm the back of your sofa rather than the room. Pulling furniture just six inches away allows warm air to rise and move freely, and you'll feel the difference almost immediately.”

Warm air can escape through gaps around doors, windows, and letterboxes, leaving rooms colder and forcing your heating to work harder—which can push up your bills over winter.

“Many older homes with hardwood front doors will experience worse drafts in the winter as they are susceptible to expanding in heat and contracting in the cold, meaning gaps around the frame will become even wider,” warns Oliver Novakovic, head of innovation at David Wilson Homes. “To combat this, invest in a draft exclusion runner to go along the bottom of your door. You can also purchase lengths of gap sealer to run around the frame, preventing cold air from blowing in around the door frame.”

Draft blockers, letterbox brushes, and simple sealant strips are all inexpensive fixes that can help a home retain heat far more effectively.

If you’re relying on an old heated blanket to stay warm, check its age and condition. It may no longer heat as effectively, leaving you tempted to turn up the thermostat and spend more on heating.

“Older electric blankets or those without overheating protection can pose a fire hazard,” says Adele Brennan, home electrical buyer at Vonhaus. “However, there are newer, innovative electric blankets that come equipped with an automatic shut-off function.”

A newer blanket with an automatic shut-off function is a safer choice, but using one to compensate for a cold room won’t fix the underlying heating problem.

It might feel indulgent to keep the heating running from dawn until dusk, but it’s an expensive habit that can quickly inflate your energy bills. “Instead, schedule your heating for convenient times,” says Stephen Hankinson, heating expert at Electric Radiators Direct.

“For example, if you like being comfortable as you get ready in the morning, program your heating to switch on just before you wake up and then off again by the time you leave.”

This way, you’ll enjoy warmth exactly when you need it, without wasting energy. Just don’t forget to adjust your schedule when the clocks change.

It's easy to focus on simply switching radiators on and off, but regular maintenance is just as important. Bleeding your radiators is essential for keeping them running efficiently. “After a while, pockets of air can get trapped within the radiators, causing cold spots and preventing hot water from flowing through,” explains Hankinson. “The usual sign your radiator needs bleeding is that the top section is much cooler than the bottom.”

To fix this, switch off your heating system, use a radiator key to open the valve, and keep a towel handy to catch any drips of water. Doing this at least once a year will keep your heating even and effective.

Drying clothes on radiators is common in colder, wetter months, but it can stop heat from circulating around the room. Your heating may then need to stay on longer to reach a comfortable temperature, using more energy and pushing up your bills. A clothes dryer in a well-ventilated room is a better option.

“The bigger issues are its contribution to mold and dampness within your home, as well as the inability to heat your home, so you're left with a damp, cold house throughout winter, which is costing you,” says Andy Simms from MyBuilder.com. “Instead, using a clothes drying rail or taking advantage of brighter, milder days to hang your washing outside or open windows can make a real difference. And when drying clothes indoors, using a dehumidifier is effective in keeping moisture low.”

It might feel inconvenient to book in a boiler service—especially if it means rearranging your day to be at home—but skipping it can be a costly mistake, as minor issues could go unnoticed, which could reduce its efficiency.

For peace of mind, it’s best to get your service done in autumn. That way, any small problems are caught early, and you’ll avoid the risk of being left without reliable heating during the coldest months of the year.

“Having your boiler serviced annually is a great action to take to prevent boiler breakdowns, as well as improve its efficiency. It helps keep your boiler operating at maximum efficiency, consuming less fuel to generate the same amount of heat,” says Ian Palmer-Smith, appliance repair expert at Domestic & General.

#6825. My Life as an Asylum Seeker in the U.S.

I came here 11 years ago to give my daughter a better life. I believe in the American dream, even if the American government doesn’t want me here.

I want to tell you about some things that I think you can only understand when you are an immigrant.

The first, and most important, is that emigrating is not something you do without a reason to leave. I will tell you mine. In Venezuela, when I would take my daughter to school, she had to ride lying down on the floor of the car. At any moment, someone might knock on your door, point a gun, steal your phone. It was safer to have her flat on the ground, below the windows. She was a great student at a private school, and that is what it took to get her there. I challenge you to imagine being a father and seeing that little girl, not even ten years old, huddling for safety.

That was my reason for leaving, but it is not the reason I and my family have been allowed to stay here. We are asylum seekers, and there are strict rules around qualifying for asylum in the United States. The fear of being mugged is not enough. But political persecution is. In the year after the 2013 election of President Nicolás Maduro, when life in Venezuela was very difficult, the country erupted in protests. I was among the protesters, and I was detained and separated from my family for a week. We decided that when we visited my sister in Miami in 2015, we would request asylum.

This is surely something you only understand as an immigrant: the specific, and confusing, rules and requirements and procedures of the American immigration system. People who are born here can take these things for granted. They don’t understand how much of your life can be dictated by paperwork and how the paperwork your life most depends on always has the most boring name. When we got to the United States, the application for asylum I filled out and turned in to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, a part of the Department of Homeland Security, was called Form I-589.

According to the government, it should have taken at most five months for the application to be processed and to get an interview, which would allow USCIS to evaluate if my family should be granted asylum.

Our wait began when I turned in that form. That was the start of the first countdown clock. You think about it every day, because for those five months, you cannot work. When they are up, you can fill out another form, Form I-765, to apply for a work permit. But in the meantime: Five months… four months… three months…

We were fortunate: We had savings. Miami was expensive, but we were getting by.

As the clock ticked down, we had a decision to make. We had family in Miami, but it was too expensive for us to stay for the long term. If the process dragged on much longer, we’d run out of money. We decided to move north, to Orlando. We didn’t know anyone there, but it was affordable. That’s where we would wait out our chance at the American dream.

When we moved there, the newest iPhone was the iPhone 6s. A nuclear deal was finalized with Iran. Barack Obama was president.

Eleven years later, we are still waiting for an interview.

Right now, when many people here think about immigration, they think about ICE—men in the streets with guns, killing people in Minnesota, or Maine, or Texas. I worry about ICE every day, but what has defined the past decade of my life is the waiting. Maybe if I can tell you about it, you will understand something, even a small part, of what it is to be an immigrant.

In Orlando, we were alone. We moved in December, a difficult month. Christmas is a big holiday in Venezuela, and there the family is always together: cousins, siblings, uncles, aunts, grandparents. Our first Christmas was just the three of us.

I dutifully filled out Form I-765. I understood the work permit would take about two weeks to arrive. Once I had it, another countdown: It would be good only for one year. In that time, though, I could get a job and also a driver’s license, which would make our lives much easier. And surely, we thought, a year was enough time for our interview appointment to come through.

Instead of two weeks, the permit took three months. Three more months of stretching our savings. There were desperate moments. We sometimes had to go to churches to get food so we wouldn’t starve. We wondered if we should go home, live with the risks in Venezuela. Finding resolve in these moments is hard. Having a young child motivates you, but you also see the challenges they face. My daughter was nine years old when we arrived. She had anxiety attacks when she first started school, which was in a different language and organized much differently from her school in Venezuela.

When my work permit finally arrived, we were so happy. This card with the faded flag and my picture and the red border—we felt that we could sustain ourselves.

It wasn’t so easy. My English is not perfect. It isn’t now, and it really wasn’t when I started looking for jobs. I’m an engineer by training. I worked at a telephone company in Venezuela. When I first started working here in the United States, I worked overnight, I cleaned bathrooms, I cleaned kitchens, I washed cars. I’m not trying to denigrate those jobs; they were the first jobs that I could find. But I could not get paid well doing them. After we had been here for a year, our savings ran out. We were lucky to have family and friends who could help us. But we kept going to food pantries. I always tried to make sure that my daughter and my wife did not go without food. I often didn’t eat enough myself.

After we had been here for a few years, my mom came to the United States to visit me and my sister. It was the first time she had seen me in four years. I was so skinny she cried.

I did some English classes online, but they did not really work. Here is another thing you understand when you are an immigrant: When you have to start to speak on the street, it’s very different from the lessons that they teach you in class. Really, construction job sites were my classroom. With more English, I became more confident I could find a job in my field.

Before I could do that, my work permit expired. I applied for a renewal, and I got another one. Another year. Another countdown until the day I could, hopefully, make my family’s case.

By then, President Obama had left office, but before he did his administration made work permits valid for two years. That was what my next renewal got me—a little more time to work, and save, and wait. But it also made me think the government might expect us to be waiting two more years.

Meanwhile, Donald Trump had been elected president. I wasn’t blind to his attacks on immigrants, but my family and I were following the rules, working hard, just trying to survive. And Venezuelans, historically, hadn’t immigrated that much to the United States. We didn’t think the attacks were about us.

But things were changing. More and more people were coming from Venezuela and from other countries in South and Central America. My family thought of ourselves as three people looking for safety, but we could tell we were becoming something different: a problem.

We started to see news that the people who were waiting for asylum interviews were waiting four years. Later, it became five years. And then, as you started to talk to people, they tell you that they’ve been waiting six years or they’ve been waiting seven years.

It was hard to imagine how we could possibly last that long, but in 2019 I saw a listing for a job in my old profession. It was with a major telecommunications company. I was hired to join a team that climbs up telecom towers to do maintenance and upgrades. Back in Venezuela, I had supervised this kind of work.

My daughter was in high school by this time, and I would think about her when I was up there. When she was little, she would tell me she wanted to be an engineer. I told her, You don’t have to be an engineer just because I’m an engineer. It could be scary being up so high. We have harnesses, and something like parachutes, and we’re always connected to something in case we fall, but we recognize that we can’t get too comfortable, because any error could end our lives. Still, there was something special about being able to see for miles, this place that held my family’s future stretching out in every direction.

Most of us on the crew were new, many of us immigrants. Our boss was impressed at the quality of our work. He asked us once, How is it that you guys are able to do this kind of work not having done it before? I told him we were trained. And also that when you have this need to survive, you have this need to get ahead, you end up finding a way.

For years we had been saving, and Covid made it possible for us to save much more. The pandemic, strangely, was good for my family. I was an essential worker, and I received bonuses and other incentives. I never stopped working. At the same time, Covid exposed the political problems I protested in Venezuela, which made the pandemic even worse there. It was a reminder of why we had left.

And the house was a reminder of home. In Venezuela, people move less than they do here. Neighbors stay for years, for a very long time. The importance of a house there, like here, is that when you own it, no one can remove you from it. It really meant something to feel that feeling in America—like we were beginning to live the American dream. My daughter, in her way, had even become a typical American teenager. Our new house was about forty minutes from where we had been living, so she had to change schools. She sulked.

In 2023, the Biden administration extended work permits from two years to five years. I think probably they did this because, after the pandemic, everything was chaos. I knew it was a sign that my family might be waiting unimaginably longer for our interview, but when I renewed my permit in 2024, after living life in a five-month increment, then one-year increments, then two-year increments, five years felt like forever, like a clock it was hard to imagine reaching zero.

I won’t tell you that it all came crashing down when President Trump was elected a second time. It’s not just that Orlando wasn’t like Minnesota. There was no crash. It was a gradual decline in freedom and a gradual increase in fear.

Simply, here is what began to happen this past December and January, when things got bad: You’re driving on the highway and you’re followed by an undercover cop. Sometimes it’s for a few minutes, sometimes it’s for ten minutes or more. Sometimes they pull you over. Sometimes they detain you.

In Florida, many law-enforcement agencies collaborate with ICE, so almost anywhere you go is a risk. I drive a lot for work, and I see enforcement in exits and rest stops and Wawas, in school areas where you have to go slow. There are police or highway patrol or border patrol on the turnpike down to Miami, and on State Road 417, and on Interstate 4. You can be referred to ICE if you owe money for a toll, or if you don’t stop for a stop sign, or if they see that your driver’s license says that it’s temporary. Unlike most people, I like working more than fifty miles from home—it means I can stay in a hotel, which means less driving.

Going to work, or for essentials, is nearly all we do. When my family needs groceries, only my wife goes or only I go. We can’t risk both being picked up, because of my daughter. As for my daughter, she sometimes has my sister—a lawful permanent resident who moved to Orlando after we did—drive her to school.

There are a lot of people in my situation in this part of Florida, so we find ways to help each other. We have chat groups, maybe fifteen hundred people total, that warn each other about high-risk areas. And I am a member of an organization called the Asylum Seeker Advocacy Project that has helped us understand our rights and how to be safe.

Thank God, I have never been stopped. But my wife and I each keep a binder in the car just in case. We know it might not be worth a lot, but we carry it with us. It contains all of our paperwork, the forms with boring names that are proof we have a right to be here. Our immigration paperwork, our asylum applications, the approvals for work permits that we’ve gotten before, the asylum receipts and claims. Everything we might need in case one of us is detained. You have to basically see if the documentation convinces the police officer to let you go. But it is up to luck. It is up to God.

Sometimes a risk is worth it to feel that you are still living.

Once, I took a trip to a bar with a few friends. Three beers and an Uber home. It was so scary, but we did it.

The gym, four days a week, at 4:30 a.m.—before law enforcement is on the road. Once or twice a week, when her schedule allows, my daughter comes. She has the certifications to be a personal trainer, so she’s my personal trainer. There comes an age, though, at which children don’t really want to spend that much time with their parents, so she just tells me what to do and goes to do her own exercise. I still like having her there. I’ve lost about forty-three pounds, roughly twenty kilos, over the last year and a half. This time by choice.

Then there was the World Baseball Classic, in Miami last March. I love baseball. When Venezuela reached the semis, I knew I had to go. We left at 3:00 a.m. for the four-hour drive down. Venezuela beat Italy in the semis, so we stayed for the final. Of all the teams we could have faced, it turned out to be the U.S.A. The game was in the evening, and it ended late in the night. We drove directly back to Orlando.

Venezuela ended up champions, by the way. It was like vengeance, because the United States knocked us out in the quarterfinals of the last WBC, in 2023. And to top it off, my favorite player, Wilyer Abreu, hit a home run in the championship game. He’s from Maracaibo, just like me.

We have an ongoing joke with the people we know who are also in limbo. We’ll casually refer to “when we were treated like U.S. citizens.” Now we’re treated like criminals, but in other times, in other administrations, we could make a life without worrying so much, the way that we do now. Still, my family does our best to live like U.S. citizens. We follow the rules. I would not have a job, for example, if Immigration had not given me a work permit with which to do that job. I know, because for eight months, I did not work—I went hungry. We pay taxes on our house. I pay taxes on what I make at my job. I’ve always paid. I’ve been here eleven years now, making a life, paying taxes to the government, but I still am here with no status. Who is the criminal?

I guess it’s not really a joke. I can tell you that if you were an immigrant, you would understand.

Since things got really bad in December and January, they haven’t gotten much worse, but they also haven’t gotten better. We stick to our routine: work, groceries, school.

Recently, one of the places that I was going to have to service for my job was a police station, and I told my boss that I could not do it. I told him that he should change me out for a different job, and that if he didn’t I would stay at home until I could be assigned to a different site. He was able to find something safer for me to do. He is a very understanding man. Several of us are in the same situation, and the company has been very supportive.

So many people down here are like that. In my experience, the politicians do not make a country. The people do.

And so through everything, my feelings toward this country have not changed. I will stand on the fact that this is a great country. I’m very thankful. The situation in Venezuela was not good. Some people tell me that things are better there now, since the U.S. detained President Maduro. I’m not hopeful. I am grateful for the peace we’ve had here. I came for a better life for my family and for my daughter. We bought our house; we have saved for our daughter’s future.

Eleven years is far too long to live someplace and still be made to feel like an outsider, let alone like a criminal. That is the thinking of someone who takes home for granted. But I am an immigrant. One thing I understand is how to extend grace to a place that is foreign to me. I have nothing against the U.S. I am grateful for this country.

At the end of last year, for the first time since I have lived here, the government made work permits shorter. Now they last only eighteen months. The wait has not gotten any shorter, though. We’ve seen no signs we’ll get an interview anytime soon. I was very lucky to get a five-year permit when I did. Right now, my countdown runs until 2029.

If there is one thing I am certain Americans who were born here do not understand, it is the immigration system itself. I have been telling you about the past decade of my life and our excruciating wait for our asylum interview. I have not told you yet that that is not the end of the process.

After our interview, we will wait for a decision. Officially, it should take only two weeks. I know it will take longer. It will be a long time still that we live with the question that, for eleven years now, has been hanging over our heads: What if we do not get to stay? But in a way, after all this time building a life here, the uncertainty of that question has lost some of its power. If I’m honest with you, when my daughter is ready to graduate college, I can tell you that I could die in peace, because she’s my only daughter, and what I wanted most for her was a good future.

A long time ago, she changed her mind about being an engineer. After she started taking college classes, she decided to study something called media design. She’s learning how to create applications, like for phones. She graduates in May.

I still cannot believe that when we got here, she was just nine years old. It’s painful for me, as it is for any father, to look nostalgically back at when this grown woman was my little girl. But it also fills me with joy. My wife and I have spent every minute of every hour of every day of all of the time since she was young counting down—to the exhaustion of our savings, to the end of a work permit, to an interview. You persevere through these things for your children for a simple reason. Children should only ever have to count up.

#6779. Go Inside Miu Miu’s Handbag Atelier In Tuscany

Twenty years in, the brand’s matelassé leather remains a luxury calling card.

March 4, 2006, was perhaps like any other day in the mid-aughts. Myspace was the top website in the U.S., while Beyoncé’s “Check On It” was No. 1 on the Billboard charts. Cult-classic teen mermaid drama Aquamarine had just premiered the night before. And in Paris, during the fall/winter fashion shows, the date would prove to be the unexpected start of a new era—not just for one brand, but for fashion as a whole.

At the 18th-century Left Bank restaurant Lapérouse, Miu Miu presented its first-ever show in the French capital—now its permanent runway home—signaling a crucial differentiation from its sister brand Prada in Milan. Paper-thin knits, ultra-mini dresses, peplum jackets, chunky platform wedges, and mismatched-lace oxfords walked the intimate runway, on models adorned with white eyelashes and poppy-red lips. On the handbag front, an experimental leather-making technique was introduced, one that would become Miu Miu’s signature and a staple of aughts aesthetics: matelassé.

Originating in early 18th-century Marseille, the quilting technique was traditionally used in bedding or upholstery. In Miu Miu’s collection, it was developed in the form of a leather handbag—the Coffer—a slouchy shoulder tote in chocolate brown with a twisted handle and a bold metal clasp. A counterpoint to the youthfully eccentric clothes, the bag itself was a subtle debut, with only three shown on the runway and in only one colorway. Nonetheless, it was an immediate hit, carried by Anne Hathaway, Lindsay Lohan, and Isla Fisher in its early years. For the relatively young but increasingly iconic brand, a calling card was born.

Twenty years since that collection, Miu Miu has transformed its matelassé technique into a rainbow of colorways and style offerings. The seemingly seamless Wander and boxy Arcadie styles boast prime spots in the brand’s portfolio of leather goods, especially when it comes to the matelassé iterations—just take a look at the brand’s 2026 campaign starring Gigi Hadid.

The handbags come to life in Miu Miu’s leather goods workshop in Tuscany, the architecturally award-winning headquarters called The Garden Factory, built in the Valvigna region near where Miuccia Prada’s husband Patrizio Bertelli grew up, and designed by Guido Canali in the mid-1990s. Completed in 2015, the building itself is a physical manifestation of the Prada Group’s minimalist aesthetic.

The Garden Factory’s nickname derives from the property’s one-to-one ratio of building to greenery, a complete balance of cool steel and native vines, and plenty of glass windows to bridge the two. Inside is row upon row of accessories—66,000 leather goods and 77,000 pairs of shoes—carefully filed from the collections over the years to serve as both record and inspiration. Instead of season or collection, the cabinets are organized by product category, and matelassé has its own section.

Despite its creation 20 years ago—or perhaps in part because of it—the very tactility that makes Miu Miu’s matelassé leather so appealing is hitting a peak in current luxury collections across the board. To a consumer, the in-person experience was always a factor, but in the Instagram age, brands are turning to hyper-visual signifiers of craftsmanship to keep up with the market. Be it ASMR-inducing sequins, fluffy feathers, slick latex, or impossibly buttery leather, touch and feel appeal, even through an iPhone screen, is increasingly important. To see a matelassé handbag is to wonder how it feels, and to feel one is to experience its pillowy, permanent spring, an effect somewhere between plissé and origami. That effect is intentional, yet the product itself is softer in practice. That, too, is intentional.

The quilted technique is achieved by layering elastic Lycra with thin padding and nappa lambskin leather, sourced from Miu Miu’s tannery in France. Strategic patterns are stitched onto the layers and then the Lycra is removed, allowing the leather and padding to fold back into itself without losing the valleys created by the stitches. Thus, the matelassé effect comes to life. The smaller the product—like a card holder—the harder it is to craft. 3-D modeling is never used. Leather products are always assembled by hand, an important standard for the brand, and inspected manually by experts who know every detail of the bag’s design.

How does one become an expert in Miu Miu bags, you may ask (besides simply being a loyal fan)? Prada Group has its own leather goods academy where craftspeople spend six months learning about the brands’ luxury practices and standards. Even after those six months, it takes five to 10 years for a craftsperson to know how to make an entire bag themselves, start to finish.

From anywhere in the building, you have a view of the gardens outside, along with 45-degree-angled ceilings that let light in without creating too much heat. Stations are bustling with artisans, most of them women, in teams of two. They wear white lab coats emblazoned with the Prada Group logo.

The building’s minimalism, it seems, isn’t a lack, but an open space for creation to take place. And like the windows bridging steel to vine, or the archives linking past and present, or even the stitched valleys of a matelassé handbag, Miu Miu has mastered the spaces in between.
