#6973. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6964. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6957. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6951. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6932. Report: Global Ransomware Attacks Rise 23% in Q3

Global ransomware activity increased 23% in the third quarter of 2026, with organizations in North America and Europe accounting for the largest share of attacks, according to cybersecurity firm NCC Group.

The company's Cyber Threat Intelligence Report Review of Q3 2026 recorded 2,865 ransomware victim claims, up from 2,325 in the previous quarter.

Despite the quarterly increase, attacks declined 26% month over month in September to 821. North America recorded the most attacks that month with 390, followed by Europe with 211.

The industrial sector accounted for 29% of quarterly attacks, while Qilin remained the most active ransomware group for the sixth consecutive quarter, recording 365 victims. The Gentlemen followed with 356, and INC Ransom ranked third with 109.

The report highlighted a July cyber attack against a small UK power generation facility that forced operations to stop for approximately four days.

Although the incident did not affect the national electricity grid, it demonstrated how cyber attacks can disrupt physical infrastructure.

The security researchers said the incident was consistent with techniques previously associated with Iranian-affiliated campaigns, including attacks against internet-exposed industrial controllers.

However, the UK government has not formally attributed the attack, and the techniques used remain unconfirmed.

The report also identified growing security challenges as organizations connect traditional IT networks with operational technology (OT) systems for remote maintenance and centralized management.

The firm recommended network segmentation, stronger privileged-access controls, and continuous monitoring of OT environments.

The research found that more than 40 vulnerabilities affecting enterprise applications, VPN appliances, e-mail gateways, and management platforms were added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog during the quarter.

The report examined Gunra, a ransomware-as-a-service operation that has targeted 53 victims across multiple industries as of Q3.

According to the cybersecurity company, Gunra exploited vulnerabilities CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 in internet-facing Fortinet devices to gain access to corporate networks.

The group also used stolen credentials, session hijacking, and administrative tools to move between systems before deploying ransomware.

Other emerging ransomware groups included Deadlock and Storm, with the latter claiming 78 victims by the end of September.

Researchers also observed initial access brokers advertising compromised VPN, Remote Desktop Protocol, and SSH access, alongside access to cloud identities, source code repositories, and security management platforms.

The quarterly review also highlighted BraZetsu, an AI-enhanced Windows malware framework that evolved into a specialized initial access toolkit.

According to the report, BraZetsu uses AI models to automate victim profiling, analyze compromised environments, and identify valuable corporate systems.

The framework searches for enterprise software, financial data, digital certificates, and cloud credentials, helping operators identify compromised access that can be sold to other cyber criminals.

The cybersecurity firm also identified risks involving legitimate AI agents, including prompt injection, excessive permissions, and unintended access to external systems.

The report cited incidents involving U.S. and Australian government systems as examples of agents accessing resources beyond their intended scope.

Researchers warned that these incidents do not necessarily involve malicious intent but can create consequences resembling conventional cyber intrusions.

#6927. Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

#6919. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6821. Martine Rose Is the New Creative Director of Balenciaga

An appointment the fashion world is eager to celebrate

Fashion fans rejoice: Martine Rose has officially been appointed creative director of Balenciaga following yesterday’s exit of Pierpaolo Piccioli. This appointment represents a welcome departure from the circulation of familiar names for these creative director roles and symbolizes a shift back to an edgier Balenciaga established by Demna. She begins November 16.

Rose was rumored to be up for the job when it became available after Demna’s departure to Gucci in 2025. And it’s a homecoming; when Demna was appointed to Balenciaga as creative director in 2015, he brought Rose on as a menswear consultant. In her three years working there, she helped to shape the look of his very distinctive vision.

Rose was born in South London in 1980 to a British Jamaican family. Her mother was a nurse and her father was an accountant. She spent her childhood around slightly older relatives deeply immersed in ’90s rave subcultures. The symbolically rich clothing choices shaped her early aesthetic way more than any Paris runway could. She studied fashion design at Middlesex University, where she discovered an affinity with menswear. Rose began creating early, opening the small T-shirt line LMNOP with a friend in 2002. She launched her namesake label in 2007 and began showing under the umbrella of Lulu Kennedy’s fashion incubator program Fashion East in the early 2010s. That’s where she began gaining traction in niche fashion circles.

With her own collection, Rose elevated her memories of clubs—and the clashes of the different tribes within them—for a fashion audience, offering revisions of those shiny shirts, baggy pants, and anoraks. She is known for subverting archetypal garments, distorting proportions, and invoking unconventional fabrics like satin and lace. Her work nods to subcultural heroes who used fashion to convey identity, like the Lo Lifes and Dapper Dan. She can shrink a pair of pants just enough to be a little awkward and pair them with a sports jersey and loafers with ultra-extended toes in an ensemble that somehow makes total sense. Through her lens, the mundane becomes complex, thought-provoking, even extraordinary.

Demna caught on to what Rose was doing and brought her on to develop Balenciaga menswear following his appointment in 2015. She worked with him for three years and helped influence his highly recognizable men’s aesthetic, one that often featured the exaggerated shoulders, boxy cuts, and logo experimentation that she already favored. Her work at the luxury house brought attention to her own label, buoying its renown in fashion. She has since produced several high-profile collabs with brands like Nike, Supreme, and Clarks, and she counts Timothée Chalamet, Rihanna, and Hailey Bieber among her devotees.

Balenciaga is having a bit of an identity crisis. The couture house was founded by Cristóbal Balenciaga in 1917 in San Sebastián, Spain, and later reestablished in Paris in 1937. Often referred to as an “architect” of fashion rather than a designer, Cristóbal is remembered for a vision rooted in shape, volume, and silhouette. His creations were both sculptural and fluid, with iconic looks including the “tunic” and “sack” silhouettes. His legacy was carried on by Michel Goma, Alexander Wang, and most notably, Nicolas Ghesquière and Demna.

In 2025, amid an industrywide reshuffling of creative directors, Balenciaga appointed Pierpaolo Piccioli to its helm, tasking him with interpreting the house’s legacy of sculptural elegance without alienating the streetwear superfans Demna courted. It was a difficult job; Piccioli left just 16 months later. Rose’s appointment shows that the house is looking to reignite some of Demna’s subversive edge. But zooming out, her return is undeniably a celebratory one; she is beloved by fashion and streetwear fans alike. It feels in alignment with the excitement around Grace Wales Bonner’s appointment to Hermès. The current roster of creative directors at big luxury houses is mostly white and male; these are two women of color whose work has been quietly shaping the fashion landscape without always getting their due.

#6795. What to Wear to an Interview for Every Industry and Budget

Looking for a job can be a grueling marathon of resumes, cover letters, and HR acronyms—and that’s before you ever get into the room with your would-be future employer (or their AI proxy, as may be the case). Once you’ve secured an interview, the finish line may feel tantalizingly close, but the race is far from over. Before you get the chance to wow them with your articulate and highly considered insights into your personal strengths, weaknesses, career goals, and salary expectations, however, you’ve got to make a good first impression, and that starts with what you wear. So what, exactly, should one wear to a job interview in 2026? Aside from “probably not pajama pants,” the right answer depends on the job. Despite the loosening of office dress codes across the board in recent years and the rise of remote work in the post-pandemic era, every industry has its own answer to that question, from the traditional suit and tie to “whatever makes you feel the most creative.” For advice on getting the right look for the job, we consulted a bunch of folks who ought to know.

“The interview and the job are very different things,” explains Atlanta-based personal stylist Joshua Davis. “A tech founder might wear a T-shirt to a meeting with a VC, but even if all your research says the office is pretty casual, you should still go more conservative for the interview.” Davis suggests researching the company on LinkedIn to see how employees present themselves and even perusing their individual social media accounts for further insights into their style and presentation. “People expect that a lawyer will arrive in an E-Class and wear a black suit. A trial attorney might, but an attorney who specializes in tech IP will usually dress more like their client,” he adds. “The same is true for most bankers, consultants, and accountants who are client-facing – they have learned to research their client and make sure their image is usually only slightly more conservative than the person they are trying to sell to.”

Whether you’re applying at a disruptive startup or a firm with centuries of history, your clothes are an important means of determining how well you’ll fit into the corporate culture and represent the company to the world. “We get a fair number of calls from HR departments that have found a great candidate, but they don’t feel like they can fully recommend that person because of their wardrobe,” Davis says. “The most common reason is that a candidate is dressing too casually or they don’t dress for their body. To translate, that means too sloppy or too sexy.”

At the most formal end of the spectrum are law firms, and the longer a firm has been around the more traditional its dress code is likely to be. “Law firms in New York are still conservative workplaces and interviewees hoping to land a position as an attorney should still wear a suit with leather shoes,” says Jessica Chin Somers, Esq., a managing director at Kinney Recruiting, an NYC-based legal recruiter. “For men, the uniform of a blue suit with brown shoes or a gray suit with black shoes exists for a reason, but more important than color is the fit.” Aside from that, Chin says, there’s only one big rule to remember: “Lawyers do not show up to interviews wearing sneakers, no matter how “cool” you think the firm is or how expensive the shoes.”

Unless it’s a firm specializing in crypto, the finance field’s dress code remains similarly conservative. “First impressions are important, especially for a job interview in the finance industry, and the candidate needs to convey a sense of seriousness, competence, and trustworthiness,” explains J. Daniel Chi, Ph.D., the chair of the finance department at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas. “The dress code has relaxed quite a bit over recent years, however, as a job candidate, unless the firm gives clear instructions on dressing casually, dressing formally is a safer bet.”

The tech world’s reputation for preferring hoodies and sneakers over suits and brogues turns out to be well deserved. “What I see with a lot of different [tech] companies is a more casual approach to being at the office,” says Sam Fricker, the head of recruiting at Philo, a streaming service with offices in the Bay Area. “Because our company is bi-coastal, employees who are based on the East Coast sometimes are sometimes dressed more formally,” adds Maryland-based Philo Recruiter Alexis Fagin. “Departments may differ as well, like finance teams might require a little bit more of a buttoned-up look for meetings, for example.”

At Toast, a tech company serving the restaurant industry, things are similarly relaxed. “We encourage employees to wear whatever makes them feel most comfortable and confident to do their jobs, and the same goes for those interviewing,” says Toast's chief people officer Mike Gutner. “That said, our recruiters will suggest something in the realm of business casual. The bulk of our interviews are virtual at this point, so I’m sure I’ve talked to more than a couple of applicants in formal tops who are also wearing sweatpants and slippers below the camera. I won’t blame them; I do the same from time to time.”

At the other end of the spectrum are creative agencies, whose commitment to out-of-the-box thinking extends to personal presentation. “[Your] experience and portfolio far outweigh [your] presentation,” says Matt Johnson, the founder and chief strategy officer of LA-based creative agency Haymaker. “At the end of the day, we’re investing in the person and their abilities, not their appearance, so I’d say dress to represent your best and most true self. The more authentic you can be, the better.”

“NGOs value humility and authentic alignment with the cause, and your outfit should communicate authenticity, cultural sensitivity, and reliability,” says Sam DeMase, a career expert at ZipRecruiter in NYC. In other words, the safest play at your NGO interview is business casual, i.e. a blazer and button-up shirt with trousers or dark jeans, or a sweater, a collared shirt, and dress pants or chinos. Depending on the role and the organization, you should also steer clear of anything too flashy or obviously expensive-looking, the better to reinforce your focus on the mission.

Jobs in media—a range that covers everything from journalism to social media management to advertising planning—are extremely diverse, as are the dress codes at media organizations, but most media companies fall on the more casual end of the spectrum. “You want to wear something you feel genuinely comfortable in so you can focus on the conversation and be yourself,” advises Rich Kleiman, the CEO and co-founder of Boardroom, a sports, business, and entertainment network. “But comfort shouldn’t come at the expense of fit. Whatever you wear, make sure it fits well and feels intentional.” Despite this latitude, Kleiman cautions against confusing casual with careless, and advises investing in alterations to nail the fit. “Sometimes, investing a little in your presentation is worth it, especially if it helps you walk into the room feeling confident.”

Dress codes can vary widely between academic institutions and the departments within them, so the best course of action is to act like an academic and do your research, says Jocelyn Coalter, senior director of career and leadership development at St. John’s University in NYC. Beyond that, she advises candidates to err on the side of formality when in doubt. “It’s a lot easier to remove a blazer that you may not need than to put one on that you didn't bring,” she says. “I also recommend professional yet comfortable shoes for academic interviews, as there’s often a component that involves meeting different people on different parts of campus, or getting a tour.”

#6790. Can Hermès Make a Watch as Iconic as the Birkin?

I arrived in Miami expecting to see the latest watches from Hermès. But as representatives of the historic Parisian luxury house led a group of us through the Rubell Museum, there was no sign of the timepieces. After passing through rooms showcasing Basquiat, Keith Haring, and Anselm Kiefer on the walls, we came into a room that looked more like an art classroom than a luxury-watch launch. Instead of being shown new editions of the H08, which Hermès has been slowly sculpting into an icon, I was handed a paintbrush.

Present in the room was Cuban American artist José Parlá, along with tables covered in paint, brushes, and scraps of paper torn from street advertisements. On the walls hung canvases Parlá had already begun and which would serve as the starting point for our own creations.

Delighted by the surprise arts-and-crafts, I happily donned my apron. Later that day, Hermès would unveil two new versions of its distinctive H08, the sporty model it debuted in 2021 and has been carefully building up. The latest pair introduced into the catalog both feature ceramic bezels, one in blue and another in white. They share a shimmering fiberglass case coated in aluminum that gives the watch the look of a tumultuous, gray sea.

When it was first released, the H08 garnered immediate praise from the collector community and broader market. Such rave reviews out of the gate are nearly unheard of for a completely new collection. The H08 is defined by its vaguely rectangular case with soft, sloping edges; the straight lines of the watch are contrasted by a round concentric dial. Paired with colorful, complementary rubber straps or the occasional bracelet, the H08 is a collection clearly intended for an active lifestyle without veering into the realm of the tool watch.

Hermès is a Goliath in the luxury world with a massive multibillion-dollar business across leather goods, handbags, and silk scarves. But amid all of its exceptional offerings, watches were never a primary focus; they were something one might add in while buying a Birkin. The H08 is an opportunity for Hermès, a maker of icons, to establish one in a category where it’s still establishing its reputation.

Inside the museum workroom, I set about the task of customizing my canvas. I chose one reminiscent of a rich, red sunset. I sorted through the sheets of torn paper that resembled craggy mountaintops, glued them down, and began to paint. I felt like I was transported back to my childhood art classes.

I realized that this was not simply an activity to fill the time but was actually a sophisticated metaphor for how Hermès views the creative process itself, especially as it applies to the development of pieces like the new H08s. The work is collaborative, iterative, and builds on the concept of what came before to create something new. The base is familiar enough to collectors at this point, but new colors and approaches round out a growing army of H08s so everyone can choose their flavor.

“We understood that our way to make this line grow would be to animate it and go deeper into materials,” Laurent Dordet, CEO of La Montre Hermès, the house’s Swiss watchmaking subsidiary. “It’s part of the playground of H08.” While the H08 already comes in a host of colors and accents, from bright teals and oranges to blues, greens, and yellows, the new bright blue and white variants bring a fresh, almost resortwear vibe to the collection.

H08 has given Hermès a foundation to build its limitless creativity on top of. The fundamental design hasn’t changed at all, but Hermès has continuously renewed it through new colors and materials. Hermès has played with satin brushing, DLC or colored PVD-coated titanium, and carbon composites coated with graphene powder. There are also skeletonized versions of the watch as well as ones with a chronograph (or a stopwatch function). As the H08 was always intended to be the brand’s far-reaching sport offering, opening up Hermès to new clients and lifestyle applications, the collection quickly became the vehicle for such innovation.

But even if Hermès isn’t primarily a watch brand, the house’s world-class artisans never passed off second-rate design. And the playfulness that pervades the house is essential to the watches too. “That’s what our clients expect from us. Hermès is obsessed with making a beautiful object that will bring comfort and pleasure to its owner,” Dordet says. “We are not obsessed with having something fun or playful every time. It's a balance between style, elegance, sobriety, subtlety, colors.”

Later that evening, at Hermès’s Miami Design District boutique, I encountered another expression of the company’s creative imagination: a smaller version of Jean-Simon Roch’s kinetic sculpture Mysterious Mechanics, which had appeared at the industry’s flagship trade show Watches and Wonders earlier in the year. Its elaborate system of ropes, pulleys, and moving frames was a theatrical interpretation of mechanical timekeeping, part of the broader cabinet of curiosities that informs Hermès’s creative universe.

As I stood before Mysterious Mechanics, I realized it would be all too easy for Hermès to tap into this same type of whimsy to create an H08 that was loud or multicolored in some obvious embodiment of abstract art. But that just isn’t how Hermès works. The brand understands that a signature watch is forged over generations. Look at a model like the Rolex Submariner—one of its most valuable assets is that the base design is largely unchanged over 70 years. There’s a beautiful juxtaposition between the very serious, capital-L luxury done at the highest levels of capability and concept, and the fun-loving, whimsical approach to how Hermès creates. Perhaps that’s why the brand has endured for nearly 200 years. And also why the H08 may just be its next iconic creation.

He doesn’t have a phone, dances to vinyl records, pays at diners with cash, and rents Barbara Stanwych movies (“tapes,” he calls them) from the public library for his wife.

Naturally, Harry is drawn toward watches, both for his personal use as items to steal. Along the way of The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, there’s a cornucopia of watches and Easter eggs for collectors to appreciate: Director Noah Segan meticulously selected a watch to fit every character’s personality. It matters deeply to Segan whether a character is wearing a vintage Rolex Explorer 1016 or a modern Hublot; and the movie even gets deep into the inner workings of timepieces. A pawn shop owner, played by Steve Buscemi, deems a Patek Philippe “fugazi” and shows Harry the lack of jewels in the movement. Watches, as totems of an analog world imbued with personal and cultural history, aren’t just a detail, but a vital motif.

She challenges the norm. I’ve also always been a big believer in trying to be a trendsetter rather than a follower. In some of these moments, I would find it difficult to make a mood board because the references weren’t there. That was where I would say to Kim, “Trust me.”

For Season One, we started reaching out to brands, and Jean Paul Gaultier was [the first to say], “Let’s do it.” The minute we had them, the whole narrative of the show changed. They went deep into the archives and started sending these looks that hadn’t been worn in 20 years. It was amazing. We were sourcing vintage and archival pieces on the side, but we really focused on Jean Paul Gaultier because everything else compared to that just looked sad.

When the images started circulating from the first season, a critic that I respect wrote that the fashion was “kind of obvious.” Although I disagreed, I understood where he was coming from. Maybe Kim wearing a Mugler suit that accentuated her body and her curves was something we had seen her wear before. This season, I wanted to do something really different. I wanted to go less out-there with the colors and a little less predictable with the shapes and the styling. I picked designers and pieces that weren’t obvious choices, and I think it showed growth from last season. We took it somewhere else completely.

For hair and makeup references, I would go on Pinterest and pull old Helmut Newton shoots because I wanted it to look very timeless. I think that was the key. Everything looked … not minimal, there was definitely no minimal… but very ’90s-based and gorgeous.

Kim loves Madonna. There were a lot of old Madonna images for her platinum hair and makeup, a lot of which was done by Pat McGrath.

I think I’d made a point this year that I wanted to take it down a dark route. I almost wanted Allura to be like, “I just had a shit experience in the first season My man’s left me. I’m pissed off. I’ve gone away, and I’ve come back, and I’m serious now.” So everything’s much more covered up. There are darker layers. She’s experimenting. When I was going through the [recent] runway shows, everything was very pink and quite eclectic, so we mainly went with archival pieces. I used a lot of really rare Yohji Yamamoto, Comme des Garçons, and Lee McQueen.

There was a Margiela skirt suit [from the Spring 1994 collection, Retrospective] and it was just very kitsch. It was a long skirt with a suit jacket and was paired with a black leather baker boy hat from Hermès. We were in a bidding war with a collector in Japan. When we were filming the scene, someone in production or maybe Ryan [Murphy] had mentioned they wanted to tone down the hats for this season. But I got that memo after styling the look, and I was adamant that the look needed the hat. I begged. And then she sat at the table wearing a hat and I could see everyone was like, “How is she getting to wear a hat?” I just felt like the look needed it. It took [Kardashian] out of her normal visual stance and how she usually portrays herself.

Comme des Garçons is very destructive and not really tailored to fit a woman’s body in the way [Kardashian] usually likes. And we got her in [a gray jacket and long lilac-gray column skirt] and worked it around her body. She never once made any comments. She would just look at herself for a second and be like, “Cool.” And then go on set.

I have to let Kris Jenner take credit for [Elizabeth Taylor’s 1969 Scorpio cape, worn by Kardashian in Episode 1], because that’s her cape. She would come to set all the time and once had a conversation with Ryan about the fact that she has this cape. He was like, “That’s it, we need it.” From there, we shipped in a $10 million diamond that was most similar to what Elizabeth Taylor was wearing [in original imagery]. It was flown into L.A. in someone’s pocket, and we had it on set for like five days with Kim’s security. It’s a piece of history. It’s really special. Each day, it would go to Kim and then back in her car home because no one wanted to take responsibility for it.

In general, a lot of my choices in clothing and costume referenced old Bond films where the women were super sexual and curvy and had Brigitte Bardot hair. There was glamour, sex, opulence, money. The outfits were completely inappropriate and not really wearable in everyday life. But if I’m going to watch a show, that’s what I want to see. I want to be like, “What the hell? What is that?” There were comments about Kim’s clothing being inappropriate in the workplace, but that’s what it should be. It’s fun.

#6777. Shooting Matt Damon in the Desert and Leaning Into Discomfort

It was a beautiful day to be indoors. In barren Lancaster, California, the temperature had hit the mid-90s, and the feels-like figure in my weather app topped triple digits. We were professional. We were stoic. But at a certain point, you just give in to the heat of the desert. At a certain point, you wilt a little.

Matt Damon, mercifully, never did. Damon may be the least-debated pick in GQ Men of the Year history—he’s still riding the billion-dollar wave of The Odyssey, one of the great cinematic accomplishments this century. Damon was also, by some distance, the most unbothered man on that desert set, and all while being the only one wearing heavenly black tailoring (a peak-lapel Norton & Sons jacket, and glorious mohair Celine pants). It was Bourne-esque. Odyssean, even! Three decades into being a film star, Damon has familiarity with endurance, both creative and literal. In his cover story, he walks our Frazier Tharpe through the physical cost of making Christopher Nolan’s epic, up to and including a night in Scotland on which he didn’t sleep at all. The piece is a portrait of a movie star who still treats the job as a job, most of all when it is inconvenient, unfair, or uncomfortable.

Discomfort has been something of a theme for me this year. I will confess that I knew 2026 would be an administrative ordeal from the moment I accepted this new role at GQ. There was a move from London to New York (including a cat, whose opinion was not consulted). There was a home to find. And there was the discovery that the land of the free has impressive disdain for a grown man with no credit score. None of it was enjoyable. All of it, I think, is good for me.

I have the good fortune to work with many brilliant editors and writers. A recurring note I like to give is: Where is the tension? Without tension, there’s no story. I am not a confrontational person, but I find confrontation on the page irresistible. I want you to stumble, in every issue of GQ, onto something uncomfortable, or uncomfortably new. A magazine that agrees with you from cover to cover is a magazine that you have already read. And I would hate to do that to you.

This issue does not let you off lightly. After some life advice from an honest-to-God astronaut who went deeper into space than anyone that’s ever lived, you’ll read about Chris Rock’s unvarnished thoughts on divorce, discomfort, and reclamation, and Andrew Garfield’s very real anxieties in playing Sam Altman, the complicated man who’s been described as the Oppenheimer of our time. From there, we hang out with the men risking the unknown to get hot on peptides, which is, for better or worse, the defining male-vanity story of 2026.

A few months ago I started putting my email address in these letters (adam@gq.com). You’ve been generous. You’ve been thoughtful. A handful of you have been unhinged. But one note showed up way more than I thought it would: NO POLITICS, PLEASE.

I understand the impulse. I know These Modern Times are stressful, and wouldn’t it be nice if GQ were a place of apolitical tranquility? Alas. GQ has always taken men seriously as full human beings, and full human beings can handle tough conversations, opposing viewpoints, and a little gray area. There’s nothing noble about steering clear of the uncomfortable.

Which brings me to our special package on Cornell, anchored by a dispatch from campus by Savannah Sobrevilla. It is about an alleged sexual assault. It is also about the men on the sidelines of sexual assault. Some of the young men we meet through the story are students who are earnestly and inelegantly trying to grapple with how serious the alleged crimes are, with the crude and cruel culture that surrounds them, and with what role, exactly, they play in exacerbating that culture—or could play in diminishing it. It’s not easy reading, but not everything should be. ✦

#6776. All of Princess Kate’s Remembrance Sunday Looks Over the Years

The Princess of Wales first attended the event in 2011

Princess Catherine has joined the royal family for the National Service of Remembrance every year since 2011, the same year that she got married to Prince William. The somber annual ceremony, held on Remembrance Sunday at the Cenotaph, a war memorial in London, honors service members who have lost their lives at war. It takes place on the second Sunday of November, and it involves a church service, a wreath-laying ceremony, and a formal procession, all of which are attended by various members of the British royal family.

When it comes to her royal style, the Princess of Wales has long embraced a more sustainable approach, frequently rewearing dresses, jewelry, and accessories. Over the years, that style philosophy has been on full display at Remembrance Sunday, where Kate has made a tradition of recycling looks and adding sentimental touches through meaningful brooches and heirloom jewelry.

One of her signature accessories is the red poppy pin, a symbol that has come to be widely associated with the holiday. Kate is often seen wearing three poppies at once, and while the royal family has never confirmed the significance behind the number, one theory suggests it may be a personal nod to her great-grandmother’s three brothers, all of whom lost their lives during World War I.

Throughout the years, Kate has also worn pieces that once belonged to late royal family members like Queen Elizabeth II and Princess Diana, which she has styled with outfits from Catherine Walker, Alexander McQueen, Temperley London, Diane von Furstenberg, and more.

Ahead, take a look back at Princess Kate’s Remembrance Sunday style through the years, from her first appearance in 2011 to now. And be sure to check back for more updates once the Princess of Wales steps out for the 2026 ceremony, too.

For her first appearance with the royal family at the Cenotaph memorial, Kate wore a black Diane von Furstenberg coat adorned with a double poppy pin along with a Jane Corbett hat and Kiki McDonough earrings.

At the 2012 Remembrance Sunday ceremony, the then-Duchess of Cambridge rewore her Diane von Furstenberg coat from the previous year, this time paired with a Philip Treacy hat, Annoushka earrings, and a single sparkling poppy brooch.

Opting for a different outerwear look for her third appearance, Kate wore a single-breasted Temperley London coat, which she accessorized with a Sylvia Fletcher for Lock & Co. hat and Kiki McDonough hoop earrings with Annoushka pearl drops.

In 2014, Kate wore a black Alexander McQueen jacket over a black ruffled BCBG dress. She rewore the Jane Corbett hat she donned in 2011, and she added a floral brooch, a Mappin & Webb diamond necklace, and Princess Diana’s sapphire earrings.

Kate stepped out in another Alexander McQueen coat for the 2015 Remembrance Day service, which featured velvet trim along the collar, sleeves, and waist. The then-duchess topped it off with a Lock & Co. hat adorned with a sculptural butterfly and black veil, the Kiki McDonough hoop earrings and Annoushka pearl drops she’d worn in 2013, and a triple-poppy pin on her lapel.

In another rewear, Kate donned the same Diane von Furstenberg coat she wore to her first and second Remembrance Sunday services in 2011 and 2012. This time, she styled it with a John Boyd hat, diamond-and-pearl earrings, and three poppies on her lapel.

Kate switched up her look for the 2017 National Service of Remembrance, opting for a double-breasted, collarless Dolce & Gabbana coat with oversized gold buttons, statement Oscar de la Renta earrings in gold, and a Philip Treacy hat she wore in 2006, during her early days of dating Prince William. She also debuted a new hair look, pinning it up to create the illusion of a short bob.

The 2018 Remembrance Day Service marked a significant milestone: the 100th anniversary of the end of World War I. For the occasion, Kate wore a custom Alexander McQueen jacket with military-inspired details, including a high white collar, red shoulder embellishments, and crest-style buttons. She completed the look with a Lock & Co. hat, a triple red poppy pin, and a brooch honoring the women who served during World War I.

Opting for another military-inspired jacket, Kate wore a Catherine Walker coat and a Philip Treacy hat featuring a black veil. Forgoing her usual poppy pin, she instead wore a special pin known as the “Codebreaker’s Brooch” in honor of her grandmother who worked at Bletchley Park during World War II.

At the socially distanced 2020 Remembrance Day ceremony, held amid the COVID-19 pandemic, Kate once again opted for a military-inspired look. She wore a Catherine Walker jacket with fringe detailing at the shoulders, complete with a Philip Treacy fascinator, pearl drop earrings, and her signature triple-poppy pin.

Kate brought back her Alexander McQueen coat from the 2018 service for the 2021 Remembrance Day ceremony. She paired the repeat look with Princess Diana’s pearl earrings, a wide-brimmed Lock & Co. hat, and her signature triple-poppy pin, along with a crystal flower brooch from the Royal British Legion.

For her first Remembrance Day Service following Queen Elizabeth II’s passing, the Princess of Wales wore a black Catherine Walker coat, Princess Diana’s pearl-and-diamond earrings, and a wide-brimmed Philip Treacy hat. She completed the look with her signature trio of poppies, paired with an Art Deco-inspired diamond brooch.

The Princess of Wales opted for multiple rewears at the 2023 Remembrance Sunday service. She repeated the custom Catherine Walker coat she first wore in 2019 and the Philip Treacy hat she previously wore at the 2012 and 2017 services. Then, for some added sparkle, she wore Queen Elizabeth’s diamond-and-pearl earrings, and her signature three-poppy pin.

Kate didn’t make many public appearances in 2024 due to her cancer diagnosis, but she still attended the Remembrance Sunday service that year. She wore a Catherine Walker coat dress, featuring a velvet bow collar, and she accessorized with a Juliette Botterill hat, Queen Elizabeth’s earrings, and her triple-poppy pin.

Kate’s look at the 2025 Remembrance Sunday service featured a rewear with a new twist. She opted for the same Catherine Walker coat from 2022, but this time, she styled it with a lace navy-blue pie-crust collar blouse draping out of the top. She accessorized with a sculptural Lock & Co. fascinator, Princess Diana’s pearl-drop earrings, a single poppy brooch, and a Royal Air Force brooch.
