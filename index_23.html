#6886. Redesigned Microsoft Copilot Will Build Apps and Work on Its Own

Micorosft has announced that it's redesigning Copilot, the company's all-encompassing AI assistant, so users can hand off complex tasks, build tools by describing what they need, and assign work to an agent that keeps going after they log off.

The new Copilot experience has three main parts: Home, Code, and Autopilot. It also brings Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into the Copilot app. For users, the pitch is simple: Start a task in one place and stay there as it grows from a quick question into a document, an app, or a longer-running project. Most of the new capabilities are still rolling out or in preview.

Home puts Chat and Copilot Cowork together. Chat remains the place for quick answers, research, and drafts. Cowork handles work a user wants to delegate, such as preparing a briefing or pulling together a response to a request for proposals. Microsoft said Copilot eventually will route requests to Chat, Cowork, or Code on its own.

Office in Copilot will allow users to create a Word document, Excel workbook, or PowerPoint deck without leaving that experience. The files remain editable in the regular Office apps, and colleagues can work on them at the same time. Someone building a presentation, for example, could draw on a workbook, draft the slides, and keep refining both files from within Copilot.

Code is aimed at people who need a tool but might never open a development environment. A user can describe a tracker, dashboard, app, or workflow in plain language and have Copilot build it. Microsoft calls these "small, purpose-built solutions." The feature uses the same underlying technology as GitHub Copilot and runs in a sandboxed environment. A separate Copilot Managed Runtime, now in preview, lets organizations host and share the resulting apps within Microsoft 365.

Then there is Autopilot, previously known as Scout. It is designed for work that does not end with a single response: following a discussion, checking in with stakeholders, or keeping a project moving over several days. "Give it a name, a role, and a goal," Microsoft said. Autopilot runs in the cloud and has its own identity, memory, and workspace in the customer's tenant. Microsoft said its actions are subject to permissions, auditing, and organizational controls.

Those longer-running tasks also change how customers pay for Copilot. In a separate pricing post, Microsoft said everyday work in Chat and Microsoft 365 apps will remain covered by a per-user subscription. Cowork, Code, and Autopilot will be billed separately through usage-based Copilot Credits. A Copilot user subscription will still be needed to access those features.

For enterprise customers, the features that charge by usage will stay off until an admin sets up a spending policy in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Microsoft said there will be no charges before then. Admins can set budgets for the whole organization or for specific groups, limit how much individual users can spend, and get alerts when costs approach a set amount. Reports will show who is using the features and which tasks are driving costs.

Microsoft is bringing Code and Copilot Managed Runtime under those cost controls alongside Cowork. Copilot Studio agents are expected to follow in October. Before turning the features on for more employees, IT departments will need to decide who can use them, how much they can spend and which tasks are worth the extra cost.

Home and Code will begin rolling out through Microsoft's Frontier early access program in the coming weeks. Code's Frontier rollout starts at the end of September, when Autopilot also expands to private preview.

For more information, visit the Microsoft site.

#6882. AI Leaders Warn of Risks at United Nations Security Council Briefing

The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

#6874. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

#6870. AI Ambitions Are Running Into a Network Infrastructure Trust Problem

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

The research found that concerns about network providers have caused organizations to rethink strategic initiatives over the past two years. Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#6862. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6846. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

It nods to the Victorian parlor, but stops short of period-drama excess; against a cleaner silhouette or in a spare room, the old-world trim feels playful and unexpectedly fresh. Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? In many ways, a great antique shop feels like the antidote to trend fatigue: a space where the true must-haves are hidden in dusty corners, with part of the fun in the thrill of the hunt. Other experts second the motion for more character. “I’m seeing clients really drawn to antiques with more to them than meets the eye: pieces that have a bit of humor or whimsy, or pieces with great provenance,” says ELLE Decor Rising Star William Cullum. Ahead, we asked top designers and experts to share the most inspiring antique trends of the moment. From neoclassic mirrors to hand-painted chests paired with modern art, their takes are sure to inform your next estate sale rummage, or inspire you to restyle an old heirloom.

We called mirrored surfaces a major trend to watch for fall 2026, and antique trends have followed suit. Unexpected (or better yet, artfully manipulated) reflections can add rich dimension and movement to any space, but in this category, antiques create a window to the past that’s tough to beat.

“One of the easiest ways to add a sense of history to a room is to include an antique mirror,” says Drake. “Whether you choose a sober and strict neo-classic piece or a more flamboyant baroque one, reflections of the past add depth and sparkle.”

“One trend I’m observing is an uptick in antique dowry chests,” antiques dealer Ali Mahon says. From Pennsylvania Dutch to Swedish Folk and Spanish carved, she’s noticed clients gravitating toward more delicately adorned storage over spare. With thoughtfully mixed furniture (from style to period) becoming more desirable than matching sets, Mahon sees more hand-painted antique surfaces in our future: “With their decorative tulips, leaves, birds, and hearts, dowry chests create a colorful splash in any room.”

ELLE Decor Rising Star Reagan Gail Struble agrees—although delicate designs are not a requirement of the trend, in her book: “Something about a playful wash of color on an awesome old piece of furniture is very alluring, especially when you can see the age and texture of the wood peeking through,” she says. “Hand-painted case pieces add a little punch of charm to any room.”

Luckily, these antiques can be just as functional as they are fashionable. Mahon recommends using a painted chest as a coffee table, at the foot of the bed, or in a space that could benefit from a bit more romance (and a few extra drawers). Adding to an existing space? Jenkins advises starting small: “Swap one overhead fixture or put a pair of vintage lamps on a console, and you’ll be surprised how much the whole room shifts,” he says. “It’s an easy way to add history without starting over.”

Those are the pairings that make people stop and look twice.” Drake adds that seemingly out-of-place seating is an easy way to add intrigue. “I love including antique chairs as sculptural notes, contrasted against modern upholstery and tables,” he says.

But when it comes to antiquing, there’s really only so much one can plan for. Letting a great find lead the way often turns out to be a better strategy than arriving to an estate sale with a rigid checklist. The best way to keep up with top designers when it comes to antiquing this year? Find a piece that has something to say, place it where it seems to belong, and then try it where it doesn’t.

#6788. 4 Weekend Decluttering Habits That Make Small Spaces Feel Bigger

A few smart systems can keep your counters, closets, and junk drawers in check.

The easiest clutter to manage is clutter that never has a chance to form. And in small spaces like galley kitchens and studio apartments, mess piles up naturally on any flat surface. Below, find the pro-approved habits that make a difference when you have more stuff than your space can hold. You can start these routines in just one weekend, and it’s far more attainable than tackling the massive pile in the garage.

Everything needs a home in your house. That’s the power of a well-placed “drop zone.” Even in small spaces, a catchall tray, bowl, or mini basket belongs at entryways or places of transition. A hook for your keys and bags when you come through the door, a bin for loose throw blankets, or a basket for remote controls on the mantel.

Throughout the day, it can take quite a bit of mental load to always put everything back when you use it. It’s just one small habit, rather than something to keep up with all day.

Impulse shopping lends itself to clutter very easily. The “One In, One Out” rule can curb this. It works like this: for every new thing you want to bring into your home, one thing has to go. Greenhalgh suggests checking your closet, fridge, and pantry before you shop—going through your back stock will save you both space and money.

“In small spaces, items accumulate very quickly,” Macie Kreutzer, founder of For the Love of Simple, says. “Before shopping for new items (home goods, clothing) clear out at least one item from this category. Don't bring in more than your space can physically contain.”

Once you have everything you’re ready to get rid of, make the donation process as seamless as possible. Fill your donation bags and boxes in the hallway or in the car. This encourages you to actually finish getting rid of the clutter, rather than letting them collect dust in the garage.

“Nothing gets to wander back inside just because it once lived there,” Carroll Cartwright, professional organizer and owner of Neatly & Co., says. “And this is why I love moving everything out of the space and into the hall or a separate area first. The things you decide not to keep are already halfway out the door. Keep them moving.”

It is so tempting to take advantage of the freebies in life. But this is how linen closets and kitchen drawers get quickly overrun. Unless there’s a specific and immediate need for them, you’re better off getting rid of them.

“They are usually cheap and break easily anyway,” Diane Greenhalgh, owner of Tiny to the Max, says. “That includes airplane and hotel freebies, special event or conference swag, and extra takeout plastic utensils and condiments. Your junk drawer will thank you.”

If you already have a drawer overflowing with freebies, it’s time to start culling and evaluating. You might part with two or 10 things, or you might choose to keep it all. “The point is that you’ve looked at every single item and made a decision about it, instead of continuing to store things simply because they were already there,” Cartwright says. “More likely than not, you’ll find something that makes you say, ‘Why on earth do I still have this?’”

The advice your grandma gave you was spot-on: It’s easier if you wash the dishes as you cook. That same principle applies to keeping your small space tidy. Mess feels larger with less square footage to work with, so once you’re finished with something, put it away.

“Put away your makeup after you've applied it in the morning, stack dishes in the dishwasher and wipe down surfaces after each meal, and tuck away work or homework into a drawer once you're done for the evening,” Greenhalgh says.

For the days you can’t clean as you go, Cartwright suggests spending 10 to 15 minutes a day “closing your circles.” It’s what helps your home stay clean over time, instead of just for a few weeks. This period is when you put the scissors back in the drawer or hang up your jacket.

“Most clutter is just a collection of unfinished tasks,” Cartwright says. “This small commitment allows you to wake up to order instead of yesterday’s chaos, and that is a much better way to start the day!”

#6783. 5 Common Mistakes That Are Making Your Vintage Decor Look All Wrong—and How Designers Get It Right

Experts say the best rooms mix eras, styles, and stories.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pictured above: This space, designed by Sarah West, strikes the right balance of vintage and contemporary decor, proving that a mix is always better than sticking to one design era.

It’s not just the Instagram algorithm playing tricks on you: Everyone really is looking to antique and vintage decor. Homeowners are growing tired of rooms that look as though everything was bought from a single brand in one fell swoop. We’re now at a point where a collected, curated, personalized feel has replaced quiet luxury.

“I think it’s important to add at least some kind of antique or vintage piece in every room,” says Sarah West, founder of Sarah West & Associates and a designer for over 20 years. “Even if you’re doing a more modern or transitional room, a sense of history adds weight to a space.”

It’s not necessarily as easy as plopping one antique into a room and calling it a day, however, and that can feel intimidating. Even once you learn to shop for antiques and vintage like a pro, what do you actually do with those finds when you’re home?

While the designers we spoke with are quick to point out there really are no strict rules here—it’s about your style and what you love—there are a few key mistakes that pop up most often when people start styling with antique and vintage decor. These designers have the scoop on how to avoid those missteps and make your treasures, and your entire room, shine.

Committing to a massive piece of furniture or tasking yourself with overhauling an entire room with antique or vintage pieces can get overwhelming fast. You may make decisions you don’t love down the line.

If you decide you’re going to decorate a whole room in a limited amount of time, and you’re set on sourcing antique or vintage items, you’ll likely end up settling for things just because you need them, says Nancy Cavaliere, a New York–based interior decorator, stylist, and vintage collector. Go slow and start small.

“I’d rather live with an empty corner while I wait for the perfect piece than fill it with something I don’t love,” she notes. “The most interesting homes evolve over time. I think a room should tell the story of the person living in it, not the story of one shopping trip.”

Ease your way into decorating with vintage and antiques by starting small. Think one piece of art, one mirror, one vase.

“Don’t overthink it,” West advises. “Take a small piece, try it out in the room for a couple of days, see how it feels.” Do you like the piece in that spot? Does it work with other elements of the room? Does it inspire other styling choices for the space? Try moving it around and interacting with it to find its perfect positioning and see where that takes you.

As you start out getting a feel for that smaller piece, remember that just because it’s unique doesn’t mean it has to be the focal point of the whole room.

“Don’t treat vintage pieces like they have to be the star of the room simply because they’re old,” says Cavaliere. “Not every antique needs a pedestal. Sometimes the most beautiful thing you can do is let an extraordinary piece sit comfortably alongside something completely ordinary.”

Fully incorporating vintage or antique pieces into a space imbues the room with that richness and intrigue.

“These pieces give feelings to a room,” West says. “If you put something in a corner, as you transition throughout the room, it gives this element of surprise.”

This includes creating high-low mixes, says Cavaliere, who points out, “a $20 flea market find can look incredible next to an expensive designer piece. It’s all about how you put things together.”

In fact, that mix is something to strive for, says Alex Bass, founder of Salon 21, Art Advisory & Interior Design Firm.

“I love a contrast, like a room with modern architecture that leans heavily into antiques or vice versa. If you want to incorporate pieces from Pottery Barn, CB2, that’s fine, but there are also so many affordable vintage finds. And something like an antique light fixture can make all of these pieces work.”

Speaking of contrasts, all of our designer sources say a very common misconception they encounter is that you can only decorate a room with pieces from the same era.

“Just because you have a Victorian chair doesn’t mean you need a Victorian living room,” Cavaliere says. “My own home is a mix of different periods, styles, colors, and patterns, and that's what gives it personality. I love the tension between pieces that seemingly have nothing in common. And it's fun!”

Bass cites the example of Art Deco being popular right now, while articles are claiming midcentury modern is “out.” She encourages people to think of pieces less rigidly, assuming they have to commit fully to one style, or toss another style because someone declared a new one the next big thing.

“These are not ‘trends,’ these are design periods that already existed,” she explains. “It’s about having the eye to pull pieces from different periods that speak to you and make sense in your room.” You can mix Art Deco and midcentury modern, for instance, by using elements characteristic of them both, like marble, warm woods, mixed metals, and bold color pops.

There can be a fear with vintage and antique pieces that changing anything “ruins” them. But unless it’s a significantly valuable piece you’re investing in or something you plan on having appraised, Bass says, embrace the ability to make special pieces work for your space.

“Don’t be afraid to reupholster,” she says—and not just if the fabric is in poor shape. “If I buy a piece and the structure is beautiful but I want to add a contemporary fabric and print, that can make for a fun contrast with the frame.”

For example, Bass fell in love with a modular midcentury sectional, but its fabric didn’t quite complement the space she was designing, so she chose a contemporary fabric that worked with its aesthetic.

This, Cavaliere points out, frees you up from obsessing over condition when shopping for pieces. If something has great bones but its fabric or finish has seen better days—and if you love it—don’t pass it up. Remember you can reupholster, strip and stain or paint, and repair.

Just factor the cost of those fixes into the price of the item to help make your decision. Especially because, as Bass advises, it’s better to consult a professional unless you’re well-versed in certain projects. Stripping and staining a wood piece is fairly easy, but when it comes to reupholstering and other repairs, you’ll likely actually save money by outsourcing to a pro rather than potentially damaging the piece yourself.

Similarly, it’s typical for people to feel they can’t actually make use of vintage and antique pieces, because they think they’re too precious. But especially considering how well-made many older pieces are, they’re begging to be put to work. Again, it’s actually incorporating them into your everyday life that gives your room and entire home that sense of personalization, curation, and storytelling.

“One hundred years ago, people were using their silver,” West says. “In my kitchen, you’re eating with hotel silver.”

This includes getting creative with how you use antique and vintage pieces—don’t feel bound by what something is “supposed to” be for.

“One of my favorite tricks is turning vintage drapes and valances into bed canopies,” Cavaliere says. “I did this in my daughter’s room and I absolutely love it.” Cavaliere also finds unexpected uses for small vessels and decorative objects: a creamer and sugar set in the bathroom to hold Q-tips and cotton balls; dishes, trays, and crystal bowls to sort accessories in her closet.

“A vintage china cabinet doesn't have to live in a dining room just because it was designed to hold dishes,” she says. “One of my favorite tricks is using china cabinets to display and store shoes and handbags in a dressing room or closet. Why spend a ton of money on custom cabinetry when you can repurpose a beautiful vintage piece that has so much more character?”

West agrees. “Purchase an armoire, flip the doors, add mirrors, and make it a bar,” West suggests. “Find an old door knocker you love? Hang a towel off of it. Use a tablecloth to make a pillow. Tell a story.”

#6708. How Her Love of Vintage Changed Writer Keli Goff’s Life

Her collection, which has given new life to some of the great Black designers, is now a museum show.

When Keli Goff began purchasing vintage, it was a way to put together stylish looks for her new job within her budget. But over the years, as her interest grew, collecting became a way of connecting to the stories of Black designers unrecognized during their lifetimes. Goff, a writer who has been on the staff of shows including Being Mary Jane and And Just Like That, now owns work from major fashion figures like Patrick Kelly, Isaia Rankin, Stephen Burrows, and Ann Lowe, together making up one of the biggest private collections of work by Black designers. This month, the Houston Museum of African American Culture presents “Collecting in Color: Highlights from the Keli Goff Collection,” which was curated by John Guess and Danielle Finnerman. It’s the first major exhibition focused on her collection, and some of the pieces will be donated to the Bard Graduate Center’s Study Collection.

Goff’s interest in fashion design has intersected with her writing career in interesting ways. The estate of legendary costume designer Edith Head chose Goff to adapt her story for a biopic. “It’s all so surreal. I obviously have admired Edith’s work as long as I can remember, so my collection bringing this project into my life is a dream come true,” she says. Last year, Goff completed studying to become an art appraiser. She now consults in the art and auction sphere in addition to writing.

“People have been fascinated by my collection,” Goff says. “It’s a bit rare to see, frankly, collectors in this space like me. I mean like me in all sorts of ways, not just as an African American woman, but an African American woman who’s not from a privileged background. It’s important to me that I’m not the last one like me in these spaces.”

Goff spoke to ELLE about the joy of working with a museum in her Texas hometown, the old movies that inspired her love of vintage, and the designers she wishes everyone knew.

It really started by watching old movies with my mom and watching her dress up [when I was] a little girl. When I show friends old photos of her, people are always thinking, “Oh, she looks like a movie star or very Diana Ross-esque.” We would watch old movies, especially Imitation of Life and things from that era, the real glamour, the golden age of Hollywood. She would always say, “Look at the dresses, look at the dresses,” and I would. I really fell in love with old movies, which I do believe is part of how I ended up becoming a writer.

But also, I really fell in love with vintage clothes. I moved to New York and didn’t have a lot of money, and vintage was not trendy and didn’t cost thousands of dollars like it can be today. For one of my earliest jobs, I had to go to fancy events, but I had no money. I bought a couple of pieces from the ’60s at a flea market. I remember being at this event, and all these women who obviously had a lot more money to spend on clothes, asking, “Who’s this by? What boutique did you buy this at?” And I was like, “Oh, I got it at a flea market on Avenue B.” I think they thought I was joking.

I wrote a piece for The Daily Beast several years ago about the most influential Black designers in history. It was one of the most memorable pieces I’ve written. I don’t think at the time I was aware of how profoundly it impacted me, but I learned how many seminal Black American designers died of AIDS in a really short window of time. Willi Smith, one of the first Black designers to achieve major crossover commercial success in department stores, died. And so did Isaia Rankin and then Patrick Kelly. What I took away is that so much history was lost, but I also think about how different the future of American fashion might be today because these were really trailblazing and hugely influential.

They weren’t just influential Black American designers, but hugely influential American designers. Isaia Rankin basically had invented athleisure wear. It was his idea that you should be able to go from a coffee shop to a gym to the club. [He made] one of the first pieces by a Black designer I acquired online. I’ll never forget that the person selling it said, “This belonged to my aunt. Apparently, this guy used to be somebody,” which I just think is the saddest thing. He had this profound impact and then had been largely forgotten.

During the pandemic, when everyone worked from home with too much time on their hands and easy access to shop online, I ended up acquiring quite a bit, in part because a lot of the brick-and-mortar stores, particularly in Europe, were really struggling. They were so reliant on tourist foot traffic, and so a lot of them started selling online [for the first time]. And so there were a number of French boutiques I started acquiring pieces from, including some Patrick Kelly pieces that still have the original store tags from the 1980s, which I won’t remove. Those I won’t wear. The rest of them I wear when they fit. As life goes, they don’t always fit forever.

I’ve become friendly with Patrick Kelly’s widower, Bjorn Amelan, who’s a lovely, wonderful man and the keeper of Patrick’s legacy. It is important to me, when possible, to try to maintain relationships with the people I view as keepers of the legacies because I see my collection as serving the legacy of the designers. The pieces don’t belong to me. They belong to our wider community because they’re a part of our lived history.

The really cool thing is how many people I’ve met along this journey who have not only become cheerleaders but actually reach out to me about pieces. Writer Jill Kargman has become a friend and she gifted me a vintage Stephen Burrows dress. She said, “It belongs in your collection,” which is just the coolest thing ever.

I’m from Houston, and we have the Houston Museum of African American Culture here. We’re living in a really interesting time where people are having conversations about the politics of diversity, but one of the things that’s cool about fashion is that people of different political persuasions were like, “I think this collection’s really cool. I read an article about your collection, I ended up ordering a book about Elizabeth Keckley, or I ended up going down a rabbit hole about Ann Lowe.” At this moment, to be able to do an exhibition like this in my hometown felt particularly poignant.

Melanie Lawson has been a legendary anchor in Houston for decades. She was asked what she’s going to do in retirement, and she said that her husband, John Guess Jr., was the CEO of the Houston Museum of African American Culture. I sent him a note about how much Melanie inspired me and then mentioned my collection.

It was just kismet. They’ve actually never done a fashion exhibition. It was a real gamble. But what I so appreciated is that they had the same vision that, in a time in which our world is so divided, it’s really beautiful when you can find pieces of art that people can come together around.

#6707. Vince Staples Is Our November 2026 Music Director

The rapper crafts an exclusive playlist for Bazaar that ranges from songs from his childhood to songs that show the vastness of what hip-hop can be

Vince Staples is known for his matter-of-fact, bluntly honest lyrics. The California-born rapper often taps into the realities of Long Beach, his hometown, meditating on urban poverty, systemic racism, and even skepticism of the music industry. This lyricism emerges from the rapper’s desire to remain authentic and has been evident throughout his career from his 2015 debut, Summertime ’06.

“Whatever your viewpoint on life is or whatever you’re experiencing at this current moment is going to translate into the music that you create, no matter what,” Staples told Bazaar in an interview this summer. “As you get older, you learn to embrace that.”

The musician, 33, did exactly that on his seventh studio album, Crybaby, an urgent, politically charged record that trades traditional beats for post-punk melodies. He confronts historical racism on songs like “Tulsa, OK” and “Cotton” while indicting the entertainment industry for continuing to incite racism against Black people in songs like “TV Guide.” Other songs tap directly into his experience, like “White Flag,” which reflects on his personal journey as an artist and entertainer.

“I want to come up with the idea that feels most organic,” he says, adding that he never listens to music when he’s in the writing process. “I don’t like to be inspired by other music,” he says. “I feel like it’s a disservice to the lives that we live every day…. Music reminds you of what you experience in your life, and it forces you to look inward.” This same instinct guided Staples as he assembled the playlist for Bazaar’s Performance issue, compiling songs that have been foundational to him as an artist and performer.

He often admires introspective songwriting. Blu’s track “Vanity,” featured on the California rapper’s album Her Favorite Colo(u)r (2011), reminds him of the personal, diaristic storytelling style that influenced his early works. “The way he tells stories, it’s the way that I really got into hip-hop music for myself,” he recalls. Today, he cites his friend Steve Lacy’s ability to capture a “good adaptation of his thoughts at the current moment” with “Nice Shoes,” the lead single from the album Oh Yeah? Staples, reflecting on how personal music is, suggests that songs “all have to mean something to us for them to even come to mind” in the first place.

Given the artist’s relationship to music, Staples chose songs that reminded him of moments in his life. One of the album’s oldest tracks is Ray Charles’s soulful “Drown in My Own Tears,” which hearkens back to his childhood. “[Ray Charles] has been ever-present in my life because growing up as a Black person in America and Southern California with older parents or older grandparents, he would be a fixture in music, whether it’s country music, soul music, R&B, music—you can put him in a lot of those different subgenres, but just as far as black music in general,” he says.

Another staple of American Black music is James Brown, but Staples decided to include the Who’s muscular 1965 cover of Brown’s “I Don’t Mind.” For him, the song offers a lesson in transformation and reinterpretation, telling Bazaar it “unearthed the idea of coverage and the ecosystem of sharing music or repurposing music.” On his new album, for instance, he samples Slick Rick’s “Children’s Story” on his song “The Big Bad Wolf.”

In this way, Staples inherently views music as an expansive medium. He recalls how recently the Beastie Boys song “Sabotage,” a buoyant mixture of rap and hardcore punk, helped him explain to a friend “the vastness of what we consider to be hip-hop music.” Ultimately, for Staples, a song’s ability to change you makes it indelible. “It’s a testament to what it meant to you in the moment you first heard it,” he says. “Having that brought back to the forefront is a cool part of the process.”

#6699. 17 Candidates Who Made History in the 2026 Midterms

From Gina Hinojosa to Peggy Flanagan, these soon-to-be senators, representatives, and governors broke barriers in races across the country.

Democrats took control over the House of Representatives and the Senate in a sweeping midterm election that saw voters reject President Donald Trump’s endorsed candidates and policies.

Amid the “blue wave,” numerous candidates made history as they won—becoming the first of their background, or their gender, to hold the office. While many of them “avoided focusing on the inherent historic value of their race, gender, religion or sexual orientation, some voters’ rights advocates are celebrating how the electorate has expanded,” notes the Associated Press. With the Democrats’ victory, too, Rep. Hakeem Jeffries will likely become the first Black Speaker of the House.

“These firsts show how our country is changing,” Alexis Anderson-Reed, president and CEO of State Voices, told the AP. “For years, people of color and LGBTQ+ communities have organized, knocked on doors, talked to voters, held conversations, and done the hard work of saving our democracy. These wins are because of them.”

While the majority of these politicians are Democrats, two Republican governors also made history last night—Marsha Blackburn, who became the first woman elected to serve as governor of Tennessee, and Bryon Donalds, who is set to become Florida’s first Black governor. Ahead, 17 soon-to-be senators, governors, and members of the House of Representatives who also claimed a historic “first” in the midterms.

Dr. El-Sayed became the first Muslim American elected to the Senate. “I’m not running to be the first Muslim anything,” El-Sayed told CNN. “I just want to be a really, really great senator for my state.”

Pappas became the first openly gay male Senator upon winning his election in New Hampshire.

Flanagan, a member of the White Earth Band of Ojibwe, is set to become the first Native American woman to serve in the Senate. (She could be joined by Alaska’s Mary Peltola, of the Yup’ik tribe, but that race has not been called yet.)

Turek, two-time Paralympic gold medalist for Team USA (with the wheelchair basketball team), was elected in Iowa. He will become the first Senator to have been a Paralympian, and the first Senator with spina bifida.

Not only did Bottoms become Georgia’s first Black and first female governor, she is the first Black woman to be elected governor of any U.S. state.

“Tonight should remind us all that no matter what community we come from, Georgia is not defined by one moment in time or by one person,” Bottoms said in her victory speech. “It’s been defined by all of us, and I will be a governor for everyone in this state.”

Becerra, the former United States Secretary of Health and Human Services, was elected the first Latino governor of California.

Hinojosa was elected the first Latina governor of Texas. She will also be the first Democratic governor of the Lone Star State in more than three decades.

Haaland, former United States Secretary of the Interior, will be the first Native American woman governor. “Tonight, in our nation’s 250th year, I stand before you as the first Native American woman elected governor in U.S. history,” she said last night.

Haaland, of Pueblo Indian descent, is no stranger to making history: She was the the first Native American cabinet secretary.

Acton defeated Vivek Ramaswamy to become the first woman elected governor of Ohio and the state’s first Jewish governor.

Crowley will become the first Black governor of Wisconsin. He actually dropped out of the race over the summer, but rejoined at the urging of the outgoing Democratic governor.

Former Senator Klobuchar will become the first woman to be governor of Minnesota.

With Stratton’s victory in the senate race in Illinois, the state will now be represented by two women of color—the first time in U.S. history both senators from a state are women of color.

Kiros’s victory in Colorado’s first district was history making in numerous ways: At 29, she is the first Gen Z woman elected to the House, and Colorado’s first Black woman representative.

Dr. Andrews, a pediatrician, became the first woman ever elected to the U.S. Senate from South Carolina.

Nevada’s Attorney General is poised to become the first Black governor of the state.

Dr. Hamawy, a retired U.S. Army combat surgeon, will be the first Muslim representative from New Jersey.

Valdez, citizen of Ysleta Del Sur Pueblo, is set to become the first female Native American member of Congress from New York.
