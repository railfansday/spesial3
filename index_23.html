#7018. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

Cybersecurity concerns were more common among organizations with mature AI programs.

Half of those reporting established AI returns identified cybersecurity as a barrier, compared with roughly one-third of those still experimenting.

Overall, 86% of respondents said their organizations are adapting their cybersecurity operating models to address AI-accelerated threats.

The report identifies accountability, operational resilience, and cost management as priorities as AI becomes more embedded in business operations.

"Many organizations have made AI models widely available and track their use," said Swaminathan Chandrasekaran, head of the US AI Center of Excellence at KPMG. "But consumption alone is not a strategy."

#7014. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

You might assume that moving your houseplant closer to a bright window is an excellent idea in winter to allow it to drink in as much sunlight as possible. And while this isn't necessarily a bad move, it just requires a little more thought.

“It’s always a good idea to move them closer to a bright window as darker days hit, but make sure to avoid cold drafts and radiators,” Mark Sage, B&Q's horticulture expert, tells us. “Cold drafts and dry air from radiators can all take their toll on indoor plants, leading to yellowing leaves, rotten roots or even plant fatality.”

Similarly, Sage suggests thinking about where the sun actually shines in your home throughout autumn and winter.

“Where natural light may be limited, consider investing in a plant light to give an extra boost, while rotating larger plants daily can help avoid leggy, awkward growth as they stretch out looking for the sunshine,” he suggests.

However, Sage warns that this is often the wrong approach. In fact, during autumn and winter, “sometimes the best thing you can do for your plants is leave them alone!”

He explains: “In a slower growth period, plants generally need less water and feeding, and winter certainly isn’t the time for regular repotting.”

Instead, Sage recommends letting the soil dry out between waterings to help prevent root rot and giving plants time to rest. “It's worth giving them a boost of plant feed ahead of the winter months, but a little goes a long way,” he says. “Give plants their final dose at the end of autumn.”

Instead, he recommends bringing your plants together during autumn and winter to help recreate the conditions many tropical houseplants naturally prefer.

“Give your houseplants a helping hand through winter by clustering them together to create a more humid microclimate, as many of these plants originally come from much hotter parts of the world,” he recommends. “Grouping several varieties close together can help maintain humidity around them, which can be particularly useful when central heating makes indoor air drier.”

While watering may have been your main plant-care task during summer, it's a mistake to neglect cleaning your houseplants over autumn and winter. Just like the rest of your home, plants can gather dust, and that build-up can make it harder for them to make the most of limited daylight.

“During winter, leaves can gather a layer of dust,” says Sage. “Give them a wipe down every one to two weeks with a microfibre cloth to support photosynthesis, and to help them benefit from even the smallest burst of sunlight.”

In fact, Sage suggests combining cleaning and watering occasionally by giving your plants a gentle shower.

Caring for your home means looking beyond the interiors. Giving neglected areas the attention they need can keep germs and grime at bay, and replacing certain items can also protect your health and safety. Consider your carbon monoxide alarm: If an aging unit fails during an emergency, the consequences could be deadly.

To help you keep everything in good shape, we asked experts which 14 household items you should replace as soon as possible. Take a look—you can thank us later!

Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors lose their sensitivity over time, which could delay detection during an emergency. The timeframe for when you should replace these will vary by manufacturer. Look up the model you have online to be sure—even a fairly new alarm should be tested once a month. The batteries should be replaced once a year.

Mold and mildew can build up on shower liners, especially in damp environments. A 2024 study by SafeHome.org showed that shower curtains had the most germs of any other surfaces in the bathroom, including the toilet seat. While Hayslett recommends washing them every three months, the material can eventually become brittle over time.

Dingy towels are the antithesis of luxe. Towels lose their absorbency and softness after years of washing.

“Avoid using too much detergent, which can build up on fibers,” Hayslett says. “Use a little vinegar during the rinse cycle to keep towels soft.” If your bath towels feel scratchy or start to smell musty despite washing, it’s time for a refresh.

To prolong the life of your towels, make sure you let them dry properly between uses to prevent mildew growth, noted Vyanca Soto of Market Studio Interiors. And you should definitely swap them out if you notice discoloration from your skincare products.

You likely spend plenty of time in your kitchen and bathroom each day. Make sure you take a few minutes to examine the grout lines around your bathtub and kitchen countertops.

“Grout breaks down over time, and you can end up with water damage to the drywall,” says Rebekah Zaveloff, cofounder and creative director at Imparfait Design Studio. “If you look and there are spots that have pulled away or deteriorated, it’s time to regrout.”

A sagging mattress is a clear sign it’s time for a new one. Mattresses lose support and can accumulate dust mites, allergens, and even mold, which is certainly not a dreamy way to slumber.

“Flip your mattress every six months [unless it's a one-sided design] to help maintain its structure and extend its lifespan,” Hayslette advised.

A mattress protector can extend its lifespan as does regular vacuuming to remove dust and debris. Soto recently bought a bed vacuum cleaner and says, “It works like a charm.” She also uses it on her dog’s bed to help it stay clean and smell fresher.

The lifespan of a pillow mainly depends on its material, whether that be foam, feathers, or another type of synthetic fill. But once a pillow of any kind loses its shape, it's time to say goodbye. Like mattresses, pillows can harbor dust mites, dead skin cells, and sweat over time. You can prolong a pillow's life with a pillow protector—Soto likes the Serta Power Chill—but you should still swap those out every year or so.

When to replace: two to three years for sheets; five to 10 years for duvets

Bed linen care and personal hygiene dictate the condition of your linens. Similar to pillows, sheets and duvets can accumulate dust mites, allergens, and bacteria, especially if you allow kids or pets into your bed.

“Over time, duvets lose their fluffiness and insulating properties due to regular use and washing,” says Rebecca Bobroff of Rebecca Bobroff Design.

“At a minimum, wash pillowcases once a week,” says Danielle Hurhula, head of home merchandising for Quince. “I’d recommend bumping washes up to twice a week if you suffer from allergies or asthma, sleep with hair or skincare products, or have sensitive or acne-prone skin.”

HVAC maintenance experts, including Energy Star, recommend checking filters monthly and replacing air filters every three months to help your system function properly and keep dust and allergens out of your home.

The nonstick coating on your cookware can wear off or become damaged over time. This decreases a pan's efficacy and also makes it a health hazard—the coating may start to flake off into your food.

“To extend their life, always use nonmetal utensils and hand-wash your pans instead of placing them in the dishwasher,” Hayslette says.

Staying cozy is a top priority in winter—whether you're curling up on the sofa or getting snug under the covers. But turning up the heating isn’t always the answer: some everyday habits can leave your home colder while pushing your bills higher.

From setting the thermostat too high to neglecting to bleed radiators, small heating mistakes can affect how warm your home feels and how much energy you use.

To help you stay comfortable without wasting energy, we asked heating experts to share the most common mistakes—and the simple fixes that can make your home warmer and your bills easier to manage.

When the chill sets in, it’s tempting to whack the thermostat up a few notches for a quick blast of warmth. But this common habit does more harm than good. “While it won’t heat your home any faster, what it will do is waste energy and therefore waste money,” say the heating specialist experts at Heatforce. “Each degree above 66 degrees Fahrenheit can add a whopping 10 percent to your annual heating costs.”

The smarter approach is to keep your thermostat at around 66 degrees when you’re at home and awake, then drop it by four to six degrees overnight. That way, you’ll stay comfortable without sending your bills soaring.

When arranging furniture, it’s easy to prioritize style over function—but placing bulky pieces too close to your radiator can drastically reduce its efficiency.

“Make sure that furniture and curtains aren’t obstructing this area of your home, as heat needs to be able to circulate freely,” says Amthal Karim, head of design at Furniture & Choice. “Where space allows, move sofas, armchairs, and other large pieces of furniture away from radiators to help warm air circulate more effectively around the room.”

Scott Byrom, a home energy expert at The Energy Shop, adds: “A large piece of furniture placed against a radiator can absorb a significant amount of the heat it emits, meaning you're paying to warm the back of your sofa rather than the room. Pulling furniture just six inches away allows warm air to rise and move freely, and you'll feel the difference almost immediately.”

Warm air can escape through gaps around doors, windows, and letterboxes, leaving rooms colder and forcing your heating to work harder—which can push up your bills over winter.

“Many older homes with hardwood front doors will experience worse drafts in the winter as they are susceptible to expanding in heat and contracting in the cold, meaning gaps around the frame will become even wider,” warns Oliver Novakovic, head of innovation at David Wilson Homes. “To combat this, invest in a draft exclusion runner to go along the bottom of your door. You can also purchase lengths of gap sealer to run around the frame, preventing cold air from blowing in around the door frame.”

Draft blockers, letterbox brushes, and simple sealant strips are all inexpensive fixes that can help a home retain heat far more effectively.

If you’re relying on an old heated blanket to stay warm, check its age and condition. It may no longer heat as effectively, leaving you tempted to turn up the thermostat and spend more on heating.

“Older electric blankets or those without overheating protection can pose a fire hazard,” says Adele Brennan, home electrical buyer at Vonhaus. “However, there are newer, innovative electric blankets that come equipped with an automatic shut-off function.”

A newer blanket with an automatic shut-off function is a safer choice, but using one to compensate for a cold room won’t fix the underlying heating problem.

It might feel indulgent to keep the heating running from dawn until dusk, but it’s an expensive habit that can quickly inflate your energy bills. “Instead, schedule your heating for convenient times,” says Stephen Hankinson, heating expert at Electric Radiators Direct.

This way, you’ll enjoy warmth exactly when you need it, without wasting energy. Just don’t forget to adjust your schedule when the clocks change.

It's easy to focus on simply switching radiators on and off, but regular maintenance is just as important. Bleeding your radiators is essential for keeping them running efficiently. “After a while, pockets of air can get trapped within the radiators, causing cold spots and preventing hot water from flowing through,” explains Hankinson. “The usual sign your radiator needs bleeding is that the top section is much cooler than the bottom.”

To fix this, switch off your heating system, use a radiator key to open the valve, and keep a towel handy to catch any drips of water. Doing this at least once a year will keep your heating even and effective.

Drying clothes on radiators is common in colder, wetter months, but it can stop heat from circulating around the room. “Having your boiler serviced annually is a great action to take to prevent boiler breakdowns, as well as improve its efficiency. Pictured above: A leather seat in a space designed by Jill Tran of Tran + Thomas Design Studio.

But the key lies in how well you care for your leather furniture. Leather may be lower maintenance than fabric upholstery, but it still needs occasional TLC to last.

We reached out to textile experts for tips on how to extend the life of leather furniture. Here's how often you should clean and condition these pieces, and how to tell the difference between normal patina and damage that needs attention.

Lisa Beach is the director of operations at BenchMade Modern, a furniture company that specializes in leather. According to her, cleaning your leather furniture regularly protects it from damage.

She recommends vacuuming your leather furniture and wiping it down with a dry cloth any time you notice dust, pet hair and dander, or crumbs starting to accumulate. This can range from every one to two weeks, depending on how often you use the furniture. The goal is to prevent these particles from settling into the furniture and possibly scratching the surface.

In general, she warns against using a damp cloth during these quick and frequent cleanings. "For semi-aniline or protected leathers, you may wipe down your furniture every few months with a damp cloth, but be sure to follow with a dry cloth to remove any excess moisture," she says. "Natural full-grain leathers are more absorbent, so we don't recommend using damp cloths or moisture unless you need to clean a spill or stain."

Leather furniture also benefits from a deeper clean with a specialized pH-balanced product every few months.

Shawn McGowen is the CEO of Leather Honey, a family-owned company that has been producing leather care products since 1968. He suggests blocking off time for a deep clean every three to six months, and following it up with a leather conditioner.

Leather naturally ages and develops a patina over time. McGowen says patinaed leather shows off a slightly darker color, softer sheen and smoother feel. You'll first notice this starting to develop in the places you sit and rest your arms most often.

"This is where your natural body oils slowly burnish the leather’s surface, giving that lived-in look that people seek after," he says. "On the flip side, damage often occurs due to neglect and looks more like cracking, flaking, stiffness, or wrinkling that doesn’t go away with cleaning. Patina comes from properly using your leather while damage comes from neglect."

Note that leather furniture can fade instead of darken with age if it sits in a particularly sunny room. However, this doesn't mean you need to keep the curtains drawn or move your leather furniture to a darker corner. McGowen says that at-home recoloring balms or leather dyes are very easy to apply to small areas.

Even if you're a very determined DIYer, it's important to know when it's best to reach out to a leather care professional to restore your furniture. Otherwise, you may end up doing more harm than good.

"When leather becomes too far gone and starts to show signs of neglect or wear such as tears, holes, large areas of discoloration, or stains, that is when the DIY becomes more difficult and potentially a waste of time," McGowen says. "These should often be handled by professional leather care specialists because they have years of experience in color matching and upholstery, and have a wide range of tools needed for larger restoration projects."

#6895. Presidents, Bullfighters, and California—Elaine de Kooning Painted Whatever She Wanted

And you can see it all at a new retrospective at Berry Campbell gallery in New York

In the history books, Elaine de Kooning is often eclipsed by her husband, the famed painter Willem. This fate befell the women in the abstract-expressionist movement, including Lee Krasner, Grace Hartigan, Joan Mitchell, and Helen Frankenthaler, who became known as the “Ninth Street Women,” all of whom received their flowers after decades of being overshadowed by their male counterparts. But you simply cannot tell the story of mid-century art—and abstract expressionism—without Elaine.

De Kooning, who died in 1989 at 70, sat at the epicenter of the art world for five decades. She deftly balanced two parallel practices in portraiture and abstract expressionism, unafraid to allow these genres to intermingle. Beyond her painting, she founded the artist collective the Club in Greenwich Village and wrote art criticism for Art News, celebrating artists like Arshile Gorky and Jackson Pollock. President John F. Kennedy, New York artist Toni Ross, former Art News editor in chief Thomas Hess, and American painter Alex Katz are among the artist’s impressive roster of sitters. A number of these portraits currently hang at New York’s Berry Campbell gallery. Each image frames its subject with expressive brushwork and vivid, bright colors. Among these works, most featuring starry figures, is a young boy in a cowboy hat, featured in the painting Jemp #1 (1963). This little kid is De Kooning’s nephew and the reason the show exists.

Gallerists Martha Campbell and Christine Berry connected with Chris Luyckx, the nephew, who had preserved De Kooning’s paintings for decades and felt her work deserved renewed attention. By the time they met, roughly a decade had passed since De Kooning’s last notable show, a retrospective at the National Portrait Gallery in 2015, followed by smaller shows that focused on specific periods of her life. “By doing these individual shows, you miss the incredible through line of her work from the late 1940s until the day she died in 1989,” says Campbell. “It goes with our ethos of always believing in the artist from the very beginning until the very end.” Berry Campbell’s show, simply titled “Elaine De Kooning” and on view through December 19, celebrates the artist in her entirety, featuring around 30 works sourced directly from the family’s collection.

De Kooning’s oeuvre retains its wonder by evading classification, collapsing boundaries between portraiture and abstract expressionism to forge a distinct visual sensibility. Part of her practice fit firmly in the world of abstract art. Paintings like Untitled (1960) reveled in obscuring the world around her; here, she depicts New York’s verticality. “Elaine de Kooning was an abstract expressionist, but as a woman artist, she didn’t have dealers breathing down her neck saying, ‘Paint more of what we see as abstract expressionism,’ ” Campbell says, noting that this provided the artist with wiggle room.

This should not overshadow her exceptional command of pure abstraction. The 11-foot-wide California (1961), a barrage of vivid colors underscoring De Kooning’s compositional mastery, sits at the very back of the gallery. Berry affirms “it is the most important piece in the exhibition,” noting that “there is no other painting of that scale or magnitude or from that time period on the market, because every other painting that is like that is in a museum collection.” Doubling down on its consequence, Campbell recalls, “There’s a great note that we found from [legendary art dealer] Joan Washburn that says that when she was her dealer, she said, ‘This is the best painting that Elaine de Kooning has ever painted.’ ”

Elements of her abstract work often defined her portraiture. Energetic bursts of bright paint consumed her subjects. Tom Hess (1963), for instance, features the titular journalist among flurries of orange, yellow, and red paint, leaving most of the canvas untouched. “She was an abstract expressionist, but within her own definition of what that was, so she used these abstract expressionist brushstrokes to glorify what she loved.”

De Kooning loved her subjects, occasionally obsessing over one topic and painting it repeatedly, ranging from bullfighting and baseball to the painter Aristodimos Kaldis and President Kennedy. This new show highlights all of these hyperfocused periods, including a standout from her paintings of the former president. Her connection to him started in 1962, when Kennedy sat for De Kooning in Palm Beach to complete a commission for the Harry S. Truman Presidential Library & Museum. The fixation intensified after the president’s assassination in 1963. JFK #7 (1963), on view at the gallery, is only one example of a year’s worth of works.

“She did get fixated on JFK, because she was painting him and then he was shot,” Berry explains. “That was pretty shocking. She painted him for a full year without stopping. People only focus on JFK. But she did portraits from the beginning, in the 1940s, all the way through. She did self-portraits, as well as portraits of her neighbors and her boss, Tom Hess. The portraits are a through line of her career.”

A sophisticated command of color is evident across De Kooning’s entire body of work. Bright colors clash in her abstract works, and in her portraiture, color builds texture. Perhaps most surprising, however, is that De Kooning often avoided looking at color when creating her images. For instance, she preferred to use black-and-white newspaper clippings as references for her basketball paintings, imbuing them with color guided by her personal sensibility. “Color is key to what she does,” Berry explains, pointing to the portrait of poet Frank Lima, a friend with a drug problem whom De Kooning rendered in muted greens.

One painting, Bacchus #20-G (1983), an image of Jules Dalou’s The Triumph of Silenus painted six years before her death, epitomizes what’s so striking about De Kooning’s paintings. The lush painting features the sculpture’s twisted bodies, rendered in an opulent violet, with a backdrop of verdant green. It demonstrates her capacity to seamlessly balance portraiture and abstract expressionism, a testament to her unwavering devotion to her distinct visual language. This vision, after years on the fringe, finally receives the flower it deserves, alongside, rather than below, her husband.

The singer’s new Gothic country album, Muse, releases on November 6

Jesse Jo Stark wrote her latest album, Muse, in the wake of heartache. Stark keeps pressing on, no matter what. Outside of songwriting, she plays a key role in Chrome Hearts as the daughter of founder Richard Stark. She spent her formative years surrounded by musicians, artists, designers, and every kind of creative person imaginable, all of whom inspired her to pursue her own vision. That world endlessly inspires her to create, even when she feels low. “I’ve worn things that are absolutely horrible, and I’ve written songs that I don’t necessarily love,” she says, “but when you grow, you go back to a less constructed version of yourself.”

This more authentic version of herself fuels Muse. She shed some of the macabre edge of her debut album, 2022’s Doomed, in favor of a softer reflection on her life, love, femininity, and the natural world that inspired these songs. But above all, Stark offers a reflection on her life being put on a pedestal, a narrative that deeply considers the women in her life, from friends to her mother, Laurie Lynn Stark. This 13-track Gothic country album appropriately conveys Stark’s energy, with visuals described as “somewhat of a horror film, but I’m wearing a lace skirt.” She wrote on her Instagram, announcing the album, that the theme is “fragile but fierce femininity,” best to “enjoy with a glass of red.”

For me, women have this pivotal point in their life—hopefully, at one point—where they regain their strength and voice, and this album was that for me. And then when you write, you really write your first song. To create something out of nothing is really funny because the fact that we think we get to do that is funny to me, but also so freeing and amazing. You never want to stop.

#6861. Melitta Baumeister Is the Designer Keeping New York Weird

The bold designer is braving the New York fashion landscape with unique shapes and high concepts.

Only at a Melitta Baumeister presentation in New York would you find the artist Kembra Pfahler playing a tuba, Paola Castro doing backflips, and Colleen Litchfield sculpting clay on the floor with sneakers gliding by on motorized platforms with chunky tires. “I really loved the idea of just doing flips in your living room,” the designer tells ELLE of her event last month. Sitting in her Greenpoint atelier in Brooklyn, Baumeister is wearing one of her signature black pointy baseball caps covered in pleated fabric. It’s become such a trademark for her that the people who know her best barely recognize her without it.

But that’s not all she’s known for. Since launching her own fashion line over a decade ago, Lady Gaga and Rihanna have worn her work, and the label has become a favorite of fashion insiders, with over-the-top conceptual shows, directional collections sold at places like Dover Street Market, and sporty collaborations. Her latest foray is a second collaboration with Nike, which involved the aforementioned performance-art-centric presentation to launch her rework of the Tabi-silhouetted Rift. Her take is a chunky, clay-inspired wedge of a shoe with gasp-inducing details like eyeball motifs and puffy logos.

Baumeister’s work has a cult following because it puts a focus on handmade details in an unusual, raw way. But she also has always gravitated towards big, voluminous shapes that take up space in an uncompromising way, with an element of sportiness. At her New York Fashion Week debut, for instance, Paralympian sprinter Scout Bassett opened the show. Sculpted dresses with bell-shaped bottoms, architectural pointed blouses, and surreally satisfying pleated dresses were put on full display with banana-shaped pouches, spiked necklaces, and, of course, that signature hat done in various colors like lemon yellow.

“I think because there’s so much out there, I feel like making statements to show creativity,” Baumeister says. “I feel like that’s why you have to push because there’s just too much boring stuff out there.” The designer describes her house codes as O lines and A lines, always with a voluminous shape. “My approach is always to make a garment kind of feel three-dimensional. There might be a drape from the side view, but it’s all very considered. It comes from hand draping, working with the fabric, and seeing what the fabric can do with its structure.”

Born in a tiny town in the Black Forest in southern Germany, Baumeister went to tailoring school at the age of 16 before studying fashion design at the Pforzheim University of Applied Sciences. She later moved to New York City to study at Parsons in the MFA Fashion Design and Society program and officially founded her line when she graduated in 2014. In 2023, she won the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund, and in 2025, she won the National Design Award for Fashion Design.

Earlier this year, the Costume Institute at the Metropolitan Museum of Art acquired her “Venus” dress and exhibited it at the “Costume Art” show. She works alongside her partner, Michal Plata, who also serves as the brand’s art director. The city continues to inspire her: “the energy is so special and so strong here that I feel like it kind of gives me that sort of harshness in design,” she says. “We’re very bold and kind of very daring. I don’t think I would do the same in Germany in my hometown.”

Though many of her pieces are designed in charcoal black, when she’s not working in neutrals, it’s neons. “I really want color to feel strong and bold rather than kind of too soft or too safe,” she says. Color is a storytelling method for her, and the brighter colors also represent energy. Baumeister continues to pull from the earliest days of her brand, like when she created clothing out of poured silicone. “I’m still very inspired by that, because it was not about caring about wearability, it was more like an art piece than clothing.” One of her brand missions is to show that garments don’t have to be traditionally sewn, or conventionally shaped. Another pillar for her is the accessories and footwear. “A bag talks so much about the character of a woman, and the shoe as well, because you can feel more feminine or more masculine or more in a certain way,” she says.

At the moment, she’s busy working on her own line (with a potential return to fashion week on her mind) after finishing her latest Nike collaboration, which just launched. The Nike collaboration includes clothing in addition to the shoes, like sculpted sweatpants accented with nose-shaped keyrings. “I always think shoes or sneakers nowadays look so much that they’re coming out of a machine and no one touched them and they’re just perfect,” she says of the collection. “I wanted to highlight that things are made by hand and there is a lot of hand work also in shoes.”

For the occasion, she actually hand-sculpted the shoes with clay in her atelier to form the final mold. That’s part of the reason why the presentation was so theatrical and involved a live sculptor: “I wanted to show how things are made. I want people to come to the thought that there’s no God-given talent, and you’re not born as a final product. Things have to be built from the ground up. There’s always another thing that you have to work on.”

That’s what makes her work so exciting. Baumeister is still most interested in what fashion can become when it refuses to conform to the typical shape.
