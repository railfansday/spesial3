#7000. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The company also cited an IDC projection that the number of AI agents could reach 1.3 billion by 2028.

Sophos researchers reported observing attackers use AI agents within a customer's network to build and test nearly 80 malware modules.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6996. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

For more information, go to the Microsoft blog.

#6991. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6980. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6973. Inside the Lavish Estates Where The Gilded Age Was Filmed

These locations are straight out of 1880s high society.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Why should the Brits have all the fun? The writer of Downton Abbey, Julian Fellowes, went stateside with the HBO series The Gilded Age, which tells the story of New York City high society in the 1880s amid the halcyon days of the industrial boom. The series explores the tension between old-money elites and their escalating feud with the nouveau riche neighbors who threaten the established social order. Meanwhile in real life, powerhouse families like the Vanderbilts and the Carnegies left their opulent footprints on Newport, Rhode Island, in the form of their famously ostentatious and absolutely gargantuan “summer cottages.” Circumstances which lend themselves to set-design magic.

In honor of season four of The Gilded Age, which premiered November 1, we’re revisiting the real-life settings that have helped bring the series to life. The social alliances may shift, but the architecture remains a dependable scene-stealer. Below, tour 21 properties that rank among the East Coast’s architectural crown jewels, from historic house museums to lavish mansions.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s ballroom and George Russell’s bedroom, as well as the billiard room.

These so-called dollar princesses and their families lived in Manhattan and spent their summers in Newport, Rhode Island. Of course, these industry magnates didn’t consider their infrequent visits (some only came a few weeks in a year) as a reason to exercise restraint. The mammoth Breakers mansion is one of four so-called summer “cottages” (read: castles) that were completed just blocks from each other in a building boom between 1892 and 1902. The mansion was designed by Richard Morris Hunt in the Renaissance Revival style, as a less-than-subtle symbol of the Vanderbilt family’s social and financial preeminence in the Gilded Age. It was architectural one-upmanship that is considered the grandest of all the manses, featuring ornate ballrooms, extensive art collections, manicured gardens, and the latest (then) in modern technology, including electric lights. It has 70 rooms, an interior using marble imported from Italy and Africa, and a library mantel purchased from a château in France.

“When you walk into the dining room of The Breakers, with its painting of the goddess Aurora on the ceiling 50 feet above you or see 500,000 cubic feet of marble in a variety of different hues, you can’t help but be awestruck by the incredible wealth of these Gilded Age tycoons,” says Trudy Coxe, the chief executive officer and executive director of The Preservation Society of Newport County, which has under its stewardship six of the grand mansions featured in the series.

The preservation society worked closely with the filming crew to ensure the safety of the objects and spaces. “We had long discussions about the billiard table at The Breakers,” she explains. “It could not be removed, but it was important to the scene being filmed. Rather than moving it, we worked with their props department to put an alternative covering on the table to simulate an actual game of pool.”

In the series, the Breakers’ neo–Italian Renaissance great hall and music room are featured as Bertha Russell’s (played by Carrie Coon) ballroom, which we see in the final ball scene. George Russell (Morgan Spector) also discusses business while playing billiards in the mansion’s massive billiard room.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s bedroom in the Russell residence

Marble House was built from 1888 to 1892 as a summer cottage for Alva and William Kissam Vanderbilt. It was also designed by Richard Morris Hunt, but in the Beaux-Arts style. For an American house, it was unparalleled in opulence, with a temple-front portico resembling that of the White House, which had been completed 92 years earlier in 1800 (lofty, no?).

Mrs. Russell’s bedroom, built on a soundstage in Bethpage, New York, was inspired by real-life socialite Alva Vanderbilt’s bedroom in the Marble House. The French Louis XV bed was painted and reupholstered in salmon-pink velvet, and the custom bedding used 300 yards of fabric that have been draped, cinched, and swagged.

And that’s just the textiles. Most of the room’s furniture is refurbished antique and everything in the room has been painted to match. Sometimes, Shaw notes, it was difficult to paint a lovely wooden antique piece. “The scenic artists would look at us and say, ‘You really want me to paint over the chestnut? Are you sure?’” says Shaw. “Yes.”

Consuelo Vanderbilt’s (Alva and William’s child) monochrome red bedroom in the mansion also serves as George Russell’s bedroom in the show.

Features as: The exterior of Sylvia Chamberlain’s house

Rosecliff was built for Nevada silver heiress Theresa Fair Oelrichs, whose father, James Graham Fair, was one of the four partners in the Comstock Lode. The famed architectural firm McKim, Mead & White was commissioned to design the mansion, which was modeled after the Grand Trianon, the garden retreat of French kings at Versailles. The French Baroque Revival gem was designed for entertaining at a grand scale—and it certainly fulfilled its purpose. Fair Oelrichs would become known as one of the three great hostesses of Newport.

The white front facade of this mansion, which recently got a $7.4 million renovation, is a stand-in for Sylvia Chamberlain’s (played by Jeanne Tripplehorn) house. You’ll see it in Episode 7, when Tom Raikes (Thomas Cocquerel) pays a visit.

Features as: The servant kitchen and the real-life Sarah Berwind’s bedroom as parts of the Russell residence

The Elms is a Classical Revival dwelling that was completed in 1901. Architect Horace Trumbauer designed it for the coal baron Edward Julius Berwind, taking inspiration from the 18th-century d’Asnières French château. It also houses an impressive collection of paintings and tapestries, as well as the latest technology of its age.

In The Gilded Age Seasons 1 and 2, The Elms stands in as part of the Russells’ lavish residence, including scenes in the kitchen and Bertha Russell’s bedroom. But it truly steps into the spotlight in Season 3, when it hosts a dazzling ball. And as a long-awaited confession of love looms (we won’t spoil from whom), the opulent Newport mansion proves to be the perfect setting for high drama and high society.

Each guest is granted a cinematic entrance, illuminated by a golden glow that bathes the grand façade—a new addition this season, as electricity is now becoming available. “The house’s interiors would not have been electrified yet, but people were beginning to use it for parties,” Shaw says. “That would mean they would have to have their own generator installed somewhere on the property to light up those lights.” To accomplish this, the crew went to specialty shops that make reproduction bulbs. “The original bulbs were much bigger than what we have in a household today,” Shaw adds. “Some of the lightbulbs we used were 10 inches in diameter, which is how big they were then.”

Features as: The exterior is used for Caroline Astor’s Beechwood house; the boardinghouse is used for a scene with Oscar van Rhijn; and another room serves as Agnes van Rhijn’s bedroom

Completed in 1852, Château-sur-Mer is one of the first grand Bellevue Avenue mansions of the Gilded Age in Newport, built by architect Seth C. Bradford for William Shepard Wetmore. Its interiors were designed by Ogden Codman Jr., who co-authored Edith Wharton’s Decoration of Houses. The mansion was host to many major parties, including the Fête Champêtre, an elaborate country picnic for more than 2,000 guests in 1857, as well as a debutante ball for Edith Wetmore in 1889. Wetmore’s son, George Peabody Wetmore, went on to have a distinguished political career as the governor of Rhode Island and, later, as a U.S. senator.

In The Gilded Age, the home’s exterior was used for Caroline Astor’s (Donna Murphy) Beechwood House. The hallway, dining room, and ballroom were used during Mamie Fish’s (Ashlie Atkinson) doll tea party. Several bedrooms serve as Agnes van Rhijn’s (Christine Baranski) bedroom, Oscar van Rhijn’s (Blake Ritson) bedroom, Anne Morris’s (Katie Finneran) bedroom, and Patrick Morris’s (Michel Gill) office. There’s also a scene filmed at the boardinghouse with Oscar van Rhijn, according to Patch.

Features as: The office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes

Hunter House was constructed in 1748 at the harborfront near the north end of the Newport Historic District at a time when the city was a bustling seaport in the British empire. The house was owned by wealthy merchants and overlooked the wharves that serviced their trading ships. It’s one of the finest examples of Colonial architecture in Newport, according to the Preservation Society. Inside, its resident families furnished the house with decorative arts produced by expert craftsmen, much of which has been preserved today. Such a luxurious lifestyle, however, depended upon the labor of enslaved people of African descent; recent research by the Preservation Society has identified at least 13 enslaved individuals who lived and worked at Hunter House. Today’s Hunter House honors their perseverance and incalculable contributions to Newport’s past.

One of the sitting rooms in Hunter House functions as the office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes, and is set fictitiously in Doylestown, Pennsylvania.

Features as: The Astor family summer home

Belcourt was completed in 1894 for Oliver Hazard Perry Belmont, a prominent American socialite and coachman who served in the U.S. House of Representatives. Like many of the Newport mansions, the Château-esque structure was completed by architect Richard Morris Hunt; it was designed to mimic the hunting lodge of Versailles, built for Louis XIII of France, but with a special ask to accommodate Oliver’s collection of horses and automobiles. In 1896, Oliver gave the house to his second wife, the former Alva Vanderbilt, who designed the neighboring Newport mansion Marble House. Alva used Belcourt as a gathering place for the elite of society, hosting lavish parties and balls and attracting notable figures in politics, business, and the arts.

In The Gilded Age Season 1, Belcourt shows up in Episode 8 as Mrs. Astor’s lavish new Newport home, which Mrs. Russell sneaks inside and is forced to flee to avoid a less-than-pleasant meetup.

Features as: The social gathering scene where Caroline Astor and George Russell meet

Built in 1867 by prolific Boston tea and china merchant Robert M. Cushing, the Ledges was originally the summer home of the Cushing family. This majestic oceanfront pile is perched on one of Newport’s most desirable peninsulas, overlooking Bailey’s Beach. It was built by architect John H. Sturgis, who designed a large, Stick-style cottage that was equal parts comfort and elegance. Today it features original furnishings, papered walls, and unique artwork, including a studio that was added to the side of the house for Robert’s artistic children.

Today, the estate remains in the hands of the Cushing descendants, who occasionally open the house up to film crews. It was featured a few years ago in the film Evening, starring Glenn Close and Meryl Streep. “Working at the Ledges was pretty extraordinary,” Gilded Age location manager Lauri Pitkus told The Providence Journal, “because I don't think I’ve ever been in a home with spectacular views out of every window.”

This historic house was built in 1864 for John Noble Alsop Griswold, an Old China Trade merchant and diplomat. The house is the work of Richard Morris Hunt; it was one of the earliest buildings in the American Stick style of architecture. The house has a complex roofline with a main mansarded section that is pierced by numerous gable and dormer sections. It boasts a number of balconies sheltered by deep eaves, with gable ends decorated with woodwork. Today, the house is a gallery location for the Newport Art Museum. In the series, the house features in Season 1 Episode 1, and again in Episode 5.

Features as: Mingling with the other elite scene

The International Tennis Hall of Fame and its perfectly preserved grass courts are in the Newport Casino, a National Historic Landmark property. The historic seven-acre estate was commissioned by New York Herald publisher James Gordon Bennett as a social club for the elite summer residents of Newport. The Newport News boasted three days after it opened in July 1880: “It is doubtful if a livelier place can be found.” Today, the space is recognized as the official hall of fame for the sport of tennis globally. Since 1955, 262 distinguished inductees representing 27 countries have been enshrined in Newport.

In the eighth episode of The Gilded Age Season 1, the characters are seen promenading around the piazza that overlooks what is known today as Horseshoe Court. A few characters are even playing tennis. “It was fitting to have that history depicted here, given The Gilded Age’s look into that time period,” the hall of fame’s Megan Erbes tells ELLE DECOR. “Several of our staff members got to work closely with the production team and actors, teaching everything from the history of our property to how to hit a proper forehand.” RI Monthly reported that crews returned to the courts last summer to film Season 2 of the show, so it’s possible that Newport Casino will make another appearance.

Features as: The parlor, library, entryway, and staircase of the Morris house, located on Fifth Avenue in the show; Mr. and Mrs. Morris’s bedrooms were filmed in the Newport mansions

William Smith Slater hired General William Walker to build a home as a wedding gift for his daughter. The result was this historic home, which was completed in 1877. The Victorian house is filled with treasures—the fireplace surrounds, for example, were carved by Charles Dowler, who came to the United States to produce arms for the Civil War. Today, the house is open to visitors and is known as the Clouds Hill Museum.

The Clouds Hill Museum is the only Rhode Island site outside of Newport that was used in the series. The crew scheduled filming for Season 1 in late February of 2021, when a few hundred people were on site during two days of filming, museum director Wayne Cabral told Warwick Online, noting that the taping finished around midnight on the second day of filming (after five days of work to prep the set). Almost all the parlor’s furniture was removed, and the head set designer brought in items sourced from local antiques stores in East Greenwich and North Kingstown, museum president Anne Holst added. The producers were adamant about removing the existing curtains, which were disintegrating, Holst added, so she negotiated to leave the replacements (which cost $10,000) with the museum after filming.

Features as: Visit to the home of Aurora Fane scene

The Gothic Revival–style Lyndhurst Mansion was designed in 1838 by Alexander Jackson Davis. Lyndhurst’s third owner, Jay Gould, is the real-life inspiration for the “new money” character George Russell. “He was fantastically ruthless in business, to an extent that Mrs. Astor would never let him in, he was never allowed into society, and she didn’t let his children in until he was dead,” creator, writer, and producer Julian Fellowes said in a podcast. “But he was an incredibly affectionate father and a very caring and faithful husband…and his children all had these nostalgic memories of Lyndhurst, their house on the Hudson.”

In Season 1 of The Gilded Age, Lyndhurst’s interiors are the home of characters Charles and Aurora Fane (Ward Horton and Kelli O’Hara), who are related to the van Rhijns and part of the “old money” side of society. The Lyndhurst lawns and grounds also doubled as Central Park and the Sheep’s Meadow in the opening sequence. #6945. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

#6898. This Breezy Los Angeles House Has “a Little Funk to It”

Designer Mike Moser creates a Santa Monica home that spans decades, styles, and influences—without sacrificing fun.

Above: The living room features a custom red marble coffee table, sofa, and side table by Mike Moser Studio. The wicker chairs are from Matahari; the vintage mirror is from A. Tyner Antiques, and the carpet is from Lawrence of La Brea.

When designer Mike Moser first saw the Santa Monica house, it hadn’t been touched since the ’90s, and no one had lived in it for over a decade. He knew it would require a full gut renovation. But Moser saw that as an opportunity for his clients—UTA’s chief communications officer Cassandra Bujarski, media executive Jason Bergsman, and their daughter—to get exactly the house they wanted. A place that’s at once a little bit Spanish, a tad preppy, and very Californian.

Moser, a Los Angeles-based interior designer who opened his eponymous firm in 2018, met the clients through their real estate broker. The agent, a friend of Moser’s, called him in while the couple was in escrow, asking him to help them imagine what this crumbling house could become with the right eye and guiding hand. They ended up moving forward with the house and hiring Moser to lead the renovation.

Bujarski’s parents were antique and art collectors and she had a vision for the home as a place that would honor that past and help her pass that legacy down to her own child. “Whatever we do,” Moser remembers her saying, “I want it to really feel collected and eclectic.” Other than that guideline, Bujarski and Bergsman gave Moser free rein—almost.

“That was really her only directive,” he says. “She was like, just have fun and play and be colorful and try new things.”

Moser leaned fully into this eclecticism, pulling references that span eras, regions, and styles. He cites 1920s and ’30s Los Angeles houses, Mexican architect Luis Barragán, Milan’s Villa Necchi, and hacienda style all as influences in the house’s design. In the dressing room, Moser added a “menswear, haberdashery, Ralph Lauren feeling,” he says—fitting, as Moser once worked for the brand. He calls this potpourri of inspiration an “amalgamation”: “It’s easy breezy California and we wanted to add a little funk to it.”

Los Angeles is the place to mix and match styles. It’s a city where modern mansions share blocks with Tudors and Craftsman; Spanish Colonials bump up against French Normandy houses. “The beauty of California, especially L.A. architecture, is it's a melting pot,” Moser says. “It's all really about these weird artists and creatives back in the ’30s, ’40s, ’50s that just were marching to the beat of their own drum. So I was like, let's do the same.”

Moser and his team gutted the Spanish-style house and stripped it down to the studs. They had to modernize all the internal systems, bring in new steel and windows, open up the layout to create more “breathing room,” as Moser calls it, and replace the laughing plaster walls. Despite the major work required to make the house livable, Moser still wanted to honor what it used to be. He added Saltillo replica floors and was able to keep the original wooden beams in the living room. In the dining room, he added new wooden beams, calling back to those in the living area, but had artist Nicke Valle paint them with swirling yellow and red spirals.

If the structural elements pay homage to the space’s origins, the furniture is tailored precisely to its new residents. The dining sideboard houses her china; a vintage Swedish armoire in the family room holds her mother’s Murano candlesticks. In the dining room, the dishware mounted on the wall is vintage Majolica, all with a seafood theme, that Moser and Bujarski hunted down—at auction, on eBay, in antiques stores—in the six months before the install. Throughout the home, “there’s a sense of reveal,” Moser notes.

“I always want to be a chameleon,” Moser adds. “I live as a joyful person. The first thing I say to a client when I take on a project is ‘If this isn't fun, we're doing it wrong.’”

#6895. Is There a Hookup to Relationship Pipeline?

Yes, it’s true: my relationship started off as a hookup. My boyfriend—who I now love, am fully committed to, and have way too much fun in Costco with—used to send me 2AM “WYD” texts and avoided any sort of conversation about the future.

Yes, I made it out of the situationship trenches. I’ll hold for applause.

While I would like to pretend that I’m better than everyone else, the reality is that there is no secret method to turning your situationship into a real relationship. Sometimes—like in my case—it’s just science.

A 2018 study revealed that sexual desire can actually encourage people to engage in behaviors that help initiate romantic relationships. Through different activities like slow dancing, researchers found that sexual attraction made people want to get physically closer, mirror each other’s behaviors, become more emotionally responsive, and even become more helpful.

We often hear of relationships that begin with emotional connection and then turn into something more romantic (and eventually physical), but the other side of the coin exists—and I’m proof of it.

“Sometimes physical attraction is what initially brings two people together, and that attraction creates opportunities to spend more time together, get to know each other, and potentially discover a deeper emotional connection,” licensed psychologist Dr Matthew Leahy tells Cosmopolitan. “What starts as something purely physical can open the door to something much more meaningful.”

I started hooking up with my now-partner purely because he was hot. Turns out, he was great in bed, too. And because we enjoyed having sex so much, we just ended up spending a lot of time together—so that we could have more of said sex, of course. Eventually, in between water breaks and refueling with Uber Eats orders, we got to know each other. I remember being shocked at what a great guy he ended up being—you know, when we actually took the time to get off each other and talk. Eventually, the booty calls turned into regular phone calls, and late night hookups turned into week-long sleepovers.

But our romantic relationship wasn’t inevitable just because we were sexually attracted to each other. We had to have a lot of conversations about our values, our hopes, and most importantly, what we wanted out of a relationship.

There were also a lot of check-ins I had to do with myself. The sex was great, so I had to constantly make sure I wasn’t confusing a sexual connection for an emotional one (AKA being “dickmatized”). Dr Leahy explains that one way to do this is to ask yourself objective questions about the other person: Are they asking you questions? Is the communication strong? Are they paying attention to you? Do you have things in common besides sexual desires?

“I think it’s good to always take a step back, have some self-awareness, and truly ask yourself: Is this going somewhere? Do I want it to go somewhere, or am I OK with it the way it is?”

Coming to terms with the reality of the situation is hard, but the hardest part of it all? You have to actually put yourself out there—especially if you really want to be with that person.

“Communicating how you truly feel about somebody and what you’re looking for can happen at any time in a relationship,” Dr. Leahy says. But when you know you’re falling hard for someone, the sooner you express those things the better.

I think it’s important to take a beat and remind you that developing feelings for someone you’re constantly being sexually intimate with is totally normal. I know we’re doomed to a dating era of nonchalance, but there’s nothing wrong with liking someone you’re exchanging bodily fluids with—especially if you’re spending a lot of time together.

You just have to know the difference between when your hookup is veering into a romantic connection and when it’s purely physical.

Before you send this article to your situationship, there’s a reality check that needs to happen. Movies like Friends With Benefits and No Strings Attached made us believe that any situation where we’re hooking up with someone we like as a person is bound to lead to us falling in love. I’m sorry to tell you this isn’t true, and he won’t be waiting for you with a flashmob.

I had situationships before my current boyfriend that went nowhere but down the toilet. While the sexual chemistry was there, nothing else quite was.

So how do you tell when something is just a hookup versus the potential of something more?

“It’s important to pay attention to how the other person responds when you communicate what you’re looking for,” Dr. Leahy says.

My partner and I actually stopped talking for a while after I first initiated a conversation about wanting more than just sex. Knowing that I couldn’t just continue to hook up with him without my feelings getting complicated, I cut things off.

“If you’ve been clear about wanting something more and the other person isn’t showing that same interest, you have to be willing to recognize that,” Dr. Leahy says.

We went our separate ways and grew as people. Years later, we reconnected and found ourselves on the same page.

While I’d love to tell you that this is how it always works out in the end—it’s simply not true. Unfortunately, sometimes no matter how much we like someone—despite having intense sexual chemistry—nothing comes of it. Even if you both do have romantic feelings, you both need to be in a place where you want to pursue a relationship. And had I let myself linger in the liminal space of hooking up with someone I had romantic feelings for, I wouldn’t even be in the right headspace to be in the healthy relationship I’m in now—even if it was with someone different.

“Sometimes we get caught up in hoping a relationship will become something different, rather than accepting what it actually is,” Dr. Leahy says. “You can’t convince somebody to want the same kind of relationship you do, and that’s an important part of knowing when it might be time to move on.”

So if you’re currently hooking up with someone you’re starting to develop feelings for, maybe it’s time to have a talk with yourself, and then them. You’ll either end up in a relationship, or better off for knowing that they don’t feel the same way.

Leroy Street Studio designed this Water Mill retreat around a large blended family—and a landscape that changes with every season.

Adopting a pinwheel-type plan, the striking 14,000-square-foot house features multiple wings radiating from a central core. These spaces vary in scale and height, and some feature deep overhangs, providing shade and amplifying the design’s dynamic horizontality. “The oversailing roofs create a solar brim that helps keep the rooms cool in the summer,” says Alex Witko, a senior associate at Leroy Street who served as the project lead. “They also amplify the views, including from the primary bedroom, where you can just sit and watch the herons on the nearby spit of land that is a protected wildlife preserve.”

Softening the formal rigor, most of the house is topped with lushly planted green roofs and terraces that offer distinct vistas, while the extensively glazed exterior is clad in handmade white brick on the lower levels and dark-stained wood above. #6881. Designers Say This High-Gloss Paint Trick Can Make Your Kitchen Look Instantly More Expensive

Pictured above: This New York City kitchen by designer Francis Nicdao features a high-gloss-painted island.

Once you’ve pinpointed the ideal color palette for your kitchen, you might think that the work is done. But not so fast. The success of your shades really comes down to finish, and there’s real potential in choosing a high-gloss paint. After all, if your kitchen is the showpiece of your home, you should make it shimmer.

“There is a richness to high-gloss paint that really can't be achieved with any other finish or product, especially in deeper colors,” says Malorie Goldberg, a principal designer at Noa Blake Design. “A high-gloss finish adds interest to cabinets that makes them feel more like furniture, immediately elevating the overall feel of the space.”

This option might be tough to imagine, given that kitchens are typically painted in a matte or eggshell finish. But that doesn’t mean that a shinier upgrade is out of the question. “High-gloss kitchens can be in a small or large space. For a smaller space, a butler's pantry might be a great place to play,” notes Danielle Davis, founder and principal designer of 56th Street Design Studio. “A larger kitchen will likely have a lot of millwork, so high-gloss paint can be applied to surfaces where it can really shine.”

If you want to make a statement in your kitchen using the budget-friendly quality of paint, a high-gloss finish could be the right move. Here’s how the pros recommend pulling it off.

When you’re considering a high-gloss finish, think of how it can be used to complement the rest of your kitchen. If you’re leaning toward trending darker palettes—like chocolate browns, emerald greens, and deep plums—then a high gloss will help bounce light around the room and make undertones more noticeable throughout the day. But if you’re doing just fine with a neutral backdrop, then a high-gloss will only make that look brighter. Either way, “high gloss” is paint speak for highlighter.

“The way that light reflects off a high-gloss finish adds a brightness that can make the space feel more expansive, even in moodier tones,” says Ariel Fischer, who is also a principal at Noa Blake Design.

Fischer and Goldberg would likely pick a darker shade on kitchen cabinetry for this effect, which Goldberg notes works on ultramodern panels and more ornate fronts. But Jen Levy, interior designer at Surrounded by Color, says it’s OK to go lighter—while looking up. “I love a high gloss ceiling in a kitchen,” Levy says. “My team and I see it used either way, but my preference is to have this paint treatment punctuate a lighter, brighter space.”

Paint is often regarded as DIY-friendly, but choosing this finish probably shouldn’t involve you rolling up your sleeves. “Hire a professional,” Levy says. “High gloss involves a lot of prep work to look right.” Since the results should almost resemble a mirror, Davis echoes this advice. “If a surface isn't perfectly sanded or smooth, high-gloss will show those imperfections,” she says. “It will ultimately take away from that perfect finish you’re after.”

As far as the best location for high-gloss paint, Davis has one strong preference. “High-gloss cabinets, from a practical standpoint, are forgiving in terms of wear,” she says. “You can easily wipe fingerprints, spills, and food right off the cabinet with a microfiber cloth.” Keep in mind, though, that chips will be noticeable, so this probably isn’t the right finish for busy households with young children.

As you determine the remaining details of your kitchen with a high-gloss paint in mind, remember to keep it front and center. Everything else in this space has to complement that sheen in order for the effect to be successful. “In a rustic kitchen, or one where natural wood is prevalent, high-gloss paint may clash with the overall vibe,” Davis says. The opposite is also true: A modern kitchen with too many shiny surfaces will feel like a carnival fun house.

To pull your kitchen together, seek out countertops and flooring with a matte finish alongside lighting and hardware that has a slight sheen. The goal is to find ways to ground the room, even as you center its high-gloss boldness. “I would love to have a client who’s daring enough to do a high-gloss kitchen,” Davis says. “Nevertheless, high-gloss paint can still have a timeless quality, while still having a big impact on the room.”

#6880. These Are the Home Trends Gaining Ground for 2027, According to Zillow

A new analysis of millions of for-sale listings reveals the features sellers are increasingly highlighting.

Above: A color-drenched den on Long Island, New York, designed by General Assembly.

Homes are getting smarter, but sellers are also advertising places to unplug. Zillow’s 2027 Home Trends Report finds growing mentions of both AI and tech-free spaces, along with color-drenched rooms, home offices, and “catios.”

The report tracks year-over-year changes in mentions of hundreds of features across millions of for-sale listings. The results reflect how sellers and agents are marketing homes—not a survey of buyers or evidence that a particular feature will help a property sell. Still, they offer a look at the amenities and design details increasingly making their way into listing descriptions. Here are the main takeaways.

Mentions of AI are up 119 percent, the largest increase in this year’s report. Zillow points to AI-powered appliances among the features sellers are advertising, while mentions of app-controlled home systems rose 34 percent.

Spaces to unplug are also appearing 34 percent more often. The report describes an interest in both technology that simplifies daily tasks and designated places to take a break from it.

“Digital detox is the new luxury,” says Amanda Pendleton, Zillow’s home trends expert, in a statement. “Homes are getting smarter, but buyers also want tech-free zones where nothing needs charging. Sellers and their real estate agents are now highlighting spaces to unplug.”

Color-drenched spaces—rooms where the same color extends across the walls, ceiling, and trim—are up 84 percent in listing mentions, making them one of the report’s biggest increases. Zillow sees the approach as part of a move toward bolder, more polished interiors. Exterior finishes are gaining ground, too: painted brick is up 27 percent, and board-and-batten siding rose 24 percent.

Color drenching takes a shade beyond the walls and onto the ceiling, trim, and other architectural details. The approach has even spawned a bathroom variation: tile drenching, which extends the same tile across surfaces for a continuous look.

The language used to describe these homes is changing alongside the features. Mentions of “elevated” rose 71 percent, while “cozy” fell 22 percent, “eclectic” dropped 19 percent, and “boho” declined 13 percent. Cozy remains one of the most common words in listings, but it’s appearing less frequently than it did a year ago.

Kitchen language is moving in the other direction. Mentions of “chef’s kitchens” fell 21 percent, and “gourmet kitchens” declined 15 percent.

Mentions of multigenerational living jumped 58 percent. Zillow connects the increase to homes accommodating the “sandwich generation,” or people caring for children and older family members.

Features that can serve different ages and needs are also appearing more frequently. Grab bars are up 18 percent, wider doorways are up 13 percent, and nurseries rose 9 percent. The report places these changes alongside the growing emphasis on flexible rooms and spaces with more than one use.

The home spa is changing. Mentions of spa-inspired bathrooms rose 60 percent, and cold plunge pools increased 44 percent. Jacuzzis and jetted tubs, meanwhile, each fell about 9 percent.

Spaces for exercise are also gaining ground. Home gyms are up 18 percent, while golf simulators rose 29 percent. Together, the findings cover both workout spaces and amenities marketed around rest and recovery, rather than a single version of the home wellness room.

Cats get a category of their own this year. “Catios”—screened-in outdoor spaces that let indoor cats spend time outside—are appearing in 32 percent more listings. A place to get some air, watch the birds, and keep an eye on the neighbors—the cat has a wish list, too.

“Funnily enough, I’ve actually started doing yoga, which has been great for mobility and stretching out my lower back,” writes Chris, 35, who battles with a recurring quadratus lumborum (QL) issue. “I was always a bit hesitant to give it a go—not because I didn't think it would help, but because I couldn't get past the fact that I'd essentially be following a similar exercise plan to my mum! I think that’s been one of the bigger adjustments as I’ve gotten older: accepting that looking after your body sometimes means doing things you might have previously associated with an entirely different generation.”

“For me, I think the most important thing is to keep going in general,” adds Brian, who recently turned 42. “I remember when I was around 20 years old, my manager at the time, who was in his late 30s and retired from playing, was doing some running on his own before the team training session started. The lads were joking and teasing him about making a comeback, but I remember him saying, ‘If you stop moving at my age, you won't be able to start again.’ I think that’s good advice.”

In some way, I suppose it’s comforting to know that this is something we all have to deal with, whether you’re a Sunday league bench warmer or in contention for the Ballon d’Or. "With age, there’s just a higher incidence of soft-tissue injuries as your muscles lose strength and elasticity,” says Dr. Brian Forsythe, a sports medicine surgeon, associate professor at Rush University Medical Center, and recreational soccer player himself. According to Forsythe, who also serves as head orthopedic officer for MLS team Chicago Fire FC and president of the Professional Soccer Physicians Society, over time athletes across the skill spectrum will ultimately begin running into the same injuries.

You may not be able to halt the inevitable march of time, but there are certainly ways to minimize and slow its toll on your body. This requires some work but could ultimately be the difference between hanging up your boots and kicking on for another 10, 20, or even 30 years.

“It's up to you to take some responsibility,” Forsythe says. “Think about the lifestyle you had when you were 17 or 18—how you took care of your body and what you looked like. Then look in the mirror and ask yourself, ‘How much of my life now resembles what it was then?’ If you’re going out and having a few pints, understand that that inhibits your recovery and decreases protein synthesis. If you’re overweight, that has effects beyond just not looking great in your clothes. It’s increased joint load, increased rotational torque. You’re more likely to get injured. So you have to set realistic expectations of what your body is capable of doing. But you can definitely still do a lot.”

If you’re not ready to call time just yet, here are a couple of strategies you can start implementing today, courtesy of Forsythe and a handful of other experts.

“Number one, and it should be obvious, is just general conditioning,” says Forsythe. Being a slightly less sedentary human being during the work week means you won’t send your body into total shock when you decide to get going over the weekend.

“A lot of injuries come from that mismatch between what we do all week and the intensity we demand from our bodies on the weekend,” says sports medicine physician Dr. Alex McDonald. As Dr. James B. Chen, orthopedic surgeon at DISC Sports and Spine Center, puts it: “The biggest risk isn't necessarily your age, but your schedule.”

“When you think about playing in your teens and twenties, you were probably close to your ideal body weight,” says Forsythe. Ideal body weight, or IBW, refers to the weight at which your body is able to function most efficiently and without a heightened risk of injury. “All of the forces across your joints are multiplied almost exponentially with additional pounds on your frame,” says Forsythe. For example, “the force across your knee can be up to six times your body weight.”

“If you've ever been overweight and you climb up and down stairs and notice that the front of your knee hurts, that's because of those increased pressures and forces,” adds Forsythe.

Jogging a couple of miles a week may help you get through your Sunday workout our pick-up game, but it’s not doing anything to prevent in-the-moment injuries. To do that, you have to train the way you play—whatever game that is. “It's changes of direction," says Forsythe. It's rapid acceleration and deceleration. It's pivoting, twisting, turning.” Basically, bring back whatever drills you used to do on your sports team when you were younger, and adjust them for the demands of the sport you currently play.

No matter how frequently and comprehensively you train, a complete warm-up before any big athletic event is a non-negotiable. "It helps dramatically, which is why you'll always see professionals warm up for 30-45 minutes before a match,” says Forsythe. When it comes to soccer, FIFA actually has developed a warm-up program, called FIFA ll+, specifically for injury prevention. “It’s a free routine that only takes about 20 minutes, and includes running drills plus strength, balance, and landing exercises such as Nordic hamstring curls and single-leg balance work,” says McDonald. “It’s been shown to cut injuries by about a third, but it needs to be done at least twice a week—ideally three or four times a week—to really reduce the risk of injury.”

Stretching after your warm-up is important, too, but just be careful not to overdo it. “If you stretch too aggressively, you can actually predispose yourself to muscle tendon injuries,” says Forsythe. “First warm up, and then stretch out within your range of comfort. But right before a game is not the time to all of a sudden correct tight hamstrings.”

Often, being sidelined by an injury can be more painful than the injury itself, causing guys to get back out there earlier than they probably should. “A common issue that I see is when someone sustains a minor sprain or strain, and they return to sport-specific activity too early,” says Chen, “which can result in either worsening the injury or even picking up a secondary injury.” As annoying as it might be to sit out for 2 or 3 weeks with a strained hamstring, turning that into a tear will bench you for far longer. Believe me.

Playing sports in your 30s, 40s, and beyond also means coming to grips with your body’s increasing need for routine recovery between training sessions and games. "You have to recognize that your body needs more time to recover,” says Forsythe. You may have great cardiovascular fitness and feel ready to play on consecutive days, but your muscles and connective tissues need time to bounce back. “It’s important to respect fatigue, and ensure proper recovery after games or training,” says McDonald.

To be fair, this is universally sound guidance regardless of age. But it's advice that tends to take decades to sink in. “I think that one of the key advantages of playing as you get older is that you know your body better in your 30s and 40s,” Brian’s response concluded. “You understand what it's saying to you now. You might not have even listened when you were 21!”

#6806. To Penny, or Not to Penny?

Few shoes are as essential in the modern menswear landscape as the classic leather loafer. Whether you’re dressing up or dressing down, it is the “the Swiss Army Knife of shoes,” an any-occasion solution to many footwear conundrums. But within the broader loafer genus, one particular species—the penny loafer—includes a divisive design feature: a small, coin-shaped slot in the leather strap “saddle” across the vamp of the shoe.

As the lore goes, Ivy Style pioneers and sock-hopping teens in the mid-20th century liked to store a penny in their shoes as “payphone change.” But now that payphones are a thing of the misty-eyed past and the American one-cent penny is no longer minted for circulation, is it still cool to put a penny (or any other coin, or coin-shaped object) in your penny loafers? What is the line between timeless and passé?

We invited two of GQ’s most well-heeled staffers, associate grooming and wellness editor Tyler Chin and senior style commerce writer Gerald Ortiz, to ponder the pros and cons of the penny loafer.

Mahershala Ali, an ostensible penny proponent, in London on October 19.

Tyler Chin, associate grooming and wellness editor: Sure! I’ve seen so many guys wearing loafers these days, and yet still haven’t seen any of them harking back to the shoe’s origins with a penny in them. Maybe I’m not looking hard enough or I’m not in circles where they are doing that, but it’s an inoffensive and easy way to accessorize the shoe. Plus, I had an English teacher in high school who did it, and while she wasn’t particularly cool, I did think the sartorial choice was neat.

Gerald Ortiz, senior style commerce writer: No. I feel like guys who put pennies in their loafers are either try-hards, or just learned about that particular piece of prep trivia. The only acceptable instance to wear a penny in your loafers is if you’re really about that prep/Ivy style, like Preppy Pete, or the guys who frequent Alfargo’s Market (shout out).

TC: I think the penny would be the most boring and obvious choice. If a quarter could fit in there, I think it’d be so funny if you were at a vending machine and short a few cents and settled up with the coin in your shoe. One time, the button on my pants fell off on the street and I lost it, but imagine I had a spare button in my shoe? I could reattach it right there and then! In the hypothetical I’d also need to be toting around a sewing kit, but hey, I’d love to think I could be MacGyver in that scenario.

GO: It doesn’t have to be anything at all. But if you’re going to do it, I don’t see why it couldn’t be another coin or object. It could be a Chuck E. Cheese token. Whatever you put in the slot, it would still be wack.

TC: I just got back from a trip to Tokyo, and I had a lot of leftover yen coins. Two of them—the five and 50 yen—have a hole on them, which differentiates them from your boring American coin. I’d throw one of them in my shoe and be one of those people who never stops talking about their travel abroad.

GO: I saw on Instagram someone stick a hundred dollar bill into their loafer and that was funny for a second. Then I scrolled on to the next meme.

TC: I’m pretty sure a coin that is that close to the ground will surely have a patina before the end of the day. Also, I can’t remember the last time I’ve seen a shiny new coin—who carries loose change these days anyway?!

GO: You know, it might be kind of cool to see a penny completely covered in verdigris slotted into your loafers. But maybe the coin should match the sheen of your loafers. I dunno, just don’t do it and you won’t have to think about it.

TC: Everyone I know is very familiar with my love of penny loafers. Lately I’ve been wearing them with bootcut jeans that are just a little longer than usual, so they have a nice drape over the shoe. Top-wise, I’d go with a tucked-in button up, T-shirt, or sweater. Pretty much anything that would go well with sneakers would be equally suited for wearing with loafers. Which is nearly all outfits.

GO: Penniless or not, a pair of wide khakis with a clean-to-slight break and a striped button-up is a failsafe outfit. It wouldn’t be very inventive, but it would pass muster for most conceivable situations. Vintage straight-leg jeans with good fades, a fucked-up band tee, and a sportcoat is also a great look. But the best looks are always the unexpected pairings.

TC: It’s sort of like how guys will go on and on about having functional sleeve buttons on a suit jacket. Is there any situation where you’ll actually need to unbutton those or do you just leave one or two undone to “stunt” on your unimpressed bros? If you really want to put a coin in your shoe for the sake of accessorizing your footwear, then by all means get a shoe with a functioning penny slot and use it! But if you’re doing it just so you can say “Yeah, these joints are the real deal!” and use it as an excuse to talk about the history of the shoe and why yours are superior, then I’d rather you wear lace-ups.

GO: I think it’s fine, even preferable to have a faux penny slot. It’s like how jeans still come with the watch pocket. It’s purely vestigial at this point, but would feel incomplete without it.

TC: One of these days, I’ll be telling my great-great grandchildren about the origins of the penny loafer and they’ll be confused because they would’ve never heard or seen the one-cent coin before. I’ll whip one out of my shoe and instead of being impressed they’ll chalk it up to another one of Old Man Ty’s tales from his youth, and tuck me back into the nursing home they checked me into.

GO: Lol. I guess it’ll make the penny more covetable. But it’ll be a long time before the penny becomes that desirable. That said, I love this British brand Six 95 that’s designing cool pieces using coins, so I can absolutely see more labels farming pennies for their clothes and accessories.

TC: Men will break up anything other than a toxic relationship. Loafers did nothing wrong and sometimes it’s just the person wearing them that needs to hit the curb.

GO: It makes sense. It’s like any trend, really. Even if they’re as versatile as we’ve been propping them up to be, loafers reached an inflection point where, for some guys, they’re just not the vibe anymore. But I don’t think they’ll dip so low to be thought of as cringe or as an emblem of the 2020s. They’re as classic as it gets and they’ll go through their own ebbs and flows.

TC: A quarter to increase my net worth 25-fold.

She wants her fans to “experience the magic that comes with understanding someone else’s life, their point of view, and what’s making them feel inspired.”

Below, Paltrow speaks exclusively with ELLE about The Goop Guide, detailing her future hopes for the podcast, her dream guests, and more.
