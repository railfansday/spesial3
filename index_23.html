#6906. How to Talk to Your Friends and Family About the Trans Community, According to Experts

If you get flustered whenever you speak up about trans rights, you're not alone. That's why we put together this list of expert-approved talking points.

Since Donald Trump took his second term in office, he and his administration have issued a series of discriminatory executive orders restricting access to gender-affirming care for trans youth, banning trans women and girls from participating in women’s sports, declaring that the federal government will only recognize two genders (impacting identifying documents like passports and visas), banning transgender people from serving in the military, and so much more.

Thankfully, many of these executive orders are being challenged in court. But the messaging has already infiltrated the brains of Trump supporters (and beyond) far and wide, normalizing these hateful thoughts and actions in our country.

So what do we do? We speak out and spread facts. Especially with those closest to us who might not be informed or might not understand. That’s not always easy though, especially when emotions get involved and you end up getting upset and flustered. To help, I tapped three experts to create a list of responses to common (and problematic) refrains about the trans community you might come across in future discussions.

In any discourse with someone who doesn’t think like you, it’s always important to make sure the other party knows you’re actually also listening—not just arguing. But if tensions do get high, the Q&As below can serve as a script of sorts to fall back on when you want to make sure you’re hitting all the key talking points while supporting and speaking up for the trans community.

Dr. Meredith McNamara: Actually, some people are born with neither or with genitalia that doesn’t fit binary concepts of sex. What sex is someone who is born without a vagina but has XX chromosomes (MRKH Syndrome)? What sex is someone who has XXY chromosomes and little to no detectable testosterone levels in adulthood (Klinefelter syndrome)?

But let’s take a step back and look at this from a different angle: Gender and sex are different. Gender identity develops long before the physical change of puberty. People generally know their gender long before secondary sex characteristics develop, because gender identity has its own strong biological basis rooted in the brain and it exists separately from chromosomes, gonads, and hormones.

Gillian Branstetter: And why not? Every culture in human history has had people who reject their gender assignment in one way or another, and decades of feminist legal progress has challenged the idea that biology must be our destiny. Transgender people come from all walks of life—every ethnicity, every background, every country, every political party. All people deserve the freedom to be themselves without fear, and that includes transgender people.

Shane Diamond: Everybody has a gender: an internal sense of self as it relates to the gendered world around us. For many people, that inner sense of self matches the sex they were assigned at birth—for example, someone assigned female at birth who knows herself to be a woman. This simply means they are cisgender! For other people, their inner sense of self is different from their sex assigned at birth, causing an incongruence with who they know themselves to be and how the world sees them. This person might call themselves transgender. That doesn’t make anyone’s gender more or less valid than someone else’s—it just means they’re different. At the end of the day, we don’t expect everyone to know exactly what it means to be transgender, but we do expect people to respect each other, even when we are different.

Gillian Branstetter: Transgender people face discrimination, mistreatment, and abuse across our lives. We’re four times as likely to experience violence and much more likely to experience poverty and homelessness. While nobody should have to pay such a price just for being true to themselves, I hardly think anyone wants that kind of “attention.” Also, transgender people are raised around cisgender people all the time—it never seems to “rub off” on us.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: In my practice, I only see the opposite—most young people who are trans try to present as cis for a period of time to deflect attention or to not stand out. It’s never been harder to be trans. While many things can be socially influenced, we know that gender identity cannot. Studies have shown that trans people report a long-standing knowledge of their gender identity—it often takes years before they disclose that identity outwardly. Gender conformity is the real social contagion at play, not trans identity.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: Some people regret all types of medical care, whether it’s knee replacement surgery, spine surgery, or Accutane. With surgery and hormones, regret rates are less than 1 percent—among the lowest in all of medicine. But it’s also important to think about regret itself and what it means. People who express regret about surgery or hormones often say that it’s just too hard to be trans in an openly hostile society. For those people, it’s not health care at the root of regret—it’s housing discrimination, job loss, violence, and political targeting. Until we ensure the same dignities for trans people as we do for everyone else, we need to think about regret in dimensions and never use it as a reason to snuff out opportunities for care.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: You’d probably hold a door open for someone or make an effort to spell their name correctly in an email. You’d probably also expect others to get your pronouns right. Moreover, there are plenty of times when we simply can’t discern someone’s gender because they haven’t told us and we’ve never met them. We naturally revert to using gender-neutral pronouns in those cases. Keep that in mind and remember that something small can make someone else’s day.

Also, it’s totally normal to be nervous about getting this wrong. I get people’s pronouns wrong and it doesn’t feel great, but I own it and move on. I’ll usually say, “Hey, I got your pronouns wrong—next time, I’ll get it right!” It removes the awkwardness where the other person feels like they have to comfort the one making a mistake and it gets us back on track to whatever it was we were talking about.

Shane Diamond: I totally get it. It can feel unfamiliar to refer to someone using they/them pronouns, but we actually use those pronouns all the time for individuals. For example, if I found a backpack and was bringing it to a lost and found, I’d say, “Someone forgot their backpack.” I don’t know who forgot it, so using “their” acts as a singular person pronoun regardless of gender. It can take practice, but it’s not grammatically incorrect or even uncommon in our language. If using someone’s correct pronouns feels too clunky or too complicated, you can always skip pronouns and just use their name. But at the end of the day, using someone’s correct pronouns isn’t about you—it’s a way of showing respect for someone else.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: Let’s get back to biology for a second: Ovaries and testicles both produce estrogen and testosterone. So this statement has some fundamental flaws. But let’s also think about what would happen if we applied this statement’s logic broadly: We’d cosign a lot of bad health care. We wouldn’t offer testosterone replacement therapy to cis men with low libido, we wouldn’t help ease cis women into menopause safely or respectfully, and we wouldn’t support people who lose those gonadal functions due to cancer. We wouldn’t be able to suppress life-threatening menstrual bleeding in people with bleeding disorders like hemophilia! I don’t think anyone wants that. If there’s a seat at the table of good health care for these cis people, then there’s a seat at the table for trans people, too.

Gillian Branstetter: The treatments transgender people access are no different from the treatments millions of cis people rely on for their health needs. And importantly, decisions about medical care should be kept between patients and their doctors—not politicians.

Gillian Branstetter: The good news is you don’t have to understand everything about a transgender person to know they’re as deserving of respect and equal treatment as everyone else, and that’s all most are asking for. Every day we interact with people of different races, religions, and backgrounds, and we don’t need to be an expert in their experience to trust them with their experience and treat them with the same dignity we would want for ourselves.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: Imagine if you—as a woman—sprouted hair on your chest and had genitalia hanging outside of your body that made you feel completely unlike yourself. What if society would only treat you with dignity and respect if you wore masculine clothing? If you can step into that headspace for a second and experience a smidge of that discomfort, imagine feeling that every moment of your life. The fact that trans people can and do navigate gender dysphoria is beyond admirable. Let’s also reframe the concept of choice: People don’t want to be a gender—they are a gender. They choose whether to present themselves to society as that gender, and that choice is what gender-affirming care can facilitate.

Shane Diamond: We already have existing state and federal laws that protect people, specifically women, from predators, harassment, and assault. It’s already illegal to assault someone, regardless of gender or location. Despite fearmongering from anti-transgender voices, the truth is, transgender people are actually most at risk for being attacked in the restroom. Being that trans women are women and trans men are men, each should use the respective restroom that aligns with their gender. Ultimately, we’re all trying to do the same thing in restrooms—use a likely-under-serviced toilet and check our social media—regardless of our gender.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: Wow, this is news to me! Every patient I’ve had who’s gotten any form of gender-affirming surgery has gone through years of deep introspection about what’s right for them. Once they’ve arrived there, they’ve jumped through hoop after hoop after hoop to connect with the right providers.

Shane Diamond: First, let’s talk about what this really is: medically necessary healthcare for trans and nonbinary people. (“Gender-reassignment surgery” is also an outdated phrase that’s not commonly used anymore.) Second, have you ever tried to schedule any kind of surgery in this country?! Nothing in health care happens quickly or easily, and that’s not even factoring in maneuvering insurance coverage. It’s also important to remember that there’s no single way to be transgender or nonbinary, and that not every trans or nonbinary person will choose—or be able to afford—a medical transition. These decisions, like all personal and private health care decisions, are best left to patients, their families if applicable, and their health care providers.

Shane Diamond: It is not true that people are claiming to be transgender when they are not. Given how difficult it is to navigate our complicated and tedious health care system and all the discrimination and challenges trans people face, no one is faking being transgender for any advantage—be it in sports or anywhere else.

Out of the millions of global competitive athletes, there are only a handful of transgender people competing at high-level sports, and—despite sensational media narratives—we’re not always winning! It is especially important in the context of youth sports that all kids—transgender and cisgender alike—have an opportunity to participate in sports with their friends to learn valuable, lifelong lessons through being part of a team...without being subjected to gender- and appearance-based discrimination.

Ultimately, these anti-trans actions harm all girls and women, punishing gender non-conformity of any kind and imposing rigid gender roles and expressions on everyone.

Dr. Meredithe McNamara: The American Psychiatric Association doesn’t consider gender dysphoria to be a mental illness—it’s classified as a sexual health condition that arises from discordance between one’s physical body and sense of self. But let’s talk about the word “fix.” How? There is no alternative treatment for gender dysphoria beyond access to thoughtful, multidisciplinary gender-affirming medical care. It’s not right for everyone, but it’s right for many.

Shane Diamond: Being transgender and/or nonbinary is one of nature’s many variations—the way some people have blue eyes, brown eyes, or green eyes. Being transgender is an innate part of who someone is and there’s nothing anyone can do to change that. External forces attempting to change someone’s gender or sexual orientation are ineffective at best and extremely harmful at worst.

Transgender people are not the problem and are simply trying to live safely. The Trump administration has spent millions of dollars vilifying and discriminating against transgender people—why? The American public has many issues of importance, including the economy and climate change, and yet opponents of equality are wasting important resources and attention perpetuating hatred and divisiveness. If anything needs to be fixed, it’s our society's rigid rejection of anyone who is different.

#6871. MobLand Season 2’s Most Striking Watches

From Pierce Brosnan’s Omega to Paddy Considine’s Breitling.

The Harrigan men don’t do flashy. For a family sitting on a drugs fortune that stretches from north London to a sprawling estate in the Cotswolds, they dress like they’re popping out for a pint: good tailoring, wax jackets, country staples, and nothing with a logo you could spot from across the room.

Their watches are no exception. There’s nothing loud or iced ou—just good, solid classics chosen by men who know their stuff. And they really do: In the very first episode, it turns out that one of the family’s side hustles is trading stolen cars for watches. So, somewhere in the Cotswolds, a Harrigan is almost certainly arguing about reference numbers on r/Watches.

Look closer, though, and the watches say more than just “good taste.” Each matches the job the man does for the family, so that watch tells you exactly where he sits in the pecking order. From the boss (Pierce Brosnan) down to the man who’s doing his dirty work, here’s how they line up.

Pierce Brosnan plays Conrad, the family’s head honcho—the guy who runs the empire without getting mud on his brogues (most of the time, anyway). And when you're at the top, you don’t need a watch that does much. No stopwatch, no dive bezel, no second time zone (that’s what the staff are for). So, Conrad wears a dress watch, and a decorative one at that: a gold Omega Constellation, with a fluted bezel and pie-pan dial that are there, purely, to look good. Like Conrad, though, it’s tougher than it looks: the Globemaster was Omega’s first Master Chronometer, tested to fend off magnetic fields of 15,000 gauss.

Then there’s the in-joke: Brosnan wore an Omega Seamaster during his time as 007, so seeing him return to his roots is a little Easter egg for anyone who’s paying attention. The sporty diver has been swapped with something more expensive but less practical—would you expect anything less for a retired Bond?

Kevin is the heir—the son, played by Paddy Considine, who’s waiting for that top job with one eye firmly on his dad’s will. So, fittingly, his watch feels a little eager. It’s a Breitling Premier B01 (the flash of yellow under the strap gives it away). A strong watch, for sure, it’s just the kind you buy when you want to look like the boss before you’ve secured the promotion. And it tracks: When Willy Breitling designed the OG Premier in the 1940s, it was the brand’s first watch made purely for style.

The real tell, though, is that it’s still working harder than his dad’s. Conrad’s gold Omega just tells the time, because Conrad hasn’t had much else to do in years. Kevin’s chronograph comes with pushers, sub-dials, and a stopwatch, plus around 70 hours of power reserve. The watch of a man who’s close to the throne, but not quite there yet.

Paul (Emmett J. Scanlan) is the Harrigans’ head of security and Conrad’s right-hand man—the one who’s always just a step away. And when the family’s estate comes under attack in Season 2, he, of course, is the one holding the fort. So, as you’d expect, there’s no gold here. Paul, instead, gets a tool watch.

But it’s not just any old tool watch, it’s a serious tool watch: IWC’s Pilot’s Chronograph. From a long line of sturdy watches built for military pilots who needed to tell the time in an instant, it has a large steel case, big clear numerals, and it’s tough enough to survive being bashed around a little (which, in Paul’s line of work, is a regular occurrence). This one’s a vintage mechaquartz version which is a bit of a hybrid: a quartz movement keeps the time on track while the chronograph is mechanical. Part brains, part brawn. Sounds like the perfect head of security to us.

#6846. Are We Doomed to Get Cheated On?

We may earn commission from links on this page, but we only recommend products we love. Promise.

At this point, being cheated on feels like an inevitable part of the modern dating experience. Every other week, it seems, another man with a wife, a public-facing career, and absolutely no sense of self-preservation is getting caught with a woman who is decidedly not his wife. And thanks to the internet, we all get front-row seats to the fallout.

If you’ve logged onto any form of social media, you were probably inundated with headlines about Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's infidelity. And while the details of his three—yes, three—affairs seem unique (one being with a literal spy), there’s an odd sense of deja vu in there. Remember Ethan Klein? Or the Coldplay kiss-cam scandal? I know we haven’t forgotten about Jay-Z. While I don’t know any of these people personally, it’s hard not to feel affected by these headlines. I feel as though every time I look at the news or scroll through TikTok, I find myself wondering if my own partner has a secret second phone.

As entertaining (and occasionally horrifying) as these scandals are, they've helped create a rather bleak narrative about modern relationships: Everybody cheats, men in particular, and trusting someone is basically asking to get your heart broken.

“When a cheating scandal goes viral, millions of people watch it very often for the shock and may presume everyone is doing it,” Seema Sharma, LMFT tells Cosmopolitan. “The numbers, however, don't back that fear up.”

So why does it feel like everyone is cheating?

Cheating isn't nearly as universal as the internet would have you believe.

According to a 2025 analysis of General Social Survey data by the Institute for Family Studies, 17 percent of ever-married Americans ages 25 to 54 reported having had sex outside their marriage in the 1990s, compared with 13 percent in 2021 and 2022.

Of course, there are things to consider here: Infidelity isn’t just limited to marriage, and of course, this all depends on what people are willing to share. Because the survey focuses on sexual infidelity within marriage, it doesn't capture every situationship betrayal, secret DM, or emotional affair happening in every kind of relationship.

A 2025 YouGov survey found that roughly one-quarter of American adults admitted to having engaged in sexual activities with someone other than their main romantic partner without that partner's consent.

But just because cheating happens—which we were well aware of already—doesn’t mean it's practically guaranteed when you get into a relationship.

“What I often see is the cost of the fear itself,” Sharma says. “People go into dating braced for betrayal. They check phones, they hold back, and sometimes they leave first so they can't be left.”

And this collective obsession with catching cheaters might be making it harder to actually enjoy the relationships we're in.

I don’t like to point fingers... but it’s hard not to when it comes to cheating. This is because men have traditionally reported higher rates of extramarital sex than women. But the gap is getting smaller.

According to the Institute for Family Studies analysis, approximately 21 percent of men who were ever married (ages 25 to 54) reported having cheated in the 1990s, compared with just 11 percent in 2021 and 2022. Meanwhile, women's reported infidelity rates remained relatively stable, hovering around 14 percent in the most recent period. Again, this only paints the picture for married couples.

While men and women may sometimes describe their reasons for cheating differently, the motivations aren't necessarily as gendered as we'd like to believe.

"Underneath, I hear the same things from both,” Sharma says. “They wanted to feel wanted, or seen, or authentic in their relationship, but didn't.”

Speaking as someone who has been cheated on, I know firsthand how easy it is to start wondering what you did to push your partner to betray you. But it’s important to remember that cheating isn’t always a symptom of a miserable relationship. Research shows that 56 percent of men and 34 percent of women who had affairs described their marriages as happy.

"Often an affair is more about the person and not the relationship,” Sharma explains. “Someone loves their partner and still goes looking for a version of themselves they feel they lost.”

Of course, understanding someone's motivation for cheating isn't the same thing as excusing it. Feeling unfulfilled, insecure, or disconnected might explain a person's behavior, but it doesn't make betraying their partner inevitable.

Given that Gen Z has practically turned relationship surveillance into a competitive sport—checking Instagram follows, analyzing likes, investigating suspicious Venmo transactions—you might assume younger people are particularly prone to infidelity.

But just 12 percent of Americans ages 18 to 29 reported ever engaging in sexual activities with someone else without their partner's consent, compared with 29 percent of adults ages 45 to 64. Of course, older people have had more years and relationships in which to potentially cheat, so that doesn't necessarily mean Gen Z is inherently more faithful. But it does seem that younger generations are changing how they think about infidelity.

"For older generations, cheating mostly meant sex. Younger people are dealing with DMs, likes, dating apps that never got deleted, and exes who are one click away," Sharma says.

While younger people might have more opportunities to engage in what we've collectively decided to call “microcheating,” they're also navigating a dating landscape where the boundaries of monogamy aren't always so obvious.

Is liking your ex's thirst trap cheating? What about flirting with someone in your DMs? Or maintaining a suspiciously close friendship with someone you once hooked up with? The answer, increasingly, depends on whom you ask.

A 2024 analysis published by the Institute for Family Studies, found that 76 percent of Americans considered a secret, in-person emotional relationship cheating, while 72 percent said the same about a secret online emotional relationship.

The upside, according to Sharma, is that younger couples may be more willing to discuss these boundaries rather than assuming they're on the same page.

"Younger couples are much more willing to sit down and define what counts," she says—which to me personally, feels like a step in the right direction despite all of the bad press relationships have been getting.

In a perfect world, our partner would never betray us. In a semi-perfect world, we would at least easily detach or leave the second that they do. But as we all know, we live in neither.

Sure, statistically speaking, infidelity isn't exactly great for a relationship's longevity. The 2025 Institute for Family Studies analysis found that 52 percent of ever-married adults ages 25 to 54 who reported having cheated were currently divorced or separated, compared with 15 percent of those who hadn't cheated. (Of course, this data doesn’t establish that infidelity directly caused those divorces.)

But for many couples, cheating doesn't always mean the end. A 2014 study published in Couple and Family Psychology: Research and Practice followed couples who had undergone relationship therapy. Five years later, 43 percent of couples whose infidelity had been revealed were divorced, compared with 80 percent of couples whose infidelity remained secret.

Those numbers sound dramatic, but the study included only 19 couples who experienced infidelity, so we can't assume the same percentages apply to all relationships. Still, the researchers found that couples who remained married after infidelity reported levels of relationship satisfaction and stability comparable to couples who hadn't experienced an affair.

Now, this isn’t me condoning you staying with a cheater. But we each have to determine for ourselves what we’re willing to work through and forgive.

Sharma says that three things matter most for couples who are able to move past infidelity: "The affair has to be over. The person who cheated has to be willing to answer hard questions for a long time without getting defensive. And both people have to want to understand how they got there."

That doesn't mean rebuilding trust is easy—or that anyone is obligated to forgive a partner who has betrayed them.

"Recovery is slow. I tell couples to expect a couple of years, not a couple of months," Sharma adds.

And while it's comforting to know that some relationships can recover, it's equally important to recognize that leaving after infidelity is a completely valid choice. Nobody owes someone a second chance simply because reconciliation is possible.

Short answer? No. Longer answer? Unfortunately, there's no magical way to guarantee that someone will never betray your trust. But that doesn't mean you should spend your entire relationship preparing for the worst.

For starters, Sharma cautions against becoming obsessed with identifying potential cheaters. Sure, sudden secrecy, emotional withdrawal, or defensiveness might warrant a conversation, but none of those behaviors automatically means your partner is being unfaithful.

"The signs that hold up are changes, not single behaviors,” she explains. “Any of those can also mean stress, depression, or trouble at work.”

And if you've reached the point of regularly checking your partner's phone, tracking their location, or conducting a full FBI investigation every time they follow someone attractive on Instagram, the relationship might already have a trust problem worth addressing.

“If you find yourself playing detective, that tells you something about the relationship no matter what you find,” Sharma says. “A direct conversation will get you further than a search through their messages.”

Ultimately, the research paints a much less apocalyptic picture of modern relationships than the internet does. Cheating is real, it can be devastating, and it's common enough that we shouldn't dismiss people's concerns. But it isn't inevitable, despite how much our algorithm may have us believe otherwise. While the headlines might make it seem like monogamy is on its deathbed, there's little evidence that we're suddenly living through an unprecedented cheating epidemic.

“Faithfulness is less about finding the one perfect person who would never do it and more about what two people build,” Sharma says.

This means communicating about boundaries, addressing resentment before it festers, and choosing partners who demonstrate honesty and accountability.

“If you walk into every relationship expecting betrayal, you'll protect yourself so well that no one can get close,” Sharma says. “That's a lonely way to live, and it can be worked on.”

So no, we're not all doomed to get cheated on. We might, however, be doomed to hear about every celebrity affair, influencer breakup, and suspicious Instagram like for the rest of eternity. And frankly, that's exhausting enough.

#6843. This Unexpected French Antique Is Suddenly Making a Comeback, and You’ve Probably Seen It Before

The desire to preserve mementos from a wedding day transcends centuries and cultures. Today's brides still hang onto their wedding dresses, dry their bouquets, and spend a large portion of their budgets on photography. But in 19th- and 20th-century France, the art of preserving wedding-day memories took the form of globes de mariée, also known as marriage globes.

These ornate, shrine-like displays primarily showcased the bride's head crown and other keepsakes from the big day, and the additional pieces inside symbolized the couple's dreams for their life together. The cloche protected these special items from dust back then and still today.

Here's a closer look at the history and allure of globes de mariée, plus tips for sourcing and styling these gorgeous antiques.

Chris Featherston is the senior vice president of product acquisition at Austin Auction Gallery, and he and his family have been dealing in French antiques for more than 30 years. In the last couple of years alone, they've sold more than 150 globes de Mariée for an average of $400 to $600 each.

But this definitely wasn't always the case. Featherstone notes that the demand for these antiques has risen significantly in the past five years alone.

"In the early days of our business, most of what we sold came from house clearances in France," he recalls. "These globes were ubiquitous. At that time, we felt lucky to be able to sell them for $30 to $40 each."

Anyone with a strong appreciation for historic relics can see the appeal of these antiques. For instance, Mick and Paige Whitcomb have been collecting globes de mariée for nearly a decade, and they currently own 15. Their interest in these types of treasures is reflected in their careers. Mick creates sculptural lighting from architectural and industrial salvage dating back to the 1800s through his business, Artifacts by Nomad. Paige is a tintype photographer who captures staged scenes featuring antiques from the 1800s (including her globes de mariée) using a 19th-century large-format studio camera.

"While traveling in France for a wedding anniversary, we were first exposed to globes de mariée at the Marché aux Puces antique market in Paris," Mick says. "While we were initially caught by the beauty of the objects, after hearing their history and connection to French marriages, it felt like a beautiful way of commemorating our anniversary and trip to Europe."

Due to the highly custom nature of globes de marriée, each one is a unique find. However, Featherston notes that most contain a few similar components. For starters, the bride's headpiece often rests on a red cushion, and it typically shows off delicate beeswax orange blossoms that symbolize purity and virginity.

Most also contain small mirrors, which carried different meanings depending on the shape. For example, diamond-shaped mirrors symbolize how many children the couple wished to have, and rectangular mirrors stood for the length of time the couple knew each other before their engagement.

You'll also often find a wreath embellished with brass ornaments, usually flowers, birds and tree leaves. These symbolize love, a long-lasting marriage, and happiness.

"The globes de marriées integrate beautiful symbolism in every piece, and I find them fascinating," Paige says. "Each globe is so distinctive, and tells the story of the couple that created it. You can surmise the couple's hopes and dreams by minutiae tucked away behind the dome for safekeeping. I find very intimate details incorporated into the displays, such as a prayer card of a favorite saint, to photos of the bride and groom. I have even found hair and teeth, transforming the antique into a memento mori as well."

Mick has noticed that sourcing these antiques is considerably more difficult in the United States than in Europe. But with a little patience and determination, it is possible.

"Because of the glass domes, I think fewer importers are brave enough to bring them over," he says. "Despite this, we have found the occasional gem in the wild." In the States, he's had the most luck sourcing them from design-forward antique dealers, online auction sites and even prop houses that have just wrapped up a period film project.

Since there is a constant demand for these pieces, Featherston tries to feature several in every sale at Austin Auction Gallery.

Globes de mariée can add a formal and traditional flair to any space, and they even mix well with more modern accents.

"One of my favorite ways to showcase the globes de mariée is to mix them with a piece of contemporary art," Paige says. "I love to create a harmony between our collections of antiques and artwork from artists that are living and working today."

For example, Mick and Paige have styled two of their favorite globes de mariée with the piece "The Gardener" by working artist L.J. Eidolon. "I find collecting modern works that have a historic feeling to them helps create balance between the multiple centuries displayed together in one scene," Paige says.

A tartan runner on the dining table, a cashmere throw draped over your favorite reading chair, or a few leather accents on the coffee table can bring just the right amount of countryside character to an otherwise modern space. If there's one pattern that deserves a starring role in a Scottish country-inspired home, it's tartan. And you don't have to limit it to a throw blanket or a few accent pillows. Noel Gatts, a designer and HGTV personality, recommends introducing “some slouchy woven baskets” throughout the home, whether at the entryway for shoes and umbrellas or in a living room or bedroom to hold extra blankets. Try a larger basket beside the front door, a smaller one tucked under a console or an oversized version next to your favorite reading chair. You can practically picture a pot of tea warming by the fire in a Scottish country house, and Gatts recommends leaning into that feeling with a thoughtful arrangement of crockery and candles.

Wheeler also suggests incorporating antiques on a mantel, coffee table, or built-in, noting that “antiques can help bring the outside in and tell a story.” The mix of aged finishes and meaningful finds adds a sense of history and character that makes a Scottish country-inspired space feel genuinely lived in.

Because they renovated the space before moving in, she had to guess at everything from where they’d gather to how the house would work for their two young kids. “Ultimately, it was an exercise in designing for a life we hadn’t quite experienced yet and trusting our instincts about how we wanted to live.”

The nine-month project, which also launched her firm, Studio Proper Design, restored the home’s Tudor character. Hoffman and her partners at Alter Design + Build gutted the primary and powder bathrooms, overhauled the kitchen, and made smaller updates almost everywhere else. Along the way, plenty of the home’s charming original details got a second life: A pair of French doors found in the basement now anchors a custom pantry, and diamond-pattern mullions in the primary bath windows make the room look as though it had always been part of the house.

Even the house’s lore fits the mood. Neighbors insist it’s the former home of ‘60s country-rock group Nitty Gritty Dirt Band—though Hoffman admits she’s never been able to confirm it. “Whether it’s true or not, it’s fun to imagine that these walls might have witnessed a little rock-and-roll history.”

#6800. 7 Window Treatment Fixes Designers Swear By

Smart solutions for windows that are anything but standard.

You’d be hard-pressed to find a homeowner who complains about having too many windows, but not all windows are created equal. Maybe you’re saddled with a skinny one tucked in the corner of a bedroom, or an unusually shaped one in a bathroom. The right treatment can make even the most awkward window feel intentional—but the trick is knowing which solution works best for each situation.

Here, interior designers Paige Kelly and Lindsey Walsh share the window treatment fixes they turn to again and again, along with a few clever ideas you may not have considered.

If a room is short on vertical space, Kelly recommends mounting your curtain rod a few inches from the top of the wall, rather than right above the window. “Getting the drapery high and wide is going to make a room feel bigger,” she says.

Tall panels draw the eye up, but make sure they are long enough to skim the floor. “Otherwise, they look like high-water pants, and it just looks wrong,” warns Walsh.

TWOPAGES Liz Linen Pleated Drapes are a full-length option with a tailored pleat that gives the finished look a little extra polish.

Walsh points to “pattern drenching” as a way to make a space feel more cohesive and help soften the edges of an oddly placed window or render an awkward proportion less noticeable. When you carry the same pattern across the walls and window treatments, the window becomes part of a larger visual composition rather than a feature that stands out for the wrong reasons.

Walsh notes that TWOPAGES can work with paper-backed fabric to allow the same material to cover both the walls and windows. A wallpaper installer can apply the fabric to the walls, and a window treatment workroom will install it as drapery or shades, carefully aligning the pattern repeat so the two applications meet seamlessly.

Increasing the fullness of drapery panels can trick the eye so that windows appear more substantial. “You can make yours look wider by using the stack back required for a wider window,” says Walsh. “The panel fabric may just be covering a lot of the wall, but it will look like there’s a window there.”

If the window placement is part of the problem, Kelly suggests extending the curtain rod beyond the window and using a one-way draw. “Gathering a drapery stack to one side feels intentional, and can help balance an off-center window,” she says.

In rooms where you don’t need full blackout, like kitchens and bathrooms, cafe curtains—which hang in the middle of a window along the check rail—are a great solution. “They cover your neighbor’s dirty yard but you still get to see the sky,” says Kelly. TWOPAGES also sells a variety of ready-to-hang cafe curtains, along with the necessary hardware in a gold or black finish.

Long panels aren’t always practical, particularly when a window sits above a radiator, cabinetry, or a piece of furniture. In those situations, Walsh often turns to roman shades. They’re a streamlined way to introduce fabric and softness without taking up wall space.

For a more decorative look, Walsh loves adding a lambrequin, a fixed fabric-covered frame around the window, with a functional shade tucked beneath it. “It’s a great fix when the interior architecture gets in the way of having the kind of window treatment you want,” she says.

TWOPAGES carries a wide range of shade materials—from linen blends and embroidered cottons to bamboo and woven wood—and offers every fold and lift type possible, so you can find the exact form and function to best fit your space.

Walsh likes to use drapery beyond traditional windows, especially if a room feels stark or echoey because of finishes such as marble, glass, or tile. A portière—a curtain hung in a doorway or cased opening—can create a sense of separation between spaces, soften an entryway, or help buffer cold air near an exterior door.

“Even if they’re purely decorative, drapery and window treatments absorb sound and provide texture and visual interest,” adds Kelly.

The right fabric reinforces that effect. A lighter fabric like TWOPAGES Patti Belgian Flax Linen Curtain adds softness without feeling too heavy, while a drape with a more substantial texture, such as the brand’s Birkin Velvet Curtain, adds visual warmth and a richer feel, perfect for the colder months.

The smallest details can make the biggest difference, even if custom treatments are not in your budget. Instead of a traditional curtain rod with finials on each end, which can look dated, Walsh advises using a French return rod, which is mounted on the wall and curves outward. This allows the end of the panels to sit flush against the wall while still creating space for a stack back.

“A French return rod is by far the most elegant way to fake it and make your drapes look professional,” says Walsh. But stay away from grommets—“they are a dead giveaway you did it yourself,” she says—and use rings with clips instead.

Yesterday's reveal showed no watch on the newly anointed 007's wrist, and Pattinson's own taste in watches is rather different from the rugged, sporty timepieces traditionally associated with Bond.

In fact, he doesn't seem particularly keen on big watches at all.

"Whenever a watch gets too thick and it keeps your cuff up, I don't really understand the appeal," he told one interviewer as recently September. "A lot of guys really like that, but I don't think I can really pull that off. I need to only have a half reveal."

"I definitely like really thin watches. That's the main thing. In general, I would probably prefer a leather strap."

Which is quite a contrast with the substantial divers we've become accustomed to seeing on Bond's wrist.

It's a little social-media tit-for-tat, although there's no reason the two relationships can't coexist. For now, at least, there's a straightforward distinction: Pattinson can wear whatever he likes. Bond has his own watch.

#6750. Do Monsters Lurk Around Us in Plain Sight? Gentle Monster’s Director Worries They Do

Bazaar spoke with Austrian director Marie Kreutzer about her Cannes favorite starring Léa Seydoux and Laurence Rupp

A newspaper clipping about a ring of pedophiles captivated the attention of Austrian filmmaker Marie Kreutzer one morning six years ago. The article detailed the buildup and the arrest in explicit detail, but one description felt more haunting than the rest.

“It was shocking because most of the guys in that ring of criminals were normal family dads,” Kreutzer tells Bazaar on a recent call. “It made me so much more aware of how close these perpetrators are, more than we want to think, and that I must almost certainly also know one.… When I read that article, and it described how the police officers were standing at the doorsteps of these guys, my first thought was, ‘How would that be for me if it were my husband?’ ”

Kreutzer decided to channel this shock, the all-consuming fear that we cannot trust those we love the most, into her latest film, Gentle Monster. Releasing on Netflix on November 18, the film tells the story of Lucy Weiss (Léa Seydoux), an experimental pianist who leaves her life behind to raise her young son with her doe-eyed husband, Philip (Laurence Rupp). The trio exists happily in the German countryside, complete with all the comforts of a cozy domestic life. Lucy and Philip love each other and their son, enjoying a sex-filled marriage that appears to have only the regular bumps. This illusion is disrupted when police knock on the door, arresting Philip for his alleged participation in a child pornography ring. Kruetzer designed the couple introduction to feel as if the rug were pulled out from under the viewer.

“It was obviously very important to have all these scenes where they interact before everything happens and where we see that they have a very natural, loving relationship,” she says. “For me, [Rupp] has nothing secretive or mysterious about him. He’s very there. And that’s what I wanted for the role, because I thought it would be good to see him and never really be open to believing that that guy would really do something like that.” She remembered telling Rupp, “It’s not like you’re playing a petty criminal. You’re playing a liar.” Kruetzer wanted him to “make us want to come back to you.”

In this way, Rupp embodies an uncanny confidence throughout the entire movie. He, of course, carries a sense of guilt, yet an audience can’t easily parse out the weight of his internal feelings. Kruetzer also refrains from giving too much direction about his innocence or guilt. Even when he is released from prison the first time, his nonchalance diffuses any skepticism; you might believe him when he says he has nothing to do with it. And when evidence mounts, including actual files, he twists the story again, telling Lucy he did it for the money. Rupp’s sympathetic portrayal is confusing—enough so that you can understand Lucy’s turmoil. Kruetzer remembers telling him to “play it as if you just messed up the taxes,” and later he told her, “I didn’t really play the monster; I played the gentle.”

This portrayal adds weight to Seydoux’s indelible performance as Lucy. The entire film orbits her reaction to this horrific revelation. Seydoux’s anger and forgiveness oscillate at a frenetic pace throughout the movie, pulling the viewer into Lucy’s point of view, as well as what Kreutzer first felt when she read the newspaper clipping.

Casting Seydoux, the director says, felt intuitive. The French actress dove headfirst into the script, expressing great interest in the character and the story. “The subject matter is tough, but the fact that it’s through the woman’s perspective—and not through the victim or perpetrator—it’s through her emotions that the story unfolds [is what interested me],” Seydoux says in Bazaar’s profile from last week. One bone-rattling scene in particular, where Lucy faces off with Philip in a light rain and directly confronts him about his arrest, underscores why she’s considered one of the best living French actresses. “As soon as we turned the camera towards her, it was really surprising what came out of her and what poured out of her,” Kreutzer said. “I remember that the crew was in shock.”

The main storyline provides enough anxiety and suspense, yet Kreutzer wrote a side plot for Gentle Monster to offer perspective. One of the cops who knocks on the Weiss family door, Elsa (Jella Haase), provides a removed point of view on Lucy’s internal dilemma. The character provides a broader understanding of the crime at the center of this story, emphasizing how widespread and hidden this problem is. “For Lucy, her life is falling apart because of this, whereas for Elsa, it’s just one of many cases, and it’s not even a bad one,” Kreutzer says. “It was important for me to show the magnitude of the problem and to not let the audience lean back and think, ‘Okay, that’s very dramatic, but it has nothing to do with me.’ ”

#6744. Denis Villeneuve on Finishing ‘Dune,’ Casting James Bond, and Why ‘Reality Is Unbeatable’

The last days of the making of the Dune trilogy take place on an unassuming Hollywood side street in September, in a dark, mostly silent room. “We were about to have a quick look at reel one because they did some adjustments, and it’s without sound,” Denis Villeneuve says, apologetically. “So it’s a way to discover the film that is a bit brutal for you.”

Villeneuve, the director and cowriter of Dune, Dune: Part Two, and Dune: Part Three, is tall, slender, and bearded, with deep-set eyes. He has a disarming effect. Many directors of Villeneuve’s stature—Christopher Nolan or James Cameron—lead their productions with a kind of remote but dazzling competency: They can do everything better than you and are used to the deference that comes with that fact. Villeneuve’s charisma is closer and more mischievous: He speaks quietly and playfully, in Quebecois-accented English, and commands loyalty via the protective instinct he inspires in nearly everyone he meets. His method is emotional; his instructions are conveyed with sincerity and praise. When presented with the results he is seeking, he will let you know with an almost childlike enthusiasm: I deeply love it. Or: Wow.

Today, Villeneuve and his cinematographer, Linus Sandgren, are at the postproduction facility Company 3, where they have been the past weeks and months, color-timing—the process of adjusting and balancing a film’s many colors and contrasts—the 150 minutes of Dune: Part Three. For my sake, and because there is still work to be done on it, they have started, once again, at the beginning of the film. “So here we could have fucked up the Warner Brothers logo if we wanted,” Villeneuve says, gesturing at the screen at the studio’s iconic WB, “but we are nice guys.”

Then he leans forward: “No, but seriously, without joking, are they too rich, the colors? A little bit? Or is it the original to your eyes?” Sandgren—a calm Swede in Birkenstocks—and Matt Wallach, the film’s colorist, chime in to agree and to tone down the blue. “Their logo is a little cartoonish,” Villeneuve says, still staring at it. “It’s not their best work, I will say. But that feels better. Thank you, Matt.”

Villeneuve and his wife, Tanya Lapointe, who is a producer and second-unit director on Dune: Part Three, disagree on how long they have been working on Dune: Villeneuve tends to say nine years, and Lapointe says 10. (It’s 10.) Frank Herbert’s Dune books, which are notoriously complex and hard to adapt, have stymied directors as talented as David Lynch and Alejandro Jodorowsky. But Villeneuve seems to have found a way. The first two Dune films—Part One came out in 2021—have grossed over a billion dollars, been nominated for 15 Academy Awards, and helped make stars of Timothée Chalamet (who plays Atreides, the film’s complicated messiah figure and main character) and Zendaya (who plays Chani, a native Fremen warrior and the lover of Atreides) while still capturing the uniquely strange and surreal texture of Herbert’s novels. (Multiple people in the Villeneuve films imbibe what one character describes as “worm piss.”)

Right now Villeneuve is feeling the last decade acutely. “I’m at that stage of the process where all my body hurts,” he says. On Dune: Part Three alone, the production shot for nearly a hundred days, in three countries: Hungary, where the film’s main stages were; Iceland; and the Liwa Desert, in the United Arab Emirates, which doubled for the sand planet of Arrakis. They wrapped last November, and Villeneuve went directly into the film’s edit and postproduction process. “It’s a feeling of some kind of deep melancholia to finally come to the conclusion of this long journey,” he says. “Because it has been nine years of my life dreaming about Arrakis, about sandworms, about the adventures of Paul Atreides. I’m pretty emotional about that.”

In the history of Hollywood, there have been only a handful of directors who have had the ability to work, again and again, at the highest level of the art form while also connecting with audiences at the widest possible scale. Villeneuve is one of them. His signature films—2015’s cartel thriller Sicario, or 2016’s science fiction film Arrival, which was nominated for eight Academy Awards—are knotty and severe and not always easy to follow; they are also beautiful and seem to play well anywhere, whether on an airplane, in an HBO rerun, or at an IMAX theater. Villeneuve’s reward for all his success is his next project: the 26th James Bond film. “It’s a blessing,” Villeneuve says. “Because to leave Arrakis, I need some kind of strong rocket, something that will grab me and pull me away from the orbit of that planet. I’m leaving a childhood dream. I need something so powerful to pull me away from it. And Bond is the one that will do it.”

In the dark screening room, Wallach lets the first minutes of the final Dune film play. We watch soldiers on a dimly lit warship prepare themselves for battle. Sandgren and Villeneuve discuss trying to bring out one of the actor’s features more concretely. “I guess what we’re fighting is we could have lit him more,” Sandgren says, “but also what Denis really wants here is the realism and the sort of incidental light—like he just happens to sit in a shitty light here, and gets what he gets, which adds to the realism, which is nice. But you still want to see him. So it’s a balance.”

“What Linus says, in a very polite way, is that it’s my fault,” Villeneuve says, smiling, while Wallach finds a little more detail in the actor’s face.

“I deeply love it,” Villeneuve pronounces, when he is done. “Thank you. The scene is at the beginning, and becomes claustrophobic. In French, we say they are chair à canon.”

“Cannon food, we call it in Sweden,” Sandgren says.

Villeneuve watches his own film as it plays on. “Wow,” he says. “Fantastic. Fantastic. That’s beautiful. Nice work on the highlights here. Thank you, guys. Wow. Great work. It looks real now. Wow.” His voice is full of emotion as he turns to Sandgren: “It’s beautiful, my dear friend.”

In the Dune movies, you can feel Villeneuve’s desire to go deeper into the desert, to shed all the big, loud machinery of his own films, and to get to the places out near the horizon. This is one of the reasons his trilogy works where others have failed: Despite Villeneuve’s talent for spectacle—his Dune is full of menacing, chromed-out oblong shapes and eerily familiar war machines—his true gift is finding the uncannily quiet spots in the worlds he creates, the lonely places where his characters are tested against themselves.

“That was the main condition when I agreed to make these movies,” Villeneuve says, about the way all three Dune films were shot in the desert, on location. “I said that it was deeply important for me to shoot it in the real environment.” The Liwa Desert was so mind-bendingly hot while shooting Dune: Part Three that the director had to incorporate a midday break into the crew’s daily schedule. Robert Pattinson, who joined the cast for the third film, has talked about the set being so intensely warm that he basically disassociated for most of shooting. But being there is one of the main things Villeneuve is after. “I love reality,” he says. “I love to be in the environment. It’s like a pleasure that I give to myself to be in the crew with a camera in the desert. I feel that there’s all these shots that we did, the realism, that I think you cannot achieve otherwise. And you cannot feel the power of the desert if you’re not there. There are some shots you can cheat sometimes. But in general, reality is unbeatable.”

Villeneuve on the set of Dune: Part Three with Timothée Chalamet.

Villeneuve and Zendaya on location in the Liwa Desert, Abu Dhabi.

Villeneuve, 59, grew up in Gentilly, Quebec, a small town right next to a river. From that place he emerged with “a relationship with horizon and silence,” he says. “I was in contact since my birth with incredible gigantic skies, very dramatic, operatic skies. And colors as well. I grew up in a world that has a black-and-white winter, which has a tremendous, beautiful, precise palette of black and white and blue and grays.”

Villeneuve likes to point out that the two main structures in his town were a church and a power plant. “I grew up melancholic, a child that was dreaming big alone in his corner and fascinated by the engineers that were working in that nuclear power plant,” he says. “I was raised as a Catholic, and I was raised going to the church, and those chants and the passion of religion [are] all that mixed together with incredibly powerful grandmothers. I think that’s the background of Dune right there.”

Villeneuve was a reader, a dreamer. It was only as an adult, when Villeneuve became a documentary filmmaker, that he began to discover a larger world outside his own mind. “I think that there’s something about Paul Atreides in the way he’s introduced at the beginning, the way he is like a kid that has been protected in a small bubble all his life and suddenly finds himself confronted to a new reality where he has to consolidate his identity in another culture,” Villeneuve says. “I think it’s something that I, at the time when I was a kid, I really connected with. And early on when I became a young adult, I traveled a lot, a lot. I went a lot to Asia and was spending a lot of time there doing small documentaries and being in contact with so many perspectives, so many points of view on reality.”

Villeneuve made his first feature, August 32nd on Earth, in Canada in 1998. He would go on to make three more films in French, before making his first English-language film, Enemy, in 2013. “I consolidated my identity as a filmmaker,” Villeneuve says about the time before he came to Hollywood “through documentaries and movies with less commercial pressure where I was free to express myself and find my own voice.” Partially, he learned by failing. “I will say that I don’t see my first feature films as...they had some clumsiness in them. I was a beginner. I learned my craft. I made mistakes and I learned from those mistakes, a lot more than from my successes.”

Villeneuve, as a young filmmaker, was wary of Hollywood, even as he was pointed at it like an arrow. “Hollywood has always had that kind of dichotomy, to be a paradise for a filmmaker and hell at the same time,” he says. “Since the birth of Hollywood there has been a struggle between the art of cinema, the idea that you want to express yourself—and for that you need a lot of money, and somebody will write the check, and you have a responsibility. Since the birth of cinema, it has been like that.”

In 2012, Villeneuve found himself facing a rare opportunity: He had two films greenlit at more or less the same time. One was Prisoners—an in-demand Hollywood script about the abduction of two children and the vigilante efforts of one father to get them back. The other was a coproduction between Spain and Canada called Enemy, about a college professor who becomes obsessed with another man with whom he appears to be physically identical. Both were English-language films, which Villeneuve had not yet done. Both films starred Jake Gyllenhaal. But to Villeneuve, they were very different propositions: Enemy was a deeply personal film about identity and the unknown nature of the self. And Prisoners, which also starred Hugh Jackman and Viola Davis and which would be largely distributed by Warner Bros. Pictures, represented Villeneuve’s first true chance to make a real Hollywood film.

Villeneuve decided to make both. But he made Enemy first. “I did convince the studio, Alcon”—the producer of Prisoners—“to wait a few months for Prisoners because first I wanted to make a first film in English in a smaller environment. And the real reason is that I wanted to make sure that I will shoot something that will be so close to me that if ever I was smashed and destroyed by Hollywood, there will be something. If people say, ‘What’s that? Oh, Denis’s there. He’s flying over there.’ Because I did feel that I was afraid.”

I remember watching Enemy in a small screening room the year it premiered, in 2013 (the movie was distributed in the United States by a then upstart studio called A24), and being increasingly unsettled and haunted by the film. Sometimes, in quiet moments, the final shot of Enemy still comes to me, unbidden. Who is this guy? I wondered then.

Enemy and Prisoners were both stylish, morally ambiguous films that showed the extremes of human behavior without ever venturing into sadism or pure spectacle, and this style of filmmaking—brutal, unresolved, lush, and heartbreaking—would become Villeneuve’s signature as he made progressively bigger, and bigger-budget, films in Hollywood: 2015’s Sicario, 2016’s Arrival, 2017’s Blade Runner 2049. “I always did projects I [felt] ready to do,” Villeneuve says. “I was ready to do Dune after Blade Runner. I would have not been ready to do Dune after Prisoners. I slowly learned my craft one step at a time—my skills with actors and then to work with more and more background action and increase the technical difficulty slowly. I just felt secure. The bulky sport watch looked totally out of place in the restrained, elegant atmosphere of the awards ceremony…and absolutely perfect.

The “Pogue,” as the yellow-dial variant is known, is a wildly cool and beloved watch, and Corenswet smartly took advantage of the way it pops against the black-and-white of a classic tux. Plus, it doesn’t hurt that among Seiko collectors, the Pogue is a cult classic, often ranking towards the top of best-Seiko-ever lists. Not only is it an aesthetic swerve on the red carpet, but it’s the type of relatively affordable oddball celebrities rarely reach for.

What’s so special about the 6139, and why is it called the “Pogue,” you might ask? The 6139 was among the very first batch of automatic chronographs. A group of brands—Seiko; a consortium of Hamilton-Büren, Heuer-Leonaids, Breitling, and Dubois Dépraz; and Zenith-Movado—raced to be the first to debut the mechanical innovation. Who won the race is still a matter of debate, but one thing is for certain: when the 6139 hit the Japanese market in 1969, it featured an integrated design, a column wheel, and a vertical clutch. Meshing hallmarks of classic chronograph construction with the convenience of automatic winding at a relatively affordable price, it was paired with a wide variety of colorful dials and funky cases and produced for roughly a decade.

One of these designs, the ref. 6139-6002, is particularly eye-catching thanks to its bright-yellow dial with a contrasting blue-and-red tachymeter bezel, a 30-minute chronograph register, and a day-date display. American astronaut Col. William R. famously wore the Pogue during the Skylab 4 mission from November 1973 to February 1974. Subsequently, the 6139-6002 took on Pogue’s name, and is now one of the most collectible vintage Seiko references.

The best part? Though there’s unfortunately no magic bullet to make you 6’4” and handsome as Superman, you can snag a pretty good “Pogue” for under $2,000. It may not take you into space, but as Corenswet has proven, it sure livens up your average black-tie outfit.
