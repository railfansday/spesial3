#7031. Watch 9-1-1 for the Volcano Eruptions and Bee-nadoes. Stay for the Heart.

The first responder drama understands grief and the power of found families better than any other series on TV.

But 9-1-1 is also a tough show to stay up to date with—there are over 140 episodes across 10 seasons, not including the spin-offs. Critics call it trashy but, really, it’s absurd excellence. Me and the 9 million average viewers of the most recent season can’t be wrong. 9-1-1 is epic, must watch television. So, after a brief time away, I returned to 9-1-1 because I heard that something crazy occurred, even by 9-1-1’s standards. And no, I’m not talking about the volcano eruption.

Midway through season 8, 9-1-1 killed off a major character. For seven years on TV, Peter Krause played the soul of the fire station, Captain Bobby Nash. Then, in season 8, episode 15, he contracts a deadly fever during a chemical lab outbreak and decides to quarantine himself inside. Before he dies, he hands the sole antidote to his appointed successor, Howard “Chimney” Han (Kenneth Choi). Major character deaths are not new to TV. Game of Thrones famously dispatched characters like the series was pulling petals off daisies while playing, “He loves me, he loves me not.” But after eight seasons spent with Krause’s character basically serving as the paternal guiding hand of the series, it was an emotional blow.

Around the same time, I received news that my grandfather had terminal late-stage cancer. Due to distance—I live in New York City, while he lives in China—I realized that I would be unable to travel to say goodbye at his side. It was devastating, and I didn’t know how I would manage roughly 7,000 miles away from my family. We watch TV to be entertained and be comforted from the stress of our daily lives. We also tend to create parasocial relationships with our favorite TV characters. So, following my grandfather’s diagnosis, mourning the loss of someone so central to your family like the fictional Captain Nash helped me to grieve.

I originally avoided the show the best I could once images leaked of the cast carrying a casket through the streets of Los Angeles. Usually, 9-1-1’s promotion is enough to draw you back in. You’ve most likely seen the viral ads for 9-1-1 containing bee-nadoes, tsunamis on Santa Monica, or giant inflatable runaway pumpkins. But despite the wacky premises, 9-1-1 was something that I was relegated to watching alone—mostly because my boyfriend would groan and exit the room every time I had it on our TV. Plus, when I bring it up amongst friends and family, I’m usually met with raised brows and derision. But I’m tired of pretending that 9-1-1 isn’t amazing TV. It isn’t solely the ridiculous emergencies that keeps me watching: It’s the genuine portrayal of the first responders and fire station’s found family.

Following Captain Nash’s death, the most recent season focused on the aftermath among those he left behind. The tremendous loss affected every character on the show, including the tough but vulnerable lead, police officer Athena Grant (Angela Bassett). As Grant, Bassett faces an array of life-or-death situations with compassion and ferocity every episode. Together with Krause’s Captain Nash, who played her husband, they faced a lot of fires and even one cruise ship invaded by pirates. They got through it by sticking together.

Aside from the leads, 9-1-1 also features the strong and smart Henrietta Wilson (Aisha Hinds). Jennifer Love Hewitt plays Maddie Buckley, a 9-1-1 operator and domestic abuse survivor. Her husband, Han, steps up as the new captain of the firehouse. But the character I find most compelling is Evan “Buck” Buckley (Oliver Stark), the impulsive, adrenaline chasing firefighter.

Buck is the beating heart of the show, starting out as a brash and immature member of the firehouse before growing into a more level-headed and responsible emergency responder. His close friendship with the single-father firefighter, Edmundo “Eddie” Diaz (Ryan Guzman), is constantly shipped by fans of the show in a fervor that would make Spock and Kirk stans blush. (The passion from fans rooting for the two to get together has only increased since Buck came out as bisexual in season seven.) But the show wants you to root for them, often spotlighting how the two characters would literally die in a blaze of glory just to save the other.

Throughout my years watching 9-1-1, these characters became something of my found family as well. Seeing every character struggle with the loss of Captain Nash in their own ways—like reconnecting with religion, numbing themselves with substance abuse, or facing their fears—helped me accept that my grief might manifest differently than I expected. It was cathartic to see all the ways that the pain would be survivable with the help of my loved ones.

The tenth season of 9-1-1, which premiered on ABC on October 15, teases an erupting volcano in the middle of Los Angeles. That’s just good TV, folks. But as the season carries on, the show spreads a message of optimism for humanity—proving every episode that moving with compassion and relying on community helps us build resiliency no matter what absurd tragedies we may face. I don’t care if the volcano takes Los Angeles back to the prehistoric era, I know that firehouse 118 will get through it with the power of their friendship and their boundless determination to do good. Amidst our constant news cycle dominated by bad actors, we could all do with a little dose of hope.

#7028. Redesigned Microsoft Copilot Will Build Apps and Work on Its Own

Micorosft has announced that it's redesigning Copilot, the company's all-encompassing AI assistant, so users can hand off complex tasks, build tools by describing what they need, and assign work to an agent that keeps going after they log off.

The new Copilot experience has three main parts: Home, Code, and Autopilot. It also brings Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into the Copilot app. For users, the pitch is simple: Start a task in one place and stay there as it grows from a quick question into a document, an app, or a longer-running project. Most of the new capabilities are still rolling out or in preview.

Home puts Chat and Copilot Cowork together. Chat remains the place for quick answers, research, and drafts. Cowork handles work a user wants to delegate, such as preparing a briefing or pulling together a response to a request for proposals. Microsoft said Copilot eventually will route requests to Chat, Cowork, or Code on its own.

Office in Copilot will allow users to create a Word document, Excel workbook, or PowerPoint deck without leaving that experience. The files remain editable in the regular Office apps, and colleagues can work on them at the same time. Someone building a presentation, for example, could draw on a workbook, draft the slides, and keep refining both files from within Copilot.

Code is aimed at people who need a tool but might never open a development environment. A user can describe a tracker, dashboard, app, or workflow in plain language and have Copilot build it. Microsoft calls these "small, purpose-built solutions." The feature uses the same underlying technology as GitHub Copilot and runs in a sandboxed environment. A separate Copilot Managed Runtime, now in preview, lets organizations host and share the resulting apps within Microsoft 365.

Then there is Autopilot, previously known as Scout. It is designed for work that does not end with a single response: following a discussion, checking in with stakeholders, or keeping a project moving over several days. "Give it a name, a role, and a goal," Microsoft said. Autopilot runs in the cloud and has its own identity, memory, and workspace in the customer's tenant. Microsoft said its actions are subject to permissions, auditing, and organizational controls.

Those longer-running tasks also change how customers pay for Copilot. In a separate pricing post, Microsoft said everyday work in Chat and Microsoft 365 apps will remain covered by a per-user subscription. Cowork, Code, and Autopilot will be billed separately through usage-based Copilot Credits. A Copilot user subscription will still be needed to access those features.

For enterprise customers, the features that charge by usage will stay off until an admin sets up a spending policy in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Microsoft said there will be no charges before then. Admins can set budgets for the whole organization or for specific groups, limit how much individual users can spend, and get alerts when costs approach a set amount. Reports will show who is using the features and which tasks are driving costs.

Microsoft is bringing Code and Copilot Managed Runtime under those cost controls alongside Cowork. Copilot Studio agents are expected to follow in October. Before turning the features on for more employees, IT departments will need to decide who can use them, how much they can spend and which tasks are worth the extra cost.

Home and Code will begin rolling out through Microsoft's Frontier early access program in the coming weeks. Code's Frontier rollout starts at the end of September, when Autopilot also expands to private preview.

For more information, visit the Microsoft site.

#7023. AI Leaders Warn of Risks at United Nations Security Council Briefing

The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

#7017. Before & After: How an Artist and Architect Couple Turned the “Worst House” on the Block Into Their Dream Home

In Brooklyn, artist Katherine Keltner and architect Douglas Gauthier transform a crumbling townhouse with equal parts drama and restraint.

Before they stepped foot inside the crumbling brownstone on a landmark block in Brooklyn’s Boerum Hill, artist Katherine Keltner and architect Douglas Gauthier had toured 30 homes and browsed more than 200 online. Neither of them expected to become instantly captivated by the 1869 Italianate townhouse, which was being sold in a stately yet dilapidated condition, having once served as a single-room occupancy (SRO) residence. But it happened nonetheless.

“This was the first one that had the bones that Katherine wanted,” recalls Gauthier, who runs the firm GAuthier Architects. “It follows the classic real estate rule of buying the worst house on the best block.”

The strategy paid off: Once their offer was accepted, the couple embarked on an 18-month-long odyssey that saw them jump through a series of hoops (preservationists; concerned neighbors; skeptical engineers) to completely transform the moldering pile into a creative haven for them to live, work, and raise their daughter.

A true joint-effort, Gauthier oversaw a modest two-story rear expansion and structural alterations, while Keltner took charge of spatial arrangements as well as color, pattern, texture, and artwork—much of it her own.

Once deteriorating, the interiors now have an easy elegance, with plaster walls, arched passageways, and curved corners that give the soaring ceilings an intimate feel. Pink takes an unexpected starring role, appearing throughout the parlor floor and also in the zellige-clad primary bath, but other adventurous hues like mint green and crimson also feature prominently. Capping it off is a plywood-sheathed office for Gauthier’s practice, allowing the family retreat to double as a place of creative inspiration.

Below, Keltner and Gauthier share their biggest lessons (and some “before” pictures) from the painstaking passion project.

When the townhouse was converted to an SRO, it had been chopped up into at least 13 units and two kitchens, and few of the original decorative elements were still intact. A couple had bought it in the 1960s and made it a family home again and added an art gallery space. But by 2021, when Keltner and Gauthier first saw it, the house was in a state of severe neglect, with peeling paint, crumbling floors, ancient appliances—not to mention a 1,500-pound printing press in the studio (that the couple later preserved). Yet, Keltner and Gauthier saw an opportunity, not to recreate the past, but rather forge their own set of rules.

“The house wasn’t burdened with pristine historic details—our moldings were trashed,” says Gauthier. “There were a few things that we kept and celebrated, but to me, it was like a tabula rasa. We can just go in and make it ours.” Katherine agreed: “I feel like sometimes having to preserve things is more expensive than just starting clean,” she says. “And so I thought that was an advantage for us.”

Not having to be too precious with ornamentation allowed the couple to make a bold statement about crown moldings, eschewing tradition and installing curving corners and coved ceilings instead. Nowhere does the effect have more impact than in the parlor, where Gauthier devised a ceiling light in the shape of a giant oval that casts light upward rather than down. “I really like indirect lighting,” he says.

Crafted out of laser-cut plywood, the elliptical fixture is an abstracted homage to the ceiling medallions found in neighboring historic brownstones. “Some of them are incredibly ornate and beautiful, but they don't really have any use—they’re just decoration,” says Gauthier. “Adding ambient lighting to the living space takes that historical reference in pocket.”

Once they were through the process of changing the structure’s zoning from an SRO to a single-family home, Gauthier and Keltner had to contend with its landmarked status, meaning a series of approvals were required for any additions or exterior modifications.

The couple originally planned to construct a three-story extension in the back, but they ended up compromising on two floors. This expansion includes a bump-out for the parlor floor’s kitchen, leading to a rear deck that steps down to the garden. On top, a private terrace connects to the primary bath. “Instead of having that third story, we now have a deck off of our primary suite,” says Keltner. “It’s kind of amazing,” adds Gauthier.

One decision the couple wrestled with mightily was whether to replace the original, 150-year-old pine flooring with ebony-stained white oak. “The floors were part of the reason we liked the house so much,” says Gauthier. But their contractor talked them out of keeping them. “It’s a lot of extra work for them,” says Keltner. “They have to take it up, save it, put something else down, and then put it back. I think we reached a point in time and money where we were like, Okay, fine.”

Another flooring regret was not adding underfoot heating. “I was pushing the mechanical engineers to do radiant heating and radiant cooling,” says Gauthier. “Radiant heat is really lovely—you have warmth under your feet all the time.” But the mechanics behind the system proved complicated and technically redundant, as the home was already configured with a forced-air HVAC system.

“In doing research and other projects, it’s clear that the cost-to-pleasure ratio would have been worth it,” he says now. “We regret not putting the warm floors in.”

While windows might sometimes be an afterthought, Keltner and Gauthier have advice: Go with triple-glazed. “Windows provide heat and cool insulation as well as security,” explains Keltner. “And they can define both the outside and the inside of a space.” The couple went with triple-glazed windows from Austrian-based firm Bewiso that come pre-wired for easy connection to alarm systems. “They’re almost like hurricane windows,” she says.

“You can't break through three panes of glass,” says Gauthier. Due to the building’s landmark status, they had to use historic-style windows on the front facade. “We were able to get these old-fashioned type of windows that when you close them, they feel like you're closing a Mercedes,” says Gauthier. “They completely silence the outdoors.”

The final piece of advice is perhaps the most crucial: “Hire the best contractor you can afford,” says Gauthier.

“The typical advice is to get a few bids and throw out the lowest and the highest,” says Keltner. “People usually take the second lowest.” However, that could be the determining factor between an on-time project and one that drags. “You’ll end up paying the difference both in money and time,” she says. “It’s often hard for people to bite the bullet and go with the slightly higher price, but it really pays off.”

#7013. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

#6993. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6982. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

#6956. What Happens When You Take a Major GOP Donor and a Liberal Environmentalist Fishing? Something Beautiful, Actually

Doug Deason and Luke Metzger have almost nothing in common politically. Get them outdoors, though, and they’ll teach you a thing or two about bipartisanship. And about angling.

There are few Curtis Creeks in this life, so when you find it, keep its secret well…

—Sheridan Anderson, The Curtis Creek Manifesto

Let me tell you about these two men, Doug Deason and Luke Metzger. Two men who seem like they should be yelling at each other in a Buc-ees. Men who achieved something amazing together.

Where to start? Well, let’s see. Deason is a Trump guy. Actually, more salient: Deason is a Ken Paxton guy. And not just one of his guys but one of his big donors. Ken Paxton, yes, Texas’s embattled attorney general and current Republican candidate for United States Senate. Paxton, charitably, isn’t the best candidate the GOP fielded these midterms, but Trump wanted him in the race, and Trump got what he wanted, and now Paxton is locked in the race of his life, at real risk of losing to a candidate whose party hasn’t won a Senate seat in Texas in thirty-eight years. Earlier in the race, none of the GOP donor establishment wanted to support Paxton. But Deason did, out of a mix of genuine friendship and an affinity for some MAGA priorities, especially the ones that want to see the branches of the federal government pruned back.

There’s also that: Deason is a major Republican donor. You’ll see stories that call him a “megadonor.” Unfair, perhaps. He prefers “activist,” and while his father was a billionaire GOP donor (Darwin Deason sold an IT company to Xerox for $6.4 billion in 2010, providing plenty of funds for his political interests), there are people who give far more. Deason says in a two-year cycle he usually gives between $1.5 million and $2 million; Elon Musk has reportedly put more than $150 million behind Paxton in this race, via super PAC. But in Texas’s vast GOP machine, Deason greases many gears.

Then Metzger. Well. I mean. He’s the executive director of Environment Texas, the state chapter of a nationwide environmental nonprofit. Paxton “routinely sues the federal government to try and block environmental initiatives—for example, suing to undo clean-water protections,” Metzger says. Is that enough? No? Environment Texas describes itself with phrases like “guide our country onto a greener, healthier path” and “grassroots support” and “allow nature to thrive” and “infinite economic growth on a finite planet is neither wise nor possible.”

When I met Metzger, he was driving his college-age son’s Chevy Volt, an early plug-in hybrid.

When I met Deason, he was driving a blacked-out Ford F-150 Raptor, a pickup truck that is a sports car.

Deason is on the board of Galaxy, a data center and digital-infrastructure company that is building Helios, a major data center in Dickens County, near Lubbock.

Metzger? Metzger carefully says that “we’re not saying data centers are inherently bad or we shouldn’t have them but want to set the rules of the road first before it’s too late.”

Or how about this one? Deason thinks wind power—Texas is by far the nation’s most productive state—needs a closer look because of all the birds turbines kill. Metzger thinks the carbon pollution forgone, compared with fossil fuels, is far more valuable to avians (and everyone else). A fight about it on Twitter, in fact, is how they met.

Three years ago, Texans voted for a constitutional amendment to create a $1 billion fund, the Centennial Parks Conservation Fund, to buy and develop land into state parks. Before winning over voters, the legislation made it through both chambers of the state’s legislature and was signed by Governor Greg Abbott. Who masterminded the whole thing? Doug Deason and Luke Metzger. After their meet-cute by the windmill, Metzger decided to reach out privately about raising money for parks. He was savvy enough to understand that, whatever else, Deason was an outdoorsman. Deason agreed. Metzger worked the green lobby. Deason deployed his influence. Two years later: $1 billion. Sounds like a fish story.

That’s the way you might think, anyway. In 2026. When the midterm election is a partisan exercise par excellence, pitting a morally challenged good ol’ boy against a lab-created liberal Christian and attracting more than half a billion dollars in donations while attempting to draw the lines between people that seem to arise as a matter of course these days. Doesn’t it seem as if no one wants to be with the people who disagree with them? Political opponent, acquaintance, friend, or even family member?

But against that backdrop, one of Texas’s greatest and most recent bipartisan successes is starting to pay dividends. In May, the state acquired a ranch of more than fifty thousand acres, which will one day be the second-biggest state park in Texas. And in September, Post Oak Ridge State Park, which was acquired before the Centennial Fund but developed with its monies, opened for day use—the first new Centennial Fund park available to the public.

And anyway, to think that way is to ignore one simple fact: All of these reasons for Deason and Metzger to be enemies are about politics. And there’s so much more to life than politics.

Which is what Deason and Metzger were doing the very day I met up with the two of them and Deason’s communications advisor, Thomas Graham. On September 21—forty-three days before the midterm election—we went fishing at Palo Pinto Mountains State Park, an hour and a half west of Dallas. Fly-fishing, in particular. Metzger had only been a couple times, and the closest I’d gotten to it was a funny numbness in my jaw at “I am haunted by waters.” But Deason had been fly-fishing for fifty-four years, so he took it upon himself to teach us.

We’d left Dallas, where Deason is based, at 5:30 a.m. and arrived at the park to find a perfect morning. I’d flown in from New York girded for 100 degree days, and this might still have become one, but the morning was cool. “Cool”—70 degrees. We parked above the park’s boat ramp and the fishing pier and walked down carrying rods and tackle. The pier arced a couple hundred feet between two points on the shore of Tucker Lake. The water was calm, gently bunched by ripples of wind or rent by the oscillation of a water snake. The lights were on and the sky was beginning to grow big by then, the way the sky does in Texas.

Fly-fishing, it goes without saying, is not bait fishing. I didn’t fully understand that until I saw Deason preparing the fly rods. We had two—one his, one Graham’s. The line on the reel, which an inexperienced fisherman like me could confuse for a lime-green version of the standard stuff my dad picked up at Walmart when I was a kid, is in fact deceptively complex. It’s engineered with four parts—backing, the fly line, the leader, and the tippet—so that an angler can land a fly in the precise pocket of water where hungry fish reside without any but the smartest fish able to connect the whole system back to the guy with the creel at his feet.

Fly-fishing, in other words, is a sport of cunning. Jabbing a nightcrawler onto a hook and dropping it into the water is not unlike bombing an electorate with rage-bait political ads—playing on the most primal, knee-jerk evolutionary instincts. Fly-fishing? It’s a sport of persuasion.

Anyway, this is all very fussy to set up. As Deason got to work, he almost immediately began to apologize for the poor quality of his leader, the lack of appropriately sized line, and so on and so forth. He was working at a wooden bench in the middle of the pier, upon which he’d spread his voluminous fishing tackle—line, clippers, forceps, boxes and boxes of flies—and human tackle—jerky from Buc-ees, a Yeti full of waters. He gave me a spinning reel, a bait-fishing rod, to play with while he worked.

I took up a position near Metzger and practiced a cast. Tall and scruffy at fifty years old, he was dressed in flannel, shorts above the knees, crew socks, and sturdy boots. Which is maybe a caricature of a crunchy environmentalist but is also simply what one wears if they’re expecting to get outdoors. Metzger and Deason had been telling me about their initial meeting, so wind power was on my mind.

“Is there offshore wind in the Gulf?” I asked, metronomically casting out and reeling in.

“Not yet,” Metzger said. “Not anytime soon, either,” he added, seeming rueful.

“I mean, I don’t even know if it makes sense there,” I said. “I’m just curious.”

“To me, the debates about offshore wind are interesting,” I said.

“Yeah,” Deason said, hearing our conversation. He couldn’t help himself. “Well—” he started to jump in.

I finished my thought first. It seemed to me that the real, if at times unspoken, barrier to offshore wind around us, in New York, was NIMBYs who didn’t want blemishes on their horizon line. “But there’s huge shipping traffic coming in and out,” I said. “You don’t have some pristine, unblemished view when you look out on the water anyway.”

“Well, supposedly they mess with whales,” Deason said. “I don’t know if that’s true or not,” he admitted—could be alternative facts.

Metzger picked this right up. “I mean, any offshore disturbance can impact whales, but you know, offshore drilling certainly is, and the boats—that’s one of the big concerns, that boats can drive so fast there’ll be collisions.”

Graham had been preparing his rod, and he got Metzger’s attention. “You want to use this rod to try to fly-fish?” he asked.

“Oh, sure.” Metzger said. And just like that, offshore wind was tabled.

“I wish I had this magazine, Luke. I’m going to send you one. It’s called The Curtis Creek Manifesto.” Deason had already mentioned it once before. It was written by a hippie, he’d said, but it had its insights. Granted, fly-fishing usually happens at water level, and we were up on a pier. And usually you’re working with moving water, building a strategy around the way fish face upstream, waiting for sustenance to come floating down. Still, “it’s just logic,” Deason said of the Manifesto. Still useful. “It’s just common-sense little things. So you know: Control the line.”

The three of them, Graham, Metzger, and Deason, fiddled with the fly rod and adjusted the slack cascading off the reel.

Deason got Metzger set, then came to work with me instead. We got a little lost for a minute, looking at the open water on the lake side of the pier, across to the hills of the other side, monochromatic green under a pale-blue sky painted with wisps of white like an old-timey movie backdrop. The temperature was still in the 70s. “God’s country,” a friend from high school, who’d decamped from California to Texas, used to remind me when I visited. Indeed it was.

Deason held the cork handle of the second fly rod, the one I was going to use, in his right hand and pulled line off the reel with his left until his hand was ringed with a nimbus of fly line. Then he started showing me the motion. He’s sixty-four years old, but Deason has the compact, solid build of someone who has never gone long without physical activity. His movements were efficient, even elegant. Arm more or less straight, maybe cocked a little at the elbow. The rod is what bends. Arm comes up, back just behind the head, then after the slightest second of a pause pendulums forward. Maybe a flick of the wrist. Line shoots.

He let me try. I was not elegant. The line came off the rod for me with no carry, the way a paper airplane thrown carelessly dives as soon as the force of the throw dissipates.

I kept trying, and Deason turned to watch Metzger, who was casting repeatedly. He offered a minor correction to his form.

“I heard someone said, like, it’s a martini glass,” Metzger said. “So like here, to here.” His fly rod precisely described the shape contained by the sloping sides of a cocktail glass.

“Oh yeah!” Deason said. “Yeah, yeah! Looks like a martini. That’s good! I’ve never heard that.”

Deason was interested, doubly so—criminal-justice reform is one of his passions, the one where he cut his teeth on bipartisan politicking.

So the guy asked, Metzger was saying, if he had any idea how to get money from the state. “And I’m like, Ask Doug Deason.”

“I think so,” Metzger said. “Yeah, and you know, if we weren’t just spending the interest on the Centennial Fund…”

There was a digression, because Graham knew a guy with a bison herd. And he was a guy Metzger knew too, because between these three men they seemed to know all 30 million people in the state. And so—

Deason cut back in. “What’d you just say?” He was stuck on something. “Are they still trying to say they’re just gonna spend the interest?”

Yeah, Luke said. It was a billion-dollar fund, but they—the lieutenant governor, maybe, or the state budget commission—were only going to spend the interest. Treat it more like a trust than a spend-down.

Deason didn’t care for that. When he and Metzger had initially strategized on the legislation, the environmental movement was pushing for modesty. Maybe a few hundred thousand dollars. Deason wanted to shoot for $1 billion to spend over three years. Then he wanted to go back and get more.

“That’s absolutely absurd,” Deason said. He stared absently out the windshield as oaks and junipers skipped by. “We should have done $10 billion.”

Metzger let that sit for a beat. “I understand you want to stretch the principal out,” he said, “but if you have a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity, you should be able to dip in—”

“That’s the whole point,” Deason said. “Because these beautiful places are going to get developed.” In an op-ed the two men wrote in 2022 arguing for more parkland, they noted that between 1997 and 2017, rapidly growing Texas lost ten football fields of land per hour to development. Thanks for the idea, Beto. “And nobody knows Talarico, so he’s trying to create this reimagining of himself.” (Deason, by his account, met with Talarico two sessions ago as he was talking to Democrats about school choice. Bipartisanship might not win the day, but it could win the long game. After all, Metzger said, that’s what happened after Beto’s near miss.

Post Oak Ridge, about an hour and a half outside Austin, near the small town of Lampasas, wasn’t officially open yet—it would open for day use the week after I visited. “I think it’s around twenty or so, and then hundreds get injured.” Texas is number one for fatalities from animal/vehicle collisions.

Deason was incredulous. And then, almost as if listening in, a spot of color like gunmetal dropped from a perch among the trees beside us, along the shore. “See that kingfisher?!”

Deason took over Metzger’s rod to have a few goes of his own, to demonstrate.

“It’s a simple move,” he said, and in a sudden but controlled motion, he whipped the line up into the air. “A River Runs Through It with Brad Pitt tries to make it look like casting is an art,” Deason said. He brought the rod forward and down and placed the fly near the foot of a spindly tree, just beyond a long, dark shape in the water. “Look at that snake,” he said.

He pulled in some of the slack on the line with his left hand, then, just like that, cast out over the snake again. He pulled in the line. “Oh, you got ’im,” said Graham. No fish, but maybe we could land a serpent. The hook grazed across the snake’s midsection, pulling it briefly into a chevron before slipping free, and the snake sluiced off.

Deason laughed. Maybe it was no coincidence we couldn’t get a bite. Maybe the snake was leveraging our lures. “He’s fishin’,” Deason said.

An hour later, Deason was irritated. He’d wondered aloud a few times whether there was a place in the park to get bait. A ranger came by, and Deason asked. The closest place with bait was a shop in Strawn. We weren’t about to leave and come back. Deason fiddled with his tackle. He put a new fly on the end of the line. “I’d hate for these guys to get skunked,” he said.

He gave me the rod and pointed me to a spot several yards beyond where he’d played with the snake. Right in the pocket under the tree, he said. Fish like that.

His instinct was good, if for no other reason than that the distance from the pier to the target was one even I could manage. I could place my fly pretty much where I wanted to on the calm water. I cast out, then started slowly pulling line in with my left hand, guiding it along the pole with the index and middle fingers of my right, just as I’d seen Deason do.

Metzger was working the other end of the pier. Deason and Graham were still talking to the ranger. Maybe it had broken 80 degrees. I felt like I could be getting a sunburn, a pleasant heat.

I looked down at my fly. A pair of fish had materialized next to it. My breath caught. The fish struck at the lure. I yanked the pole so violently the fly lifted off the water and the nearly weightless line bounced unnaturally, like a half-filled helium balloon that can’t make up its mind whether to float or sink.

I checked to see if anyone had been watching. They hadn’t, so I pulled the line in and cast again, to the same spot.

As soon as the fly was back on the water, the fish returned. I saw one make its move, and this time, with a little more grace—not much, but a little more—I pulled the line.

Instead of using the reel, I pulled the slack in by hand, piling it at my feet on the pier until I could grab the line above the fish and pull it over the railing. “I got one!” I said. It was a bluegill, fantastically small, maybe the size of my palm. I started toward the guys at the other end of the pier, ungainly with the fish on a line in one hand and the fly rod in the other and a pile of slack, which I stumbled on, at my feet.

Metzger and Graham saw me first. “Look at that!” Graham said, coming over. It was a tiny fish, but I had caught it. And now I needed to let it go—catch and release.

“This is the part I’m not so good at yet…” Metzger said.

Graham told me to grab the fish around the body with one hand, then pinch the head of the fly between the thumb and finger of my other hand. “Push down, twist, and pull the hook out,” he said. I tried, but the hook wasn’t coming. I could just see the yellow bead at the top—the head—where the fly connected to the tippet. I kept trying. The fish was uncomfortable, and I could feel Metzger wincing. Graham tried. Nothing. The fish had nearly swallowed the thing whole.

We took it to Deason. His hands failed, so he got a pair of forceps from his tackle. He gripped the head of the fly with the forceps, and then, with great delicacy, pushed the lure deep into the fish’s throat, so that he could turn it and extract it. There was a tenderness in the way he treated the animal. I don’t mean to exaggerate this, but I thought of an uncle of mine, a hunter, and the way he loves the things he kills. He calls himself a conservationist. The lure was free.

Deason tossed the fish unceremoniously off the pier. I saw Metzger wince again.

Metzger caught a fish too, before the heat rose too much. I caught another, a second tiny bluegill. This time, when I held the fish in my hand, I could see the hook protruding from the skin just below its mouth. I grabbed the top, twisted until the point of the hook withdrew, and pulled out the fly. I tossed the fish back to the water. I still couldn’t cast worth a damn, but I felt like I’d learned something.

We stopped in Strawn on the way home, at a famous greasy spoon called Mary’s. Metzger, Graham, and I all ordered the house special, chicken-fried steak. Mary’s offered small, medium, and large. We each chose small, and none of us could finish the meal. Deason unwittingly ordered a two-pound hamburger. He thought he was getting two patties. It was the biggest burger I’d ever seen by a fair margin. He picked at it with a fork and knife while his phone pinged over and over with messages from the Paxton people.

We made the drive back after lunch. It was hardly into the afternoon, but we’d had a full day. We were mostly quiet, except Graham, who was working his phone for a fundraiser the following weekend. “Doug, you’re not going to come on Saturday?” he asked, between tapping out messages.

“No, thank you for the invitation, though,” Deason said, eyes on the road.

“You sure you don’t want to come shooting with Rick Perry?” asked Graham, trying to sweeten the deal.

“Ah,” Deason said, dismissively. “I don’t like shooting clay. I think we should preserve the clay,” he added, for Metzger’s benefit, chuckling at himself.

“You should see what windmills do to clay,” Metzger said, and the chuckles became true, deep belly laughs.

The laughter settled as the truck continued through Fort Worth. Passed a billboard with a massive portrait of the president: TRUMP—“Born in New York, But TEXAN in SPIRIT” A few fishing poles were propped over the center console, and the smallest eyelet on the end of each one tracked imperfections in the road like a phonograph needle. We were all sun-spent. The postmodern skyline of Dallas would soon appear out of the distant haze hanging in heat over the horizon.

“Let me know what you guys are trying to do this session and we’ll find something to work on,” Deason said, quietly. Metzger promised that he would. There was always more to do. Soon after, Deason would start calling legislators about lifting the cap on spending only the Centennial Fund’s interest. Metzger published an op-ed in the Houston Chronicle arguing forcefully against the state’s reported consideration of selling Boca Chica State Park to Elon Musk, whose rocket company launches nearby. Metzger and Deason had worked on it together. “Doug and I were 95 percent agreed on language in the op-ed but couldn’t hammer out some of the final pieces quickly enough” to get Deason’s byline on it, he told me. No word yet on the wildlife overpasses. No word yet on the midterms.

When I got home to New York, I ordered a copy of The Curtis Creek Manifesto. It turned out to be a hand-drawn comic in the irreverent style of comix of the sixties and seventies. (“The Upstream Crawl,” it said, describing a careful tactic for approaching fish, “Originated by the Marquis de Sade in February of 1764…”) The author was a Utahn named Sheridan Andreas Mulholland Anderson, whose author photo shows a longhair with a black cape and mustache, hand on chin in a parody of self-seriousness. I could see immediately why Deason liked it. It was populist, prickly, but also eminently practical.

I read it in one sitting. On the very last page of the book, Anderson offered a plea. “Most of the waters I fished as a kid have been channeled, piped, or dammed,” he wrote, under the heading “Sanity, Anyone?” “Greedy little minds are ever busy converting our landscape into slag-heaps, housing tracts, canals, freeways and shopping malls, a perversion they zealously pursue under the ragged banner of progress.”

As I flipped back to the beginning, I read over the very first page one more time. It explains the basic task of the sport and describes in large type the characteristics of its two principal actors. The fisherman: “deceive — trick — fake — hoodwink — beguile — ensnare — trap — con — bamboozle — inveigle.” The fish: “vigilant — cagey — shy — careful — cautious — alert — wary — guarded — watchful.”

I thought of Deason delicately releasing the hook from the fish’s mouth and Metzger wincing when it hit the water. The key to angling was to recognize that the superior being was the fish. And maybe that’s why Deason and Metzger are successful. I’m not saying they don’t enjoy the organizing, the activism, the political maneuvering. But even more, I think they love the part today’s politics often forgets, which is the part where you and everybody else around you is just trying to live. These guys. They could be screaming at each other on Twitter or in a Buc-ees or I guess even at the statehouse. Instead, they’re a couple of fish, facing upstream, basking in the holding water, seeing when something good comes floating down.

It helps that Abbott has a baby at home to think of now too. “There are hard parts, but it’s also fun to watch everything, the little growths,” he says. It’s a long way from the twenty-something who felt trapped if he stayed on one job for too long.

But raising a child? There may be emotional centers in your brain that you can’t even access until you become a father. Then it all comes pouring out. As he says, it’s better than any movie. Plus, it’s sure to show up in his work. He figured out Death of a Salesman and East of Eden before his daughter was even born. Imagine what he’s capable of next.

#6940. How One Mistake Unleashed a Horde of Night of the Living Dead Bootlegs

A simple copyright error allowed George A. Romero’s zombie classic to enter the public domain. For years, the filmmakers fought to get the rights back.

would you rather accidentally set your arm on fire with spilled gasoline or spend decades grappling with a paperwork mistake? Gary Streiner, who personally experienced both while making 1968’s Night of the Living Dead, says there’s no contest: “Oh, believe me, I would take the burn again.”

Streiner was the sound man on George A. Romero’s classic indie zombie flick, but since everyone on the makeshift Pittsburgh project did multiple jobs, he also helped create visual effects, like the fireball on a porch chair that would later engulf his limb. His brother, Russ Streiner, was another multitasker, playing the taunting brother Johnny in the movie’s opening, and serving as jack-of-all-trades co-producer. Both were loyal Romero confidantes who helped him found Image Ten, the production company that created the apocalyptic undead tale.

So both were among those bitten in the ass when Night of the Living Dead accidentally ended up in the public domain due to a copyright mistake that was anything but simple to fix. Even today, it hasn’t entirely been resolved, which is why there is a right way and a wrong way to watch the movie (at least, if you care about seeing it in the best possible format, while also not picking the pocket of the people who made it.)

Today, Romero is gone, and his debut feature is considered an all-time American classic. Stephen King himself calls it the scariest movie he’s ever seen. But the independent film garnered such success partly because it was so widely and freely shared. The friends and colleagues who helped Romero make Night of the Living Dead have finally figured out a way to pull the movie back into their legal possession. But the freebie versions are still out there, so they are hoping fans will be honest and honorable about the version of the film they choose to play this Halloween.

Don’t worry, they aren’t “coming to get you, Barbara,” as Russ intones in the iconic opening of the movie. They are just, once again, asking nicely.

Basically, look for the Criterion Collection logo. The beloved cinema preservation group helped the Streiners restore the original black-and-white negative for the Museum of Modern Art, and by refining the moody imagery as Romero originally intended, they technically created a “new” work that Image Ten could officially claim as their own. You can find it on the Criterion streaming service, but also HBO Max is among those that license their version of the movie.

“All of it’s licensed now. Now we get paid for it. That’s the point, every time it’s screened on any of those platforms,” Gary says.

“There are other streaming channels that aren’t,” adds Russ. If you don’t spot that big, silver C when Night of the Living Dead starts playing, you’re probably watching a bootleg.

The average joe might not care much whether these aging producers or Romero’s estate get their share, but keep in mind that these were average joes too, not Hollywood big shots. They were working stiffs who had a dream, made something immortal (in many senses of the word), and then got stiffed for real.

“To be honest with you,” Russ begins. “George—and I want to measure my words pretty carefully …”

“He was not a great businessman,” Gary cuts in, not measuring his words at all.

“None of us were great businessmen,” Russ adds with a sigh.

The Streiner brothers were toiling away on local Western Pennsylvania commercials when they helped Romero bring his feature film dream together, and their collective work inspired generations of other nobodies to max out their credit cards, load up on cameras and lights, and make a crazy movie with their friends. “It’s fair to say that without George A. Romero, I would not have the career I have now,” Shaun of the Dead and Hot Fuzz filmmaker Edgar Wright wrote when Romero died in July 2017. “A lot of people owe George a huge debt of gratitude for the inspiration. I am just one of many.”

It’s easy enough to find the right version of Night of the Living Dead, and it happens to be the one that looks the best too. Many of the bootlegs are copies of copies of copies. When the Streiners got MoMA and Criterion to revive a new version of the movie, they went back to the original negatives, part of which they possessed, and some of which belonged to Romero’s cowriter. “We gathered up all of the elements from Russ’s basement and John Russo’s garage, and we trekked out to their facility, and we were all quite amazed at the good condition that everything was still in, not having been kept in absolute ideal conditions,” Gary says.

Now it looks as good as it did when the very first prints were sent out into the world. That also happened to be the moment everything went wrong.

You know how old movies have a noticeable copyright tag in their opening credits, usually right under the title? Today, that’s an affectation, used by filmmakers like Quentin Tarantino and Rian Johnson to conjure a feeling of nostalgia. But once upon a time in real Hollywood, that was actually a legal requirement to establish ownership of a film.

Romero and his team understood this, and had it on their initial cut of the movie. The mix-up happened when they were negotiating to get the zombie picture into theaters and struck an agreement with the indie distribution company the Walter Reade Organization. “That was the beginning of our nightmare,” Russ says. “The movie at that time was called Night of the Flesh-Eaters. The executives had a point: Night of the Living Dead has a certain ring to it, so Romero and the Streiners agreed. No one realized the mistake until the prints were shipped out, and by that point it was too late. “Not that ownership was ever denied,” Gary says. “There’s never been any, ‘Well, no, you didn’t make Night of the Living Dead.’ We made it.”

Under the United States’ 1909 copyright law, this technical mistake just meant no one was legally obligated to account for tickets or pay them a portion of the sale. “That was the start of it, and we’ve been spending tons of money legally to get this thing on its right feet,” Gary adds.

If that seems both unbelievable and unfair, then welcome to the copyright law revision of the mid-1970s that eliminated this requirement. “It’s totally different now,” says Russ. “As a matter of fact, Night of the Living Dead was one of the reasons that the language for the copyright office changed, because it was preposterous.”

“I’m literally pouring gasoline out of a plastic jug, and the flame shot right up,” Gary says, mimicking the motion.

Spilled gas on his sleeve meant his whole arm was engulfed. “Fortunately, Bill Hinzman, who was a cemetery ghoul, was actually a volunteer fireman, and he had enough common sense to just tackle me and roll me to the ground,” Gary says.

It was over in seconds, with only relatively minor burns that eventually healed. The copyright conflagration took a lot longer to resolve, but now, finally, they don’t feel so scorched by it anymore.

#6921. 7 Gilded Age Homes Where You Can Live Like the Astors

In honor of The Gilded Age’s fourth season, here are the period mansions-turned-hotels you can stay in right now.

Above, Castle Hill Inn in Newport, Rhode Island.

Gilded Age barons of industry constructed extravagant homes—year-round mansions and summer “cottages”—that were more palaces to their wealth than practical residences. Notable families like the Vanderbilts, Carnegies, and Astors once had houses dotted across New York City and throughout fashionable summer retreats like Newport, Rhode Island. Many of these elaborate structures have since fallen to the ravages of time, with the staggering costs of upkeep rendering their use as private residences all but obsolete. Yet, though some of these private homes have disappeared, our curiosity about them has only increased. Since HBO’s hit show The Gilded Age debuted, interest in visiting the era’s architectural marvels has surged, and in Newport, which is home to one of the country’s largest concentrations of Gilded Age mansions, there’s been a steady rise in visitors touring the town’s mansions specifically because of the show.

Visiting the opulent, and often wildly impractical, mansions that inspired the fictional lives of The Gilded Age’s characters has its charms, but rather than just taking a tour, why not spend the night? Up and down the East Coast, a handful of Gilded Age mansions are preserved not as museums, but as hotels—inviting curious travelers to do more than just visit and admire the history, but to sleep within the jewel boxes of their walls.

Castle Hill Inn is a different kind of Gilded Age mansion, one that turns to timber and antiquities over marble and gilding. The home’s original owner, Alexander Agassiz, was a Harvard professor of natural sciences who made his fortune in copper mining. With his newfound wealth, Agassiz built his summer home—Castle Hill—on a relatively isolated parcel of forty acres, a fifteen minute drive away from the pomp and pageantry of the Vanderbilt’s side of town. He used the home as a summer research lab, hosting students in its many rooms and running the lab in what is today the parlor, while also entertaining a who’s who of literary luminaries, including Edith Wharton and Mark Twain.

Today, Castle Hill Inn is one of the most famous places to stay in Newport, not only for its luxurious rooms, afternoon tea, and award-winning restaurant, but also for its sweeping ocean-front lawn and seaside views. Thorton Wilder lived in the turret suite—Agassiz’s former observatory and today the hotel’s most beautiful room—while writing his last book, and Grace Kelly lived in one of the beach cottages during the 1955 filming of “High Society.” Stopping by Castle Hill Inn for a drink and taking a short walk to its still in-use lighthouse remains a summer essential in Newport, but there is nothing quite like staying the night.

Hotel Chanler is, unlike Castle Hill, right in the thick of Newport’s Gilded Age neighborhood, and it’s clear from its location that it was built (to use a period expression) to keep up with the Joneses. The opulent mansion was constructed in 1873 as the summer “cottage” of New York Congressman John Winthrop Chanler and his wife Margaret Astor Ward. Chanler hosted an elaborate range of guests at his summer home, including President Theodore Roosevelt and poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. The home, which Chanler called “Cliff Lawn,” was the very first mansion to be built on the Cliff Walk, now a public walkway with views of these historic homes. Over the years, the mansion went from being a private residence to a museum and later a girl’s school. It wasn’t until 2003 that it opened as the twenty-room Chanler Hotel. Today, it’s ornately decorated in various period-specific styles such as Regency, Louis XVI, Renaissance, and English Tudor. Last spring, The Chanler opened a more pared down restaurant, Remy’s Loose. Remy’s feels fit for Mr. Chanler and his guests, who we can imagine sprawling out across the chenille, fringed edged slipper chairs, and enjoying the just fussy-enough French menu.

The Fifth Avenue Hotel, which opened its doors in late 2023, has a multi-layered history rooted in the Gilded Age. In 1856, legendary hostess and doyenne of New York society, Charlotte Goodridge, commissioned the construction of a lavish private home and carriage house at the coveted corner of Fifth Avenue and 28th Street. Goodridge filled her mansion with treasures from around the globe and entertained lavishly. But following her death, Goodridge’s home met the same fate as much of the neighborhood and was torn down. However, rather than being replaced by a skyscraper like much of the surrounding area, Goodridge’s mansion was reborn in 1907 as a striking five story neo-Renaissance palazzo design by McKim, Mead & White, the architects behind many of the New York’s most famous early 20th century buildings including The Morgan Library and Museum, the Bowery Savings Bank, and the Metropolitan Club. More recently, a decades-long renovation overseen by Flâneur Hospitality, Martin Brudnizki Design Studio, and architect Perkins Eastman transformed the building into a 153-room hotel, with a restored limestone and brick façade, an added-in glass tower, a ballroom fit for a Goodridge party, and a European inspired restaurant, Café Carmellini. The hotel now has a Gilded Age-inspired look, complete with polished marble floors, grand arched windows, rich color palettes, and art and curiosities from around the world.

Hotel Bardo may have opened in 2024, but its history extends much further back. It was built in 1888 as a private home for Lewis Kayton, a businessman from Baltimore who settled in Savannah. Kayton tapped architects Alfred Eichberg and Hyman Witcover to create his brick and terracotta mansion right on the city’s central Forsyth Park. In the nearly 140 years of its existence, the Romanesque Revival building has served as a private home, a funeral home, and an inn before becoming Hotel Bardo. The original Lewis Kayton home now functions as the hotel’s coastal Italian restaurant, Saint Bibiana. Though it remains true to its original bones, the space also feels decidedly modern, thanks to its playful touches and large-scale, panoramic paintings. A rear addition houses 149 guest rooms that feel very contemporary with their rattan accents and hues of pink, nude, and green. A large pool, glass roofed terrace, and a brand-new spa, which feels more like a wellness retreat, further bring the home into the present day.

Built in 1900 as a summer retreat for Margaret Ricketson, the niece of steel industrialist, philanthropist, and Gilded Age titan Andrew Carnegie, Greyfield was a private home for decades before being turned into a hotel in 1964. Today, it’s the only hotel on the eighteen-mile-long Cumberland Island, and remains a highly coveted escape thanks to its 200-acre property filled with live oaks, marshland, and surroundings complete with wild horses and pristine white sand beaches. The Carnegie family still owns the property, making the luxurious hotel still feel a bit like a private home. Family portraits still hang on the walls, and the fourteen guest rooms are filled with antiques and Carnegie family heirlooms including Tiffany lamps, Chippendale furniture, and horsehair chairs. Plus, the Inn’s library is stocked with classic and first editions owned by the Carnegies.

New York City real estate tycoon Robert Goelet looked toward the Hudson Valley town of Chester to build his vast country escape. The result was Glenmere, a hilltop mansion designed by leading Gilded Age architects Carrere and Hastings. Completed in 1911, the home drew heavily on the European style popular in that period, and Glenmere’s architects filled the home with pieces sourced from across France and Italy, including marble columns, fountains, hand carved mantlepieces, and a surplus of furniture. For the grounds, Goelet tapped Beatrix Farrand, largely considered to be America’s first female landscape architect, who created a sunken Italianate Garden that led to the house’s own lake, Glenmere Lake. Murals by artist Alden Twachtman adorned the center courtyard of the home and Italian-style iron balustrades and window grilles were specially designed and created by master blacksmith, Samuel Yellin. Goelet enjoyed his retreat for nearly 25 years, but after his death it went through many chapters, transforming into a hotel and then back into a private home. Glenmere was purchased in 2010 by Alan Stenberg and Daniel DeSimone, and underwent a multi-year renovation to turn it, once again, into a hotel. Palm Beach interior designer Scott Snyder breathed some new life into the property, and although today’s look feels more contemporary than historic, many of the rooms take their names from icons of the past, like the Vanderbilt Suite and the Goelet Suite. With just fifteen rooms and 150 acres, the hotel feels like a true private hideaway. And Glenmere’s sprawling spa makes use of enough marble to make even a Vanderbilt jealous.

Preserved in its Gilded Age style, the Ivy Hotel in Baltimore's historic Mount Vernon neighborhood is an ode to the past. Built in 1889 for John Gilman, a prosperous banker and industrialist, the home was designed by architect Charles Carson, who was also behind other Baltimore landmarks like the Grand Masonic Venue and the Methodist Church. Carson designed a home that spoke to the opulent style of the time, with a staggering 23 fireplaces, elaborately carved wood wainscoting, leaded glass windows, stained glass accents, a turret, skylights, wall murals, and a grand central staircase climbing three stories. Gilman unfortunately passed away before his home was completed, but his widow lived in the home for several years before selling. A series of deep-pocketed owners called the building home until it was donated to the Baltimore Parks and Recreation Department and later used by the mayor in the 1980s to house visiting dignitaries. After a total rehabilitation, the John Gilman house opened as a hotel in 2015. Interior designer Joszi Meskan was tapped to bring the property to life, reimagining it as the home of a well-traveled woman of the world, filled with exquisite treasures and sumptuous attention to detail with nine suites, eight guest rooms, a library, and conservatory. Today, the Ivy is Baltimore’s most luxurious boutique hotel with just the eighteen rooms, and one of the best restaurants in the city: Magdalena.

#6910. The Kitchen Trends Designers Expect to See Everywhere in 2027

The National Kitchen & Bath Association’s 2027 forecast has arrived.

Above: the kitchen of an Athens home designed by Nashmita Rajiah.

Partial walls, hidden appliances, and pantries with room for prep are among the kitchen features expected to gain ground next year, according to the National Kitchen & Bath Association’s 2027 Kitchen Trends Report. Based on a survey of 830 kitchen-industry professionals—including designers, manufacturers, remodelers, and architects—the report looks at the layouts, materials, lighting, and technology they expect to be popular over the next three years. Here are the main takeaways.

NKBA reports growing interest in partial walls, movable partitions, and separate kitchen zones—a shift from last year’s forecast, when open layouts were still gaining ground. Dedicated prep spaces and back kitchens are also emerging features, cited by nearly half of designers.

Kitchens are still expected to get bigger, with 52 percent of designers anticipating growth. The kitchen’s surrounding rooms are growing, too. NKBA reports that mudrooms and laundry spaces are increasingly being integrated into kitchen plans. The vast majority of designers expect attached mudrooms to be popular over the next three years, with 45 percent anticipating mudrooms getting larger and 43 percent expecting laundry spaces to grow.

Appliances are increasingly being incorporated into cabinetry rather than left on display. Eighty-five percent of designers agree cabinets are being designed to hide appliances and other functional elements, and roughly two-thirds say open shelving is on its way out in favor of enclosed storage.

Pantries are taking on more work, too. Nearly 90 percent agree they are becoming multifunctional extensions of the kitchen, and three-quarters expect dedicated small-appliance stations to be popular. Separate beverage areas continue to gain support, with 90 percent anticipating their popularity, up from 85 percent in the 2026 report.

Neutrals remain the leading color of choice, followed by greens and blues. For cabinetry, light and medium tones draw equal interest, and white oak is the preferred wood for roughly half of designers. Islands don’t necessarily have to match: a little more than half expect them to feature a different color from the surrounding cabinets. Quartzite and quartz lead countertop preferences, with nearly half of designers favoring honed or matte finishes.

The lighting forecast includes under-cabinet lights, illuminated cabinet interiors, nighttime path lighting, sconces, and pendants. Eighty-four percent of designers agree kitchens will incorporate multiple layers of light, and 78 percent say lighting is increasingly being used as a design feature rather than just a functional necessity. Under-cabinet lighting leads the forecast at 87 percent, followed by interior-cabinet lighting at 76 percent.

NKBA identifies ventilation, water filtration, touchless faucets, and sound-absorbing materials among the report’s wellness features. Designers are also discussing ways to make kitchens more resilient: 65 percent cite leak-detection systems and automatic shut-off valves, 62 percent cite backup power, and a little over half point to ventilation and air filtration.

#6883. Bob Haircuts Are Everywhere—9 Signs It Is (or Isn’t) Right for You

Time to think long and hard about your hair type, style, and concerns.

If you can count on one thing, it’s that the bob haircut will be a forever trend. It’s evolved past trend status at this point, with a new style emerging every few weeks whenever the latest celebrity cuts theirs (most recently, it was Billie Eilish and Ariana Grande). There’s the clavi cut, the boyfriend bob, the shaggy bob, the elongated bob—the list goes on. But is the haircut actually right for you?

At Cosmo, we refuse to believe anyone “can’t pull off” a bob, or any haircut really. “The bob is a great style because it’s highly adaptable to most face shapes,” says hairstylist Anne Veck. “You can tailor it perfectly by playing with the length or deciding on a dramatic micro fringe or a soft, side sweep.” In other words, there’s a bob for everyone.

But cutting a bob isn’t just getting a new haircut—it’s a full-on lifestyle shift. As a proud bob-haver myself, I ran through all the considerations before chopping my hair to its shortest length ever a few months ago—and ya know what, I have zero regrets, but definitely lots to think about. So… how do you know if you’re actually ready for one? Let’s get into it.

Texture plays a huge part in the type of bob that will suit you. For instance, thinner hair will look great with a blunt cut, while thick hair looks best with a graduated style to take some of the weight out of the hair. But if you have medium-thickness, straight or wavy hair, a bob is a no-brainer, since almost any cut will work with little daily styling for that effortless, cool-girl vibe.

All this to say, bobs often work well on curly hair too, especially if you go to a hairstylist who is well-versed in curly hair. They might have to cut more layers to take off some weight if your hair is very coarse, or they could opt for a dry, blunt cut to achieve more thickness if your curls are fine.

No matter your hair thickness, texture, or density, bobs require a lot more upkeep than longer styles. Bob maintenance is nothing to sniff at when you have to make sure you have a trim in the diary every few months. And if your hair grows quicker than normal, then it might need to be more frequent than that. Can you afford this kind of upkeep? It’s definitely something to factor into your beauty budget at least quarterly if you do have the snip.

Keep this in mind if you have very damaged hair, too. Bobs can require some heat styling, so if your strands are fighting for their life from color or chemical damage already, all that heat styling could cause some unnecessary breakage.

Bobs are a statement style, and if you go from really long to really short, you will get comments and questions about your new look. As someone who thrives on people complimenting my hair, I loved this, but if you don't, you might want to do a gradual cut, rather than a big chop to stop the inevitable attention your new look will bring.

Bobs are an excellent way to do some gentle facial contouring without booking in with an aesthetics clinic. Working with your stylist, you can cut and layer your bob to emphasize the contours of your face. Styles like the bottleneck bob are built to flatter when gently waved and suit everyone.

Something I didn’t think about enough was the constant styling I would be doing after working out, as it’s harder to hide hair that needs a wash when your hair is shorter. Also, you need to think about how long your hair needs to be to be tied back. It sounds small, but dozens of hair ties later, you will be cursing the day you sat in that salon.

If your hair is highly porous (curly-haired gals and bleached peeps, I’m talking to you), it’s not impossible to have a bob; it’ll just take a few more products to keep it looking fresh when it’s sticky and humid outside. I mean, I live in drizzly London and have a bob, so it is doable, but there have been weeks where I have to wear my hair in a low bun for the majority of the days to keep my strands tucked away from the elements.

Low maintenance? We don’t know her here in bob-land. Yes, it’s less laborious to style than, say, bangs, but any bob (no matter your hair texture) will require at least a few minutes each morning to zhuzh, so you have to factor this into your getting-ready time.

Regardless of the type of bob you cut, be warned that there can be some awkward moments in the growth phase. When your bob starts skimming your shoulders, the hair can flip in and out in a way that some might find a bit wonky, tbh. If you’re not committed to having this length for a while, then you might be setting yourself up for a few months with an awkward length.

Your curls are likely to spring up and look much shorter than they’re actually cut. For some, this look is super cute and accentuates your facial features. But if you’re not ready to go extra short, bring this up to your stylist; they might recommend opting first for a lob length.

A night cream is any cream that you use at night before bed to hydrate your skin. The idea is that night creams are thicker and made with rich emollients that work well to seal in hydration overnight versus your standard moisturizer that could be applied day or night. But a night cream can be a cream for nighttime, even if it’s not explicitly called a night cream. FWIW, that’s more of a marketing term than anything.

You absolutely need a moisturizer before bed, even if you have oily skin. But do you need a specific cream that’s called a “night cream”? Not necessarily, so long as your nighttime moisturizer is doing its job of hydrating and locking in moisture while you sleep.

If you have dry skin, the night cream selection process will probably be a breeze for you. Most night creams contain a plethora of ingredients to moisturize your skin. Look for a mix of hydrators and sealants.

The key to moisturizing your skin is two-fold: Hydrate, then lock in moisture. Hydration comes from humectants, aka ingredients that pull water into your skin. They create a barrier over your skin to keep moisture from seeping out. Together, the two create a formula that offers lasting hydration, not just a quit hit of plumpness.

#6855. Inside the Underground Harry Styles Hospitality Network

And you'd think all that inside knowledge would make buying her a gift a cinch, and yet here you are, looking up the best gifts for wives. No judgment—we've been there. Whether it's an anniversary, a birthday, or just an occasion to remind her how much you appreciate her, we've rounded up a few things that have been on our radar all year. And yes, you should probably make that dinner reservation, too.

Still got names on your list? We've got ideas. Peruse the rest of our gift guides, and sign up for the GQ Recommends newsletter for a regular dispatch of things we think are worth your money.

It's easy to get distracted by all the excellent cashmere at Banana, but the brand knows its way around silk, too. This shirt is cut roomy and relaxed, with the sort of effortless drape that makes getting dressed easy.

Coach's Empire Carryall comes in a whole family of sizes, all cut from the brand's soft glovetanned leather. It's a beautiful bag on its own, but I'd suggest picking out a few charms to dangle from the handles to make it hers.

Phoebe Philo's Peak sunglasses are aptly named: the shape is, quite simply, peak. An absolute stunner of a pair, and just imagine her walking into brunch wearing them.

Depending on whom you ask, the Moon Shoe might just be Nike's defining sneaker of the moment. Credit Jacquemus, whose collaboration turned a relic of the brand's running history into one of fashion's most coveted shoes.

J.Crew has barn jackets on the brain this year, and its considerable efforts might just have you lusting after one yourself. I'm partial to the studded version, but there's plenty to choose from, including corduroy and faux shearling renditions.

A silk scarf is an endlessly good gift, if only because there are endless ways to wear one. Around her neck, in her hair, knotted onto a handbag, however she pleases, really.

Brooches are all over the runways right now, and might just be the easiest way to lend an outfit a little extra panache. This dangly little number from Lie Studio features carnelian stone, with a rich, brownish-red hue that feels of the moment.

A coworker Chris first put me onto Simuero, but it wasn't until a trip to Barcelona earlier this year that I got to see the Spanish jewelry label's work in person, at Bassal Store. Beautiful stuff, with an organic, slightly imperfect quality that's even better up close.

This is about as chic as it gets, as far as under-eye patches go. The silky patches promise a little firming power, and the set also includes the Le Lift hand cream as an extra treat.

Makeup, of course, is a tricky thing to gift, but a colleague of mine showed up to the office wearing this purple blush from Violette_FR, and I was immediately sold on the shade. I've had it on my running list of recommendations ever since.

Soft Services has shrunk its beloved exfoliating Buffing Bar down to miniature proportions, with four little bars in a very giftable set. A posh way to keep skin in check, and low-key great for travel.

There was plenty of oohing and aahing when a small sample of Maison Francis Kurkdjian's Oud Velvet Mood landed on my desk this year. A gorgeous oud with notes of peach, suede, and leather.

Dr. Sturm has built quite a following for her science-minded approach to skincare. This travel-friendly set brings together some of her signature formulas, all tucked into a fuzzy fleece bag.

Sidia's Braless Duo pairs a subtly scented mist and serum, a welcome salve for winter-weary skin.

Rocco's game-changing beverage fridge now has a more affordable sibling. The Essential holds more than 100 cans and costs considerably less than the flagship Super Smart Fridge, while retaining the same compressor.

One floral and bittersweet, the other peachy and citrus-forward, these botanical aperitivi are deliciously complex without trying to taste like booze. Serve over ice or topped with soda.

Caraway isn't the first to make a coffee maker with an all-glass-and-steel brewing system, keeping hot coffee away from plastic. But it might just be the best-looking of the bunch, which counts when it's one of the first things they see in the morning.

What, your dinner parties don't involve serving salad with horn-handled, mother-of-pearl spoons? Gohar's nautilus shell-inspired salad servers are spectacularly unnecessary, which is why they make such a great gift.

Hario might be best known for its coffee gear, but the Japanese brand knows its way around matcha, too. This gift set includes a double-walled glass bowl and everything she needs to prepare a prower bowl of matcha.

Just the thing to christen that new matcha set. Kettl sources its teas directly from growers, and I'm currently taken with its Okuju matcha. Developed with a longtime partner in Uji, Japan's storied tea-growing region just outside Kyoto, it's a particularly fine powder with a delicate nuttiness and a wonderfully smooth finish.

Edie Parker's gemstoned lighter case has long been a go-to gift around these parts, but this year we've got our eye on the matching ashtray. No smoking equired—it makes a pretty fab catchall for jewelry, keys, and other little things.

I love the freaky pop-art vibes of this collaboration between Dusen Dusen and Venetian glassmaker Laguna~B. The pitcher is great on its own, but you can also spring for the set with two matching glasses.

Apple's new AirPods 5 come in two tiers, and the more giftable of the two is the version with the wireless charging case. You get longer battery life and swipe-to-adjust volume controls right on the stems.

For the record collector, U-Turn's Orbit Plus is one of the finest midrange turntables around. This version has a built-in phono preamp, so she can get straight to spinning records with a pair of powered speakers.

Taking its cues from Kodak's '70s-era 110 camera, Camp Snap offers a screen-free digital experience with an 8MP sensor, which is modest by today's standards, but entirely the point.

Nothing says great gift quite like an overengineered personal fan she'd never buy herself. Dyson's bladeless design is the clever bit, delivering a steady stream of air that's whisper-quiet.

If she's going to track her health, she might as well do it with the latest Oura Ring. The fifth-gen model is 40% smaller, with more proactive insights and personalized guidance to help her make sense of all that data.

Electric toothbrushes aren't exactly paragons of sustainable design, but Suri is doing its damnedest to change that. Its Pro 2.0 has recyclable plant-based brush heads, a repairable body, and a rechargeable battery designed to keep the whole thing out of a landfill for longer.

How could Theragun possibly improve on its already excellent mini massage gun, you ask? By adding heat, of course. The Mini Plus combines percussive massage with warming therapy to help loosen up sore muscles.

If cupping is good enough for the sporting elite, it's certainly worth a spot in her recovery routine. Wthn's kit includes eight silicone cups for a gentler take on the practice that can still help relieve muscle tension.

Wonder Valley's hinoki-scented bath products have won over a few of GQ's more relaxation-minded staffers. This set pairs a handsome bar of soap with a sachet of bath tea, which can be steeped a few times before it's finally spent.

Red-light technology is coming for sex toys, and Lelo's Mona Spectra is among the first to get in on the action. Expect to see plenty more of it in the years ahead, but there's nothing wrong with being an early adopter in this particular department.
