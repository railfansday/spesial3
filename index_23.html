#7081. IDC Report Finds Significant Gap Between AI Spending and Measurable Financial Returns

Nearly 70% of organizations report a significant gap between artificial intelligence spending and measurable financial returns, according to a recent survey by IDC.

The research firm's 2026 Worldwide AI Economics and Cost of Ownership Survey found that 69% reported such a gap, while only 15% had achieved an AI return on investment exceeding 100%.

In a separate May 2026 survey, 73% of respondents identified excessive AI costs as a significant risk.

IDC highlighted the findings in an October 20 analysis examining what enterprise customers expect from AI technology vendors.

Enterprise customers are placing greater emphasis on measurable business outcomes when evaluating AI products, according to IDC.

The firm identified three priorities for vendors: demonstrating financial value, addressing governance and security requirements, and building support beyond IT departments.

The analysis also highlighted the gap between initial agentic AI deployments and broader adoption. Although 80% of organizations have started deploying agentic AI, only 17% are using it across the enterprise.

IDC said evaluating AI investments can be difficult when the technology changes how employees work and how business processes operate.

The firm expects AI infrastructure capacity to catch up with demand around 2029, potentially easing costs. Until then, vendors face pressure to demonstrate the long-term financial value of their products.

AI funding decisions involve a broad range of executives. IDC's September 2026 C-Suite Tech Survey found that CIOs and CTOs were involved in 63% of those decisions, business-function executives in 62%, and chief financial officers in 61%.

A separate IDC survey found that 42% of CEOs planned to hire a chief AI officer within the next 12 months.

Buyers also reported concerns about the products and terms vendors offer. Limited integration with existing systems, insufficient model and data transparency, and concerns about vendor lock-in were each cited by 34% of respondents.

Overpromising and underdelivering, along with unclear pricing models, were each cited by 31%.

IDC said vendors need to substantiate financial returns and address security and governance requirements while working with decision-makers across IT, finance, and other business departments.

For more information, go to the IDC blog.

#7076. Report: Global Ransomware Attacks Rise 23% in Q3

Global ransomware activity increased 23% in the third quarter of 2026, with organizations in North America and Europe accounting for the largest share of attacks, according to cybersecurity firm NCC Group.

The company's Cyber Threat Intelligence Report Review of Q3 2026 recorded 2,865 ransomware victim claims, up from 2,325 in the previous quarter.

Despite the quarterly increase, attacks declined 26% month over month in September to 821. North America recorded the most attacks that month with 390, followed by Europe with 211.

The industrial sector accounted for 29% of quarterly attacks, while Qilin remained the most active ransomware group for the sixth consecutive quarter, recording 365 victims. The Gentlemen followed with 356, and INC Ransom ranked third with 109.

The report highlighted a July cyber attack against a small UK power generation facility that forced operations to stop for approximately four days.

Although the incident did not affect the national electricity grid, it demonstrated how cyber attacks can disrupt physical infrastructure.

The security researchers said the incident was consistent with techniques previously associated with Iranian-affiliated campaigns, including attacks against internet-exposed industrial controllers.

However, the UK government has not formally attributed the attack, and the techniques used remain unconfirmed.

The report also identified growing security challenges as organizations connect traditional IT networks with operational technology (OT) systems for remote maintenance and centralized management.

The firm recommended network segmentation, stronger privileged-access controls, and continuous monitoring of OT environments.

The research found that more than 40 vulnerabilities affecting enterprise applications, VPN appliances, e-mail gateways, and management platforms were added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog during the quarter.

The report examined Gunra, a ransomware-as-a-service operation that has targeted 53 victims across multiple industries as of Q3.

According to the cybersecurity company, Gunra exploited vulnerabilities CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 in internet-facing Fortinet devices to gain access to corporate networks.

The group also used stolen credentials, session hijacking, and administrative tools to move between systems before deploying ransomware.

Other emerging ransomware groups included Deadlock and Storm, with the latter claiming 78 victims by the end of September.

Researchers also observed initial access brokers advertising compromised VPN, Remote Desktop Protocol, and SSH access, alongside access to cloud identities, source code repositories, and security management platforms.

The quarterly review also highlighted BraZetsu, an AI-enhanced Windows malware framework that evolved into a specialized initial access toolkit.

According to the report, BraZetsu uses AI models to automate victim profiling, analyze compromised environments, and identify valuable corporate systems.

The framework searches for enterprise software, financial data, digital certificates, and cloud credentials, helping operators identify compromised access that can be sold to other cyber criminals.

The cybersecurity firm also identified risks involving legitimate AI agents, including prompt injection, excessive permissions, and unintended access to external systems.

The report cited incidents involving U.S. and Australian government systems as examples of agents accessing resources beyond their intended scope.

Researchers warned that these incidents do not necessarily involve malicious intent but can create consequences resembling conventional cyber intrusions.

#7070. Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

For more information, go to the Microsoft blog.

#7066. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#7061. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#7035. Hailey Bieber Went From Honey Bronde to Lacquer Brunette

Consider this your sign to get a new season hair refresh!

TikTok has a habit of turning the most niche beauty observations into full-fledged cultural phenomenons. One minute we're debating high contrast, low contrast makeup, the next we're all channeling “Tomato Girl Summer” or dabbing broccoli onto our faces in the pursuit of faux freckles (yes, it is I who did this in the name of boots-on-the-ground journalism). The app has become something of a crystal ball for beauty trends—that is, provided you're willing to sacrifice a few hours of your life to the algorithm.

But sometimes, it's worth looking up from your For You Page and remembering that celebrities have been setting beauty agendas long before TikTok got its hands on them. So, in the spirit of good old-fashioned beauty reporting, we took to the red carpet at the 2026 CFDA Fashion Awards (read: scrolled through pictures online from the comfort of our office desks) to see what the A-listers are wearing on their faces, and more importantly, doing with their hair this winter.

And at the front and center of the cultural zeitgeist? Hailey Bieber,, who arrived at the awards with a seasonal hair transformation that, of course, caught our eye. Enter: dark romance girl winter.

Gone are her familiar bronde lengths, and in their place comes a glossy, richer-toned shade of brown that looks almost lacquered. London-based hairstylist Marley Xavier previously dubbed this hair color “lacquer brunette,” predicting it would be a huge trend for the autumn/winter season. And, in our books, it's the unofficial hair hue of what we're calling the “dark romance girl” aesthetic. Think: moody autumn evenings, cashmere sweaters, burgundy nails, and vampy red lips.

Now, with Hailey being the face of said dark romance girl trend, it's likely we'll see an influx of blondes take to the at-home hair dye. But (!!) before you take the plunge, it's worth considering your natural contrast level—AKA, the difference in depth between your skin, hair, and eyes.

If, like Hailey, you have high-contrast coloring (where your features have a noticeable difference in depth), deeper shades like lacquer espresso and chocolate brunette can really complement your complexion. Those with softer, lower-contrast features, meanwhile, might find that more tonal shades, like mocha or mushroom brown, offer a more harmonious finish.

So, while you may want to make like Mrs. Bieber with a dark romance look for winter, it's worth considering the contrast of your features beforehand. Either that or you can go experimenting—after all, beauty is multifaceted; it’s functional while also playful, so do as you please!

The 2027 Costume exhibition and corresponding gala is titled “extraOrdinary: Fashion and the Poetics of the Everyday.” It will explore how designers reimagine quotidien ideas for the high-fashion world and how wearing, styling, and caring for these garments shapes their significance over time.

The exhibition will be divided into two larger themes. The first, “Familiar Forms,” will center archetypal garments like jeans, T-shirts, biker jackets, and trench coats, looking at how designers reinvent these pieces and play with the symbolism surrounding them. The other, “Common Matter,” will focus on disposable materials like paper, aluminum, and plastic, and how they have been elevated to fashion.

“The ordinary is never quite as simple as it appears,” said Andrew Bolton, Curator in Charge of the Costume Institute. “A pair of jeans, a white shirt, or a sweatshirt can hold traces of a life—the habits, memories, and attachments that make something our own. Designers work with that familiarity, revealing unexpected possibilities within forms and materials we think we already know. The exhibition invites us to discover how much imagination the everyday can hold, and how looking again might change what we value and care for.”

Bolton began where he left off last year with “Costume Art,” and the reality of placing the body at the center of your design process. He was particularly interested in moments like Jun Takahashi’s fall 2024 collection for Undercover, titled “Watching a Working Woman,” which was inspired in part by Wim Wenders’s 2023 film Perfect Days. He created ensembles that joined and layered familiar garments in new ways to subvert and complicate traditional perceptions of identity and form. The exhibition will open with pieces from this collection and expand to include over 200 items, including Matthieu Blazy’s trompe l’oeil leather jeans for Bottega Veneta, pieces from Marc Jacobs’s 1990s Perry Ellis grunge collection, Brooke Shields’s Calvin Klein jeans, Junya Watanabe’s leather biker jackets, Charles James’s 1930s couture puffer coat, and more.

The original theme, which would have centered on Galliano’s life and work, was initially announced in late July. After donors and critics spoke out against the designer’s past antisemitic and anti-Asian remarks, it was then recalled in late August. This decision was unprecedented; the Met had never made such a late-stage switch-up.

Galliano was known for his objectively extraordinary work subverting themes of historical excess and grandeur. His shows were more like theater productions. Now, it seems they’ve chosen a balm for the raucous in skewing in the complete opposite direction, promoting real life over fantasy. That said, this new theme is purported to examine familiar ideas through the extraordinary lens of fashion. There is certainly nothing ordinary about a Junya Watanabe biker jacket.

There is certainly much to explore here. The theme reminds us how much inspiration comes from the people walking down the street every day, the uniforms that have shaped our perceptions of society. Those ideas are ripe material for fashion designers’ creative minds —and can cause quite the stir among audiences. Just think of how shocked the industry was after Marc Jacobs elevated the way youth culture were dressing, what we call “grunge,” to the runway with his 1993 collection for Perry Ellis. Or the fascination with those optical illusion leather jeans Matthieu Blazy put on his Bottega Veneta runways a few years ago. After all, fashion is only fantastical relative to what we deem commonplace. We need a bit of the the familiar to help us digest something radical.

#6932. Jesse Jo Stark on What It Means to Be a Muse

The singer’s new gothic country album, MUSE, releases on November 6

Jesse Jo Stark wrote her latest album MUSE in the wake of heartache. She left Los Angeles to find reprieve in the Colorado Rocky Mountains, where she found sanctuary among friends, a bottle of rosé, and a pink halter top and sweatpants combo. The melancholic album flowed out of her for three weeks, during which she transferred her emotions onto the page. “I am no longer holding on to a version of myself,” she tells Bazaar on a call from Los Angeles, on her way to a photoshoot. “There’s a freedom in moving forward.”

Stark keeps pressing on, no matter what. Outside of songwriting, she plays a key role in Chrome Hearts as the daughter of founder Richard Stark. She spent her formative years surrounded by musicians, artists, designers, and every kind of creative person imaginable, all of whom inspired her to pursue her own vision. That world endlessly inspires her to create, even when she feels low. “I’ve worn things that are absolutely horrible, and I’ve written songs that I don’t necessarily love,” she says, “but when you grow, you go back to a less constructed version of yourself.”

This more authentic version of herself fuels MUSE. She shed some of the macabre edge of her debut album 2022’s Doomed in favor of a softer reflection on her life, love, femininity, and the natural world that inspired these songs. But above all, Stark offers a reflection on her life being put on a pedestal, a narrative that deeply considers the women in her life, from friends to her mother, Laurie Lynn Stark. This 13-track gothic country album appropriately conveys Stark’s energy, with visuals described as “somewhat of a horror film, but I’m wearing a lace skirt.” She wrote on her Instagram, announcing the album, that the theme is “fragile but fierce femininity,” best to “enjoy with a glass of red.”

A few weeks before the release, Bazaar spoke to Stark about the women who inspired her, Napoleon’s poems to Josephine, writing songs in Colorado, and what it means to be a muse.

I was looking at a lot of mythology and history and the muses of rock and roll—women of rock and roll. If you break down the word itself, you get “used,” “amused,” “music.” I think women are admired and desired, but not always heard. For me, women have this pivotal point in their life—hopefully, at one point—where they regain their strength and voice, and this album was that for me. The muse, throughout history, could also have a negative connotation and feel lonely. But I felt like putting emphasis on the reciprocity of it—then owning that and allowing it to feel beautiful. Fletcher’s task was to design around storied memories, the family’s obvious love for winter sports, and their passion for entertaining—all while combining the crisp aesthetic of the alpine wilderness with cozy textiles and natural details to create the ultimate mountain sanctuary. Natural grain runs through the room’s doors and vanity, topped with soft white Calacatta marble, channeling the classic ski chalet aesthetic. Gold fixtures extend from the vanity to the lighting, towel rack, and tub, for a touch of glamour.

Boasting the project’s biggest splurge, a custom bunk-trundle bed, the kid’s room combines playful hues with forest-inspired details and maximum sleepover potential. “We wanted to maximize sleeping spots in this home,” Fletcher explains. “The Casa Kids bunk bed in the kid's room was a splurge, but it can sleep 5!”

#6923. Why Is Everyone Bragging About Their Ferritin Levels?

Losing hair, feeling sluggish, or having brain fog? Experts are turning to a blood-work statistic that may be the sneaky cause

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

“Maybe you’re not depressed and unmotivated … maybe you just have low ferritin,” says Elizabeth Orringo, a wellness influencer and host of The Wellness Process podcast, in an Instagram reel. The caption then prompts viewers to comment “Iron” to get a link to her Substack, which details how she raised her ferritin from 16 to 142, making her body “feel like a new person.” The video has 244,000 views and nearly 5,000 “Iron” comments. But what is ferritin, how does it relate to iron, and why do thousands of women online care?

Ferritin and iron aren’t synonymous, but they are closely linked. “Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in your body, and iron is essential for producing hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells responsible for carrying oxygen throughout the body,” says Pooja Gidwani, a Los Angeles–based internist and longevity expert. “Ferritin is one of the best markers we have to assess how much iron your body has stored, rather than just how much is circulating in your blood at a given time.”

Iron has had its moment in the wellness world, but ferritin is just getting started; it stores iron for later use, so the less you have, the less iron supply your body has in times of need, like during increased blood loss, growth stages, and life in general. “I like to think of serum iron as your checking account and ferritin as your savings account,” says Amanda Kahn, a New York–based internist and longevity specialist. “You can have enough money to cover today’s expenses while your savings are becoming depleted.” While iron gets most of the spotlight, Kahn says ferritin often gives a clearer picture of the body’s iron reserves and can more efficiently identify iron deficiency before it progresses to anemia and other problems.

Even without a blood test, people often notice iron deficiency through low energy, hair loss, and fatigue, which can also be side effects of low ferritin. But another marker of low ferritin that has taken social media by storm is “ferritin face.”

“While ‘ferritin face’ isn’t a recognized medical condition, the broader connection between iron status, energy, and overall well-being is worth understanding,” Kahn explains. “Social media has introduced terms like ‘ferritin face,’ bringing attention to the relationship between iron stores, energy, and appearance.”

According to Google Search, searches for “ferritin face” are up 450 percent in the last three months and 5,000 percent year over year. Ferritin face refers to the more visible hallmarks of low ferritin, like pale skin, dark, hollow under-eyes due to lack of color, and puffiness as a result of inflammation. In a beauty landscape where going under the knife is often the first line of defense, “ferritin face” offers another explanation that plastic surgery can’t fix. “When iron is in short supply, the body deprioritizes skin and hair and redirects it to support vital organs, which is why it shows up visibly first,” says Tiffany Lester, an integrative medicine physician and women’s health medical director at Function.

Just because you have dark circles or look pale doesn’t mean you have low ferritin—and vice versa; someone without “ferritin face” can still have low ferritin. The first step is a blood-work panel that covers both iron and ferritin levels, since iron levels can be normal while ferritin can be low. “If your iron levels read normal, but you have low ferritin, which means your body may still have enough circulating iron to meet its immediate needs, but its stored iron is depleted,” says Gidwani.

However, experts point out that laboratories often have different ideal ranges for ferritin levels, and a “healthy” ferritin level can be hard to pinpoint. “Reference ranges reflect a population average, not your optimal baseline,” Lester explains. This means that an adequate level for you may not be normal for someone else. “A lab may tell a woman that a ferritin of 16 nanograms per liter is ‘normal,’ while another woman has a ferritin of 100 nanograms per liter and is also considered normal. I often ask, ‘How could women possibly feel the same across such a wide range?’” says Kahn.

Doctors agree that ferritin below 15 nanograms per milliliter and iron below 30 nanograms per milliliter indicate a deficiency. However, Kahn points out that ferritin levels that are too high aren’t good either. “I also pay close attention to women with ferritin between 30 and 50 nanograms per liter, particularly when they’re experiencing fatigue, exercise intolerance, or hair shedding,” she says. This isn’t to say levels in the 100s are unhealthy. “Reference ranges reflect a population average, not your optimal baseline,” Lester adds.

There is no one-size-fits-all approach to improving your ferritin levels, but you can follow the same protocol you’d use to increase iron and ferritin, since more iron means more for ferritin to store. You can’t pinpoint the causes of the deficiency, but the best place to start is diet. “Diet is often assumed to be the cause, but menstrual blood loss, gastrointestinal blood loss, and impaired absorption are important considerations,” says Gidwani. Historically, the best food sources of iron have been animal sources like red meat, which contains heme iron, generally absorbed more efficiently than the nonheme iron found in plant foods like lentils, beans, and spinach, Gidwani adds. “Pairing plant-based iron sources with vitamin C, such as citrus fruits or bell peppers, can improve absorption.”

Habits are important too, Lester explains. Your daily coffee or tea with breakfast may be affecting your iron and ferritin levels. “Caffeine blocks gut absorption of the iron-rich foods we are consuming,” she adds. Consider having your morning caffeine an hour or two before or after you eat.

The same goes for supplements—experts say to avoid drinking caffeine when you take an iron supplement. “Oral iron is usually the first treatment, and alternate-day dosing can improve absorption and sometimes tolerability,” Gidwani says. “IV iron may be appropriate when oral iron isn’t tolerated, isn’t effective, or absorption is impaired.” Experts also recommend pairing an iron supplement with a vitamin C supplement, similar to the aforementioned food pairings, because vitamin C helps iron absorption.

If ferritin remains low despite consistent supplementation, it may be worth looking into why the iron isn’t being absorbed. Popular culprits are gut conditions like celiac disease, autoimmune gastritis, SIBO, and IBS, as they all interfere with iron absorption. Even poor stomach acid can affect absorption.

Bragging about your TikTok followers may not be the most socially appropriate thing, but bragging about your ferritin levels certainly is. However, more isn’t always better. “I always remind my patients that we need to look at the entire physiological picture, and there is also a meaningful difference between treating a genuine deficiency and simply trying to push a laboratory value higher,” Kahn emphasizes.

“My philosophy is that laboratory values should be interpreted in the context of the individual, not just whether a number falls inside a ‘normal’ reference range,” she adds. “The goal isn’t to chase a perfect ferritin number. It’s to identify deficiencies, understand why they’re occurring, and help women feel and function their best.”

“A ‘successful threesome’ isn’t about fulfilling the couple’s fantasy—unless that is explicitly discussed, negotiated, and consented to,” sex therapist Dr. Ty David Lerman, PhD tells Cosmopolitan. “Rather, it’s about all parties showing up as equals with all needs, desires, pleasure, and boundaries being honored.”

“It kinda takes all of the pressure off of me,” Jess, 30 says.

It’s reserved for a couple, usually heterosexual, seeking a third, usually a woman, to fulfill a fantasy, usually the man’s. Features as: The servant kitchen and the real-life Sarah Berwind’s bedroom as parts of the Russell residence

The Elms is a Classical Revival dwelling that was completed in 1901. Architect Horace Trumbauer designed it for the coal baron Edward Julius Berwind, taking inspiration from the 18th-century d’Asnières French château. It also houses an impressive collection of paintings and tapestries, as well as the latest technology of its age.

In The Gilded Age Seasons 1 and 2, The Elms stands in as part of the Russells’ lavish residence, including scenes in the kitchen and Bertha Russell’s bedroom. But it truly steps into the spotlight in Season 3, when it hosts a dazzling ball. And as a long-awaited confession of love looms (we won’t spoil from whom), the opulent Newport mansion proves to be the perfect setting for high drama and high society.

Each guest is granted a cinematic entrance, illuminated by a golden glow that bathes the grand façade—a new addition this season, as electricity is now becoming available. “The house’s interiors would not have been electrified yet, but people were beginning to use it for parties,” Shaw says. “That would mean they would have to have their own generator installed somewhere on the property to light up those lights.” To accomplish this, the crew went to specialty shops that make reproduction bulbs. “The original bulbs were much bigger than what we have in a household today,” Shaw adds. “Some of the lightbulbs we used were 10 inches in diameter, which is how big they were then.”

Features as: The exterior is used for Caroline Astor’s Beechwood house; the boardinghouse is used for a scene with Oscar van Rhijn; and another room serves as Agnes van Rhijn’s bedroom

Completed in 1852, Château-sur-Mer is one of the first grand Bellevue Avenue mansions of the Gilded Age in Newport, built by architect Seth C. Bradford for William Shepard Wetmore. Its interiors were designed by Ogden Codman Jr., who co-authored Edith Wharton’s Decoration of Houses. The mansion was host to many major parties, including the Fête Champêtre, an elaborate country picnic for more than 2,000 guests in 1857, as well as a debutante ball for Edith Wetmore in 1889. Wetmore’s son, George Peabody Wetmore, went on to have a distinguished political career as the governor of Rhode Island and, later, as a U.S. senator.

In The Gilded Age, the home’s exterior was used for Caroline Astor’s (Donna Murphy) Beechwood House. The hallway, dining room, and ballroom were used during Mamie Fish’s (Ashlie Atkinson) doll tea party. Several bedrooms serve as Agnes van Rhijn’s (Christine Baranski) bedroom, Oscar van Rhijn’s (Blake Ritson) bedroom, Anne Morris’s (Katie Finneran) bedroom, and Patrick Morris’s (Michel Gill) office. There’s also a scene filmed at the boardinghouse with Oscar van Rhijn, according to Patch.

Features as: The office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes

Hunter House was constructed in 1748 at the harborfront near the north end of the Newport Historic District at a time when the city was a bustling seaport in the British empire. The house was owned by wealthy merchants and overlooked the wharves that serviced their trading ships. It’s one of the finest examples of Colonial architecture in Newport, according to the Preservation Society. Inside, its resident families furnished the house with decorative arts produced by expert craftsmen, much of which has been preserved today. Such a luxurious lifestyle, however, depended upon the labor of enslaved people of African descent; recent research by the Preservation Society has identified at least 13 enslaved individuals who lived and worked at Hunter House. Today’s Hunter House honors their perseverance and incalculable contributions to Newport’s past.

One of the sitting rooms in Hunter House functions as the office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes, and is set fictitiously in Doylestown, Pennsylvania.

Features as: The Astor family summer home

Belcourt was completed in 1894 for Oliver Hazard Perry Belmont, a prominent American socialite and coachman who served in the U.S. House of Representatives. Like many of the Newport mansions, the Château-esque structure was completed by architect Richard Morris Hunt; it was designed to mimic the hunting lodge of Versailles, built for Louis XIII of France, but with a special ask to accommodate Oliver’s collection of horses and automobiles. In 1896, Oliver gave the house to his second wife, the former Alva Vanderbilt, who designed the neighboring Newport mansion Marble House. Alva used Belcourt as a gathering place for the elite of society, hosting lavish parties and balls and attracting notable figures in politics, business, and the arts.

In The Gilded Age Season 1, Belcourt shows up in Episode 8 as Mrs. Astor’s lavish new Newport home, which Mrs. Russell sneaks inside and is forced to flee to avoid a less-than-pleasant meetup.

Features as: The social gathering scene where Caroline Astor and George Russell meet

Built in 1867 by prolific Boston tea and china merchant Robert M. Cushing, the Ledges was originally the summer home of the Cushing family. This majestic oceanfront pile is perched on one of Newport’s most desirable peninsulas, overlooking Bailey’s Beach. It was built by architect John H. Sturgis, who designed a large, Stick-style cottage that was equal parts comfort and elegance. Today it features original furnishings, papered walls, and unique artwork, including a studio that was added to the side of the house for Robert’s artistic children.

Today, the estate remains in the hands of the Cushing descendants, who occasionally open the house up to film crews. It was featured a few years ago in the film Evening, starring Glenn Close and Meryl Streep. “Working at the Ledges was pretty extraordinary,” Gilded Age location manager Lauri Pitkus told The Providence Journal, “because I don't think I’ve ever been in a home with spectacular views out of every window.”

This historic house was built in 1864 for John Noble Alsop Griswold, an Old China Trade merchant and diplomat. The house is the work of Richard Morris Hunt; it was one of the earliest buildings in the American Stick style of architecture. The house has a complex roofline with a main mansarded section that is pierced by numerous gable and dormer sections. It boasts a number of balconies sheltered by deep eaves, with gable ends decorated with woodwork. Today, the house is a gallery location for the Newport Art Museum. In the series, the house features in Season 1 Episode 1, and again in Episode 5.

Features as: Mingling with the other elite scene

The International Tennis Hall of Fame and its perfectly preserved grass courts are in the Newport Casino, a National Historic Landmark property. The historic seven-acre estate was commissioned by New York Herald publisher James Gordon Bennett as a social club for the elite summer residents of Newport. The Newport News boasted three days after it opened in July 1880: “It is doubtful if a livelier place can be found.” Today, the space is recognized as the official hall of fame for the sport of tennis globally. Since 1955, 262 distinguished inductees representing 27 countries have been enshrined in Newport.

In the eighth episode of The Gilded Age Season 1, the characters are seen promenading around the piazza that overlooks what is known today as Horseshoe Court. A few characters are even playing tennis. “It was fitting to have that history depicted here, given The Gilded Age’s look into that time period,” the hall of fame’s Megan Erbes tells ELLE DECOR. “Several of our staff members got to work closely with the production team and actors, teaching everything from the history of our property to how to hit a proper forehand.” RI Monthly reported that crews returned to the courts last summer to film Season 2 of the show, so it’s possible that Newport Casino will make another appearance.

Features as: The parlor, library, entryway, and staircase of the Morris house, located on Fifth Avenue in the show; Mr. and Mrs. Morris’s bedrooms were filmed in the Newport mansions

William Smith Slater hired General William Walker to build a home as a wedding gift for his daughter. The result was this historic home, which was completed in 1877. The Victorian house is filled with treasures—the fireplace surrounds, for example, were carved by Charles Dowler, who came to the United States to produce arms for the Civil War. Today, the house is open to visitors and is known as the Clouds Hill Museum.

The Clouds Hill Museum is the only Rhode Island site outside of Newport that was used in the series. The crew scheduled filming for Season 1 in late February of 2021, when a few hundred people were on site during two days of filming, museum director Wayne Cabral told Warwick Online, noting that the taping finished around midnight on the second day of filming (after five days of work to prep the set). Almost all the parlor’s furniture was removed, and the head set designer brought in items sourced from local antiques stores in East Greenwich and North Kingstown, museum president Anne Holst added. The producers were adamant about removing the existing curtains, which were disintegrating, Holst added, so she negotiated to leave the replacements (which cost $10,000) with the museum after filming.

Features as: Visit to the home of Aurora Fane scene

The Gothic Revival–style Lyndhurst Mansion was designed in 1838 by Alexander Jackson Davis. Lyndhurst’s third owner, Jay Gould, is the real-life inspiration for the “new money” character George Russell. “He was fantastically ruthless in business, to an extent that Mrs. Astor would never let him in, he was never allowed into society, and she didn’t let his children in until he was dead,” creator, writer, and producer Julian Fellowes said in a podcast. “But he was an incredibly affectionate father and a very caring and faithful husband…and his children all had these nostalgic memories of Lyndhurst, their house on the Hudson.”

In Season 1 of The Gilded Age, Lyndhurst’s interiors are the home of characters Charles and Aurora Fane (Ward Horton and Kelli O’Hara), who are related to the van Rhijns and part of the “old money” side of society. The Lyndhurst lawns and grounds also doubled as Central Park and the Sheep’s Meadow in the opening sequence. The home boasts a bowling alley that dates back to 1894, which was re-created to be the ferry terminal that Marian Brook (Louisa Jacobson) and Peggy Scott (Denée Benton) take to Manhattan for the first time in Episode 1. And finally, the house’s carriage house was transformed into The New York Globe newspaper offices.

The Belvedere Estate, located between Lyndhurst Mansion and writer Washington Irving’s Sunnyside home, is a century-old mansion on 25 acres in historic Tarrytown. The estate was originally part of Philipsburg Manor, a 90,000-acre property built for Florence and Casper Whitney. “While the mansion dates from the 1920s, it has a much older European feel that could represent something from decades earlier,” says Belvedere Estate sales director Linda Fiorentino, who recalls the massive legwork required to film at the mansion.

“I will never forget the background actors descending from a yellow school bus, fully dressed in period costumes and felt hats,” she says. The Season 1 scene was filmed at the pond at the back of Belvedere Estate, acting as a stand-in for Central Park in the scene when Marian meets Mr. Raikes at Bethesda Fountain in Episode 1. The pond is a man-made feature, and the water level sometimes sinks to a mere foot or two, Fiorentino explains. “Before filming, they filled the pond with roughly 600,000 gallons of water to fill to capacity, giving the illusion of a much larger body of water and to allow them to have pleasure boats filled with passengers on the water,” she says. They also re-created Central Park’s Shakespeare Bridge from rough-hewn logs and brought in trees, shrubs, and foliage to cover the existing link fence because it was too modern.

Features as: The drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain

Toward the end of the 19th century, Colonel Elliott Fitch Shepard, a lawyer who founded the New York State Bar Association in 1876, purchased more than 500 acres on a hill high above the Hudson River north of Tarrytown. He engaged the renowned architecture firm McKim, Mead & White to design his family’s manor house here. The result was a 75-room Victorian structure that mimicked 18th-century Italian villa architecture. Think: hand-carved mahogany paneling, a grand stairway near the front entrance, high patterned ceilings, and elaborate cornice work. Unfortunately, Shepard died before it was completed in 1893, and it passed into the hands of William Rockefeller and Frank Vanderlip. These men were part of a group (including John Jacob Astor, who died on the Titanic in 1912) who went on to convert the building to the Sleepy Hollow Country Club, which remains a private members-only club today.

Scenes in the drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain were shot on location here. “The entire room was redressed by our team in the Rococo style,” set decorator Regina Graves told 1st Dibs.

Features as: Part of Mrs. Astor’s residence

Glenview was built in 1876 by John Bond Trevor, a highly successful stockbroker, who engaged New York City architect Charles W. Clinton to build his estate. The mansion was a paragon of luxury and modern comfort, featuring indoor plumbing, gas illumination, and a coal-burning furnace to circulate heat throughout its 37 rooms. The exterior had elements of Victorian Gothic and French Second Empire design, complete with a mansard roof, a gray stone facade constructed of blocks hewn from a Hastings-on-Hudson quarry, and decorative sandstone elements brought from Indiana.

The mansion makes an appearance as Caroline Astor’s residence in Season 1. “They were all so complimentary about Glenview, and of course we did a lot of polishing and waxing,” Laura Vookles, chair of the museum’s curatorial department, told The Rivertown Enterprise. “They focused on our exquisite interiors and filmed in six rooms, including the dining, library, and sitting rooms, and used all but one painting that is normally on view.”

The John Paine Mansion, also known as the Castle, was built in 1896 and was deemed the grandest house in Troy, New York. It has since served as a private residence, a college administration building, a movie set (see: Martin Scorsese’s 1993 drama The Age of Innocence), and, currently, a fraternity house. In the show, one of the bedrooms in the Castle features as Tom Raikes’s office.

This residence, the home of Troy locals Bill and Sue Comiskey, stands in for the Brooklyn residence of the well-off Scott family. At one point early in the series, Arthur Scott (John Douglas Thompson) and his daughter, Peggy (Denée Benton), sit on carved-oak chairs around a damask-covered dining table. The home’s elaborate details really shine in this historic home, which dates back to the turn of the 19th century. Stained-glass windows and an elegant china cabinet are memorable architectural features that are original to the location and transport the viewers back in time.

While filming what would be the Scott family residence, Bill, the homeowner, told News10 that a few prized family possessions actually made the cut. “My desk was in it, front and center…you [also] saw the whole front and back parlor into the dining room where they were having a meal,” he explained. “[There were] great shots of this room catching the stained glass, the fireplace, [and] this bookcase my grandfather made.”

Features as: The interior is used as George Russell’s workspace

Hempstead House was built for Howard Gould by architects Hunt & Hunt in 1912 on the north shore of Long Island. The mansion was eventually sold to art mogul Daniel Guggenheim (yes, that Guggenheim). Hempstead House is one of four mansions on the grounds of the Guggenheim Estate—which includes Castle Gould, Falaise, and Mille Fleur. The Tudor-style castle was the embodiment of Gold Coast opulence, a place where the esteemed family hosted grand parties, performances, and exhibitions by gatherings of the elite of the time. To say it is massive would be an understatement: 50,000 square feet, with 40 rooms and a 60-foot-tall entry foyer. In The Gilded Age, the mansion’s interior was used as George Russell’s workspace.

Features as: The late General Henry Brook’s house

Rock Hall Museum was initially an 18th-century plantation. The two-and-a-half-story Georgian-style home was built in approximately 1767 and later restored to its original appearance in the 1950s. In Season 1 Episode 1, the house’s white facade is featured within the first 15 minutes as a backdrop for the late General Henry Brook’s house. Marian, his daughter, is seen walking out of the home on her way to the train station.

The Hart Cluett Museum is a work of Federal architecture designed by Martin E. Thompson in 1827. He was hired by William Howard. Thompson brought the design of the Hart Cluett House back to New York City, where many examples of the 1827 Federal-style townhouse were copied throughout Manhattan. This house, a museum open to the public today, was featured in The Gilded Age in Episode 2 as Anne Morris’s home.

#6894. Martine Rose Is Balenciaga’s New Creative Director—and the First Woman to Lead the House

The London designer consulted on menswear at the brand under Demna’s tenure.

Balenciaga is entering a new era with a familiar face. Martine Rose, the British-Jamaican designer who served as a menswear consultant under Demna at the brand, is officially taking over the post following Pierpaolo Piccioli’s departure. The news was confirmed by Balenciaga’s parent company, Kering, on Friday.

“It is a true privilege to be appointed Creative Director of Balenciaga, a House with an extraordinary heritage and a fearless creative spirit,” Rose said in a statement. “Balenciaga has been shaped by remarkable creative talents before me. I am committed to honoring this legacy and look forward to creating the next chapter in this rich and beautiful story.”

While Rose might be a new face to the mainstream, she’s something of an industry darling among those in the know. Born in South London, she launched her namesake menswear label in 2007, drawing on 1990s counterculture and youth scenes, hip-hop, reggae, and football. Her design philosophy challenges conventional ideas of masculinity and gives familiar wardrobe staples an unexpected twist: She might stretch a suit’s proportions or turn sportswear into something sharper and more formal.

Rose, however, is more than a cult designer. Her clothes have found fans in Hailey Bieber, Timothée Chalamet, and Rihanna. In 2021, she sold a 60 percent stake in her brand to London-based fashion accelerator Tomorrow and has sidestepped the traditional fashion calendar for more community-focused showings. Her appointment at Balenciaga brings her back to the house and is also history-making: She is the first woman to lead the brand creatively.

From 2015 to 2018, Rose was brought on by Demna as a menswear consultant, helping launch Balenciaga’s first menswear collections and shape the oversized proportions and brutalist, severe aesthetic that became a signature of his tenure. Her influence on contemporary menswear has earned her the moniker “Your Favorite Designer’s Favorite Designer” among insiders.

“Demna was so brave in giving me a real shot,” Rose told British Vogue in 2021. “Up until that point, I was basically squatting,” she said. “It gave me the chance to actually do something, to put it all back into my own label and make a proper jump forward. It also gave me confidence.”

“Balenciaga has always been a house of radical creativity, challenging conventions and redefining the boundaries of fashion,” Luca de Meo, CEO of Kering, explained. “Martine Rose embodies this spirit with a fresh, fearless, and deeply contemporary vision. Her connection to the new generation and her instinctive understanding of culture bring a new dimension to the house. Her appointment reflects our ambition to keep Balenciaga at the forefront of innovation, creativity, and culture.”

Rose will be based in Paris and begin her role on November 16; the date of her first show has not yet been announced.

#6892. Van Cleef & Arpels Announces Support for Montecito’s Lotusland

The jewelry house heads to Santa Barbara with a fitting gift from a brand captivated by botanic motifs.

Above: The Rose Garden Terrace at Lotusland in Montecito, CA.

Ganna Walska was a lifelong collector. First, there were the husbands: six of them, from a Russian baron to a yoga instructor. Then came the jewelry: hundreds of carats of emeralds, diamonds, and sapphires. Finally, there were plants, her love of which ultimately led her to create a 37-acre botanical garden in Montecito, California.

Walska, an opera singer born Hanna Puacz in Poland in 1887, purchased the Cuesta Linda estate in 1941. She intended to both live there and house Tibetan monks, but after her final divorce (and after the monks failed to show), Walska devoted herself to collecting rare plants. She renamed the property Lotusland, for the Indian lotus in one of the ponds, and poured her fortune into the project. In 1971 she auctioned off many of her jewels to fund her gardens. One piece was the Oiseau clip from Van Cleef & Arpels, a special order depicting a bird in flight carrying a 96.62-carat yellow diamond known as the “Walska Briolette.”

Following her death, the property was opened to the public, and now Van Cleef is making a return. The maison has announced its support of Lotusland, focusing in particular on the Rose Garden Terrace. It’s a gift that honors both Walska’s love of the brand and the inspiration that Van Cleef has pulled from nature for a century, from countless floral variations to the butterfly motif. Jewels will once again endow Lotusland—just as Ganna would have wanted.

The size of your sofa cover will depend not only on the size of the sofa or couch you want to protect, but also on the properties of the fabric itself. Leigh says that with a stretchy fabric, you may want a cover with measurements slightly smaller than the dimensions of your sofa for a tighter, more fitted look. For sofa covers with corner ties that allow you to tighten or tie bows, the fabric may have a bit less give, so you'll want to allow for a little extra room in the cover size.

Some styles lend themselves to being covered more effectively than others. Leigh says that sofas with thinner or rolled arms tend to look nicer with a slipcover than those without. You can also choose between having a skirt on your sofa cover or showing off the legs of your furniture, depending on your aesthetic preference. “I like when they go right to the floor because a skirt cover is a much more traditional, covered look,” Leigh says. “If you have a little bit more of a modern style, having a slipcover that stops at the legs can be a fresher, kind of more updated take.”

Natural fabrics, such as chenille or linen, are typically a great choice when selecting a sofa cover fabric. For family rooms or homes with pets, indoor/outdoor fabrics like Crypton that have been treated to repel dirt and debris are safer bets for those wanting something stain-resistant. The stretchiness of the fabric also plays a role in selecting a sofa cover. "If you wanted something a little bit more durable, canvas or cotton would be a really great material on there," Leigh says.

It's always a good idea to pay attention to the care instructions for whatever cover you buy, as fabric care really depends on the individual material. For example, a heavier cotton material can likely withstand more washing. “I would say only wash when you really need to, just like with a really nice sweater,” Leigh says. “Don't wash it every time you wear it because washing breaks down fibers. So, it could reduce the longevity of your sofa cover. Most are designed to be removed and washed. So if and when you feel like 'oh, you know what, it's looking a little bit dingy, it's time,' that's the time to do it.” Additionally, it's essential to consider the size of your cover, as larger ones may necessitate the use of a commercial washing machine.

Additionally, the cover may wrinkle over time, especially with linen, which softens with each wash. “Linen does get a little bit wrinkly, but I think that's kind of the charm of a slipcovered sofa, having that kind of rumpled, you know, 'someone just took a nap here' look,” Leigh says.

“An oatmeal linen slipcover is that very quintessential, relaxed coastal style that you see a lot of slipcovers used in, and I think it's a classic choice that you'll never go wrong with,” Leigh says. Don't run away from patterns either, though—Leigh is seeing a rise in patterned upholstery in the design industry, and it's a chance to push the envelope and have fun with your design choices. “A cover can be a great way to, you know, try out a pattern without necessarily committing to it forever, because if in five years you say, ‘okay, this pattern was fun, but I'm ready for something different,’ you can just change the slipcover as opposed to having to get an all-new sofa,” Leigh says.
