#7031. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#7026. The AI Police Are Going After Black Writers

The white powers that be have always doubted our intellect. Now they have a new tool.

Picture a waifish enslaved teen—a girl named Phillis, after the slave ship that Middle Passaged her from present-day Senegal and Gambia—trembling before a room of incredulous, austere white men, the most esteemed thinkers and politicians of colonial Massachusetts. The reason: The gifted girl wrote a book manuscript, and the family that owned her journeyed all the way to London to find a publisher for it. That prospective British publisher, a bloke named Archibald Bell, promised to publish the manuscript if the Wheatleys could prove Phillis had written it herself—an attestation tantamount, of course, to whether an enslaved African soon-to-be American possessed the intellect requisite to producing exceptional creative work.

Present at the trial, which has been described by historian Henry Louis Gates Jr. as the nascent scene of African American letters, was Thomas Hutchinson, the governor of the Province of the Massachusetts Bay; Andrew Oliver, the province’s lieutenant governor; and Mr. John Hancock himself, the future first governor of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts. Per Gates, almost all the men forgathered to pass judgment on Phillis Wheatley were both Harvard graduates and slaveholders. No record exists of what transpired, but oh how brilliant Phillis must’ve been as she convinced those 18 eminent Bostonians that she’d indeed written the poems. The confirmation was printed in the front matter of Phillis’s poetry collection—the first book published by an African soon-to-be American—Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773):

We whose Names are under-written, do assure the World, that the Poems specified in the following Page, were . . . written by Phillis, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave in a Family in this Town. She has been examined by some of the best Judges, and is thought qualified to write them.

As my great-grandmother Edith Larkin Jackson, a woman not two generations removed from chattel slavery, used to say, “There’s nothing new under the sun.” Mama Eddie was a schoolteacher, and I imagine she’d be astounded by artificial intelligence. However, she’d have no trouble whatsoever fathoming a world that called into question the creativity or intellect of her people.

Here we are in this brave new world of AI, an age hurtling headlong toward the peril of superintelligence. Here we are in the 21st century, a time when the ivory-tower panjandrums are engaged in a sinister pattern, if not conspiratorial scheme, of incriminating Black and brown writers with even the suggestion that their work is AI assisted.

For example: In March, Hachette Book Group rendered kaput the U. S. publication of Mia Ballard’s Shy Girl over claims of AI usage.

When Trinidadian writer Jamir Nazir’s story “The Serpent in the Grove” was named the regional winner of the Commonwealth Prize in May, Ethan Mollick, a Wharton Business School professor who serves as the codirector of Wharton Generative AI Labs, ran it through the AI-detection software Pangram, touted as the industry standard, and reported that the platform flagged it at 100 percent. Once Mollick shared his findings on social media, several others joined the chorus bemoaning Nazir’s work as a product of AI.

In July, academics studying AI accused H. M. Wolfe of having used a large language model to write her best seller Daggermouth (though its publisher Simon & Schuster has, thus far, stood by the book). Later that month, writer Jerry Falade had a $2 million book deal with Macmillan collapse after his agents withdrew the submission of his debut crime novel citing AI concerns.

In September, Haitian Canadian writer Thélyson Orélien’s novel C’était Ça ou Mourir (It Was Either That or Die) was proclaimed a standout of the French literary season, won France’s Prix du Roman FNAC, and was long-listed for the Goncourt, the country’s most prestigious literary prize. But then an anonymous X account (later revealed to be owned by a conservative columnist) posted that Pangram had identified all 34 chapters of the book as having been written with AI. That claim spurred accusations that Orélien plagiarized earlier work, leading the Goncourt to nix the book from its long list.

In October, The Atlantic accused writer Simbarashe Kundizeza of using AI to write “The Blue Fighter,” which won the Caine Prize, Africa’s most prestigious short-story accolade.

Germane: While Nazir is Trinidadian, Ballard, Wolfe, Falade, Orélien, and Kundizeza are all Black.

Although all those writers have denied claims of using AI to produce their work, I’m not here to dispute whether they did or to what degree they used it, nor the epistemological paradox of using AI to determine whether a writer used AI to write a story. Of equal importance is that, if we ain’t reached the point where all published writing is scanned for possible use of an LLM, what’s the logic informing which writers are investigated for AI use and which aren’t, as well as the attendant issue of which accused writers suffer the most severe fallout, including the scarlet letter of damaged credibility?

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be judged in light of what’s being described as an era of postliteracy. Of the specter of AI sloptimism becoming so ubiquitous it overruns human output. Of the cataclysmic doom of superintelligence approximating the creative work of a human brain to such a degree that the difference will be negligible to the undiscerning masses.

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be apprehended against the beef between the publishing industry writ large and AI. Against 10,000 authors having copublished an “empty” book in protest of their work being pirated to train its models. Against America’s Big Five publishers suing Meta for copyright infringement. Against the recent unsealed documents that reveal OpenAI executives knew they were training their AI on copyrighted material and, furthermore, that AI-authored books were apt to displace in the market ones authored by humans. “There will be a point where a bunch of artists express worry about what we’re doing here, and we’ll likely ignore their concerns and release anyway,” OpenAI policy director Jack Clark wrote in an internal memo back in 2020. The predicament of Black and brown writers should be weighed against the long and calculated history of portraying them as lesser intellects.

Casting doubt on the possibility of Black literary brilliance is in the spirit of a hubristic Saul Bellow asking, “Who is the Tolstoy of the Zulus?” Of 45/47 portraying beaucoup Black people as “not that bright.” Of attacking DEI to paint us as unworthy or incapable, less we are sanctioned by people in power. The same insidious forces—linked to right-wing Cambridge professor James Orr and conservative billionaire Peter Thiel—stoked the allegations that ended in Cambridge professor Jason Arday dying by suicide. (Arday has been posthumously cleared of plagiarism.)

In stark contrast, best-selling novelist and screenwriter Anthony Horowitz has admitted using AI for his work. Best-selling biographer Katie Prescott extolled AI as invaluable in writing her book on Mike Lynch, the late British tech entrepreneur and AI pioneer. And while she’s been called among other things a “grifter” and a “villain,” South African writer Coral Hart has published more than 370 books using artificial intelligence since 2025 and defended her use of LLMs despite criticism and threats. While she later clarified she used it only for research, Nobel laureate Olga Tokarczuk caused a ruckus revealing she queried AI for writing help. Other than Santiago Schnell, a white Latino, resigning as the provost of Dartmouth for using AI in his published articles and The New York Times cutting ties with Alex Preston for an AI-assisted book review, beyond vocal to tepid criticism, white writers aren’t suffering material consequences on par with those of their Black peers.

Poor Phillis Wheatley never published a second book. Despite advertising six times in the newspaper to find a publisher. Despite announcing she’d dedicate it to Benjamin Franklin. Despite General George Washington extolling her as “favored by the muses.” In fact, in 1784, Phillis died at 30 years young, destitute, having placed the last advertisement for her second manuscript just a few months prior.

Because Big Tech is big-time thriving and race is evermore an oppressive tool, critics are bound to keep casting the work of Black writers as AI assisted, in effect electing themselves nouveau versions of those privileged 18th-century Bostonians. Best believe their indictments are a tacit way to portray the work of Black people as unworthy of patronage, acclaim, awards—aka inferior. But be forewarned: If Big Tech succeeds at AI turning the creative work of human brains passé if not obsolete, it’s near foregone that most writers will lose their ability to earn a living. Indeed, the sad-ass upshot is that if AI prevails, all writers—race be damned—will be fated a Phillis.

#7025. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

As generative AI has become embedded in the tools students use to search, write, create, translate, organize, and receive feedback, the boundary between "using AI" and "not using AI" has become increasingly difficult to define. More importantly, treating AI use as a binary choice does little to help students understand the different roles AI can appropriately play in learning.

#7014. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#7005. John Madden Deserved Better Than a Messy Amazon Biopic

A movie can have all the makings of a winner and still fumble the ball.

As a movie critic, I’m not in the business of telling professional filmmakers what they could or should do with their art. What an unproductive way to do my job. But sometimes I see something so egregious, and so boneheaded, I’m compelled to scream from my seat, forehead running hot and veins bulging out—like a coach forced to watch his wide receiver telegraph his route to the defense like a GPS in cleats. That’s how I felt watching MADDEN.

Streaming now on Prime Video, right on time for Thanksgiving football, is David O. Russell’s MADDEN. It’s a big streaming snooze where Nicolas Cage dons a fat suit and putzes around in a confused performance that flip-flops on which part of the legendary coach-turned-broadcaster-turned-foot-spray-spokesman’s interiority we’re meant to sympathize with. The real John Madden suffered cognitive decline until his death in 2021, but Cage’s performance is another exhausting exercise of actors playing undiagnosed dementia—and MADDEN has no calcium in its bones to even commit to that.

Devoid of suspense, stakes, or really any answer to the question “Why?” MADDEN is a plotless dud that falls short in its game plan of probing the unbearable weight of a massive legacy. Worst of all, the spine of a fantastic movie is all there. It’s just not in playing shape. To wit: Scrubbed from the trailers is the reason anyone under the age of 35 knows John Madden’s name: video games. (It is my personal conspiracy theory this was done to mitigate losing an audience of boomer granddads, who will fire up the movie on their Roku before passing out on tryptophan.) In 1988, the former Raiders coach who scribbled on screens lent his name to a project by a small tech company called Electronic Arts. Today it’s valued at $55 billion and owned by Saudis. But back then it was just a start-up full of nerds making a football-simulation video game called Madden NFL, which has since morphed into an industry juggernaut that rakes in the equivalent of a small country’s gross domestic product.

The men and women of Madden’s era are obviously unable to fathom computer games as a big deal. (Even now, on the eve of Grand Theft Auto VI, I have heard from people grossly underestimating its economic and cultural power.) In the film is John Mulaney as Trip Hawkins, the founder of EA, whose wiry physique, skinny ties, and plaid button-downs stand in contrast to the movie’s many beefcake extras who exude ’70s-flavored machismo. But Trip’s presence in the film exists to warn the chain-smoking dinosaurs of their coming extinction. Cementing a legacy is not about winning so much that they make a bronze bust and stick you in the Hall of Fame, with the other dead guys. It’s about making your name mean something. No one sees the future until it’s in front of them—you can only hope you can find your way to be part of it somehow. And somehow John Madden did just that. Unfortunately, even MADDEN fails to recognize the awesome potential of its own story.

Russell’s latest work spends the first half cosplaying as a sober sports biopic, in the vein of Moneyball, until it eventually and perhaps inevitably pivots into a low-stakes tech-business success story like BlackBerry or AIR. This alone isn’t a bad thing—frankly, it should be the entire movie—but MADDEN’s failure to synthesize its core themes about how men can actually live forever is what makes the movie such a frustrating experience to sit through.

MADDEN (the movie, all caps) is concerned with legacy. What does it mean? What does it really take to be remembered? For Cage’s Madden, it starts out as a straightforward idea: win Super Bowls. Football runs in his blood and runs his house; early on, Kathryn Hahn, as Madden’s wife, Virginia, scolds her son for rooting for the wrong team. Raiders football is what feeds and clothes them, she says. Thanking Raiders football is akin to thanking God. When the Raiders start winning and a retired Madden watches quite pathetically with a microphone in his hand, you can see the thousand-yard stare in his eyes as he considers abandoning his faith. Later, Christian Bale’s gel-slicked Al Davis, the owner of the Raiders whose rings form a set of brass knuckles, tries to convince Madden to come back—because winning is all it takes to be remembered, and winning is all that matters.

Before this movie, I had never heard of Al Davis, but I have played plenty of Madden on PlayStation. By the end of the movie (minor spoilers), Cage spends Thanksgiving with his preteen granddaughter, who whomps his butt with a controller in her hands. You can see what MADDEN is getting at here. Real legacy cannot be measured in rings or bronze statues but in the joy you inspire in others. Like it or not, a best-selling video game is how today’s generation of professional athletes stayed interested in the sport, and it might be John Madden’s single greatest contribution to football, more than anything he’s ever done on the field.

Unfortunately, Russell’s slipshod direction ensures that nothing in the movie clicks and nothing in it breathes. Trite dialogue and a flat visual palette that’s barely a notch above Hallmark movies keep it on the bench. As it stands, MADDEN is simply not the movie I would have made. But I’m just screaming from the nosebleeds.

#6972. Has Zegna Cracked the Code to Modern Tailoring?

Pay attention to the world of menswear long enough, and you’ll notice that certain quandaries come up time and again. A favorite among traditionalists and avant-garde types alike is the fate of the suit. It’s been incorrectly declared dead enough times that the handwringing over its demise has largely shifted to questions of its evolving role in the modern guy’s wardrobe: Who wears a suit, and when, and why?

According to Alessandro Sartori, the artistic director of Zegna for a little more than a decade now, there’s another question we should all be asking ourselves: What exactly is a suit these days?

“Everything we do here is grounded in tailoring techniques and in tailoring fabrics,” Sartori tells me over Zoom from his home in Milan. “But I'm a big believer that a suit doesn't necessarily have to be a combination of the classic top and the classic bottom in the same fabric and color.” It can be comprised of those things, he stresses. But the “modern suit,” as he calls it, can also be an intentional pairing of a different kind of top—a chore coat, an overshirt—with a trouser with a different fabrication. The materials and colors should feel complementary and cohesive but needn’t be perfectly matched.

“This is not only a way to give a new life to suiting,” he explains, “but to dress men differently—and, at the same time, keep the quality, the craft, and the beautiful fit that a tailored piece has.”

The idea is to adapt classic techniques to the realities of getting dressed today. “Many fashion brands offer a nice quality, but is not the handmade quality that we do,” Sartori says. “And there are a lot of brands that are offering beautiful, beautiful quality, but they are quite classic.” Since he started in 2016, Sartori’s overarching project has been to populate the gulf between those two modes of production, making clothes that feel contemporary but are rooted in the top-tier fabrics and handwork that helped Zegna become the powerhouse of Italian fashion that it is today.

Her collaborator on the gardens was Spanish landscape architect Luis González-Camino. “My vision was to design a water-efficient Mediterranean garden, divided into distinct yet connected ‘garden rooms’ for different seasons and times of day,” he says. Indoors, López-Quesada installed French terra-cotta floor tiles chosen for their aged look. The three fireplaces were all handcrafted in traditional Menorcan style—a little imperfect, painted white—and decorated with vintage tiles the designer keeps in her Madrid warehouse.

#6953. The “Scottish Country House” Look Is the Cozy Interior Trend Designers Say to Know This Season

You don't need a sprawling stone cottage in the Highlands, a sweeping countryside view, or a roaring fireplace to bring a little Scottish country-house charm into your home. In fact, the appeal of this cozy, heritage-inspired aesthetic is that it can work just as beautifully in a city apartment as it does in a centuries-old estate.

The secret is mixing those old-world details with pieces that feel fresh and livable, so your home doesn't end up looking like a period drama set. A tartan runner on the dining table, a cashmere throw draped over your favorite reading chair, or a few leather accents on the coffee table can bring just the right amount of countryside character to an otherwise modern space. Strathberry's new tartan homeware collection is another lovely entry point, giving the traditional pattern a contemporary spin that feels right at home in all kinds of interiors.

Whether you're fully embracing your countryside daydreams or just looking to make your space feel a little warmer and more layered this winter, there are plenty of ways to bring the look home. Read on for the Scottish country-house-inspired touches that make this aesthetic so charming—and how to work them into your own space.

If there's one pattern that deserves a starring role in a Scottish country-inspired home, it's tartan. And you don't have to limit it to a throw blanket or a few accent pillows. Designer Kaitlin Smith recommends “using plaid for a wallpaper or even as an accent pillow” to bring that distinctly Scottish feel into a space.

For a bolder take, try a tartan wallpaper in a powder room, entryway, or tucked-away reading nook. If you'd rather ease into the look, a single plaid pillow or upholstered accent piece can make just as much of a statement. With so many different tartan patterns and colorways to choose from, you can find one that brings a little heritage-inspired character to your home without making the whole room feel like a theme.

A Scottish country house should feel like it's been lived in for generations, not styled within an inch of its life. That's where woven baskets come in.

Noel Gatts, a designer and HGTV personality, recommends introducing “some slouchy woven baskets” throughout the home, whether at the entryway for shoes and umbrellas or in a living room or bedroom to hold extra blankets. Their slightly relaxed shape and natural texture add to that collected, lived-in feeling while giving everyday clutter somewhere to go.

Try a larger basket beside the front door, a smaller one tucked under a console or an oversized version next to your favorite reading chair. It's a practical detail that also brings in the natural materials that make this aesthetic feel so grounded. Plus, having a cozy throw within arm's reach is never a bad idea.

You can practically picture a pot of tea warming by the fire in a Scottish country house, and Gatts recommends leaning into that feeling with a thoughtful arrangement of crockery and candles.

“Feature a fireplace or dining table with tea pots, earthenware, and an installation of candles and/or candlesticks,” she suggests. This is a lovely opportunity to bring out those pieces you might otherwise save for special occasions, from vintage teapots and handmade ceramic bowls to mismatched candlesticks and well-loved serving dishes. Grouping them together creates a charming focal point without making the space feel overly formal.

Candlelight is especially important here, adding a soft, welcoming glow that feels worlds away from bright overhead lighting. And if you don't have a fireplace, a dining table, mantel or console can give you the same cozy effect.

A Scottish country house shouldn't feel too coordinated, so don't be afraid to bring a few different patterns into the mix. As designer Sarah Cole suggests, “try pairing a wool plaid with a botanical print, a small geometric pattern or a traditional rug.”

The combination adds visual interest and gives a room that layered, lived-in quality that makes country-house interiors so inviting. You can keep the look cohesive by pulling a few warm, autumnal colors throughout the patterns—like russets, burgundies, forest greens and ochres—while varying their scale so they don't compete with one another. It's a simple way to make the space feel more personal and collected over time.

The Scottish country-house look has a wonderful appreciation for pieces that feel like they've been around awhile. Rather than filling every surface with brand-new accessories, Gatts suggests bringing in “found objects with a sense of history” made from weathered wood, pottery, or patinated brass. These are the kinds of details that give a room its personality, whether it's an old wooden box on a coffee table, a vintage ceramic vessel or a brass object with a little wear and patina.

Don't worry about everything matching, either. A mix of materials and finishes makes the space feel more authentic and less like you ordered the entire room in one shopping trip. Gatts recommends using these pieces on a coffee table for “the perfect antique flair,” but a bookshelf, sideboard, or entryway console would work just as well.

Finally, a few well-chosen antique details can give a room that Highland lodge feeling without requiring a full redesign. Jill Wheeler of Birch Hill Interiors recommends adding “a touch of polished brass like antique candlesticks or wall sconces to give the space a refined look.” Try styling a pair of vintage brass candlesticks on a mantel, placing one on a coffee table, or swapping in brass wall sconces beside a favorite reading chair.

Wheeler also suggests incorporating antiques on a mantel, coffee table, or built-in, noting that “antiques can help bring the outside in and tell a story.” The mix of aged finishes and meaningful finds adds a sense of history and character that makes a Scottish country-inspired space feel genuinely lived in.

From someone like Deborah Berke, the architect, to women who have built incredible careers in other fields, it’s important to shine a light on them. #6920. One Martini to Rule Them All

The case for ordering a vodka martini, always.

At a dinner a few years ago, my fiancé’s mother, an elegant former Wall Street banker, ordered a vodka martini, dry, with three olives. I grew up in New Orleans, where I was weaned on sickeningly sweet, sticky cocktails, but I was always fascinated by the martini’s sophistication. I’d seen the drink in the hands of characters like Roger Sterling from Mad Men, who would expertly balance his V-shaped glass as he dropped a cynical witticism. So that night, I told the waiter to make it two. Ever since then, I have favored vodka martinis over gin, the base spirit for the by-the-books recipe.

I’m not the only one. Bartenders in New York have started to notice a trend away from gin. Chris Amirault of Bar Chimera in Manhattan says that he makes four vodka martinis for every one gin martini. “Vodka pays the bills, and it’s what a lot of people prefer,” says Brian Evans of the Lobby Bar at the Hotel Chelsea, also in Manhattan.

For those who insist that a true martini must only be made with gin, Tristan Stephenson, host of the Curious Bartender podcast, says, “History isn’t on the side of purists. The martini has been a moving target since the 1880s. It started sweeter, with maraschino, orange curaçao, and sugar syrup making appearances. Then it dried out over decades, then had the vermouth all but exorcised from it. Then came olive brine and everything since.”

Why do many martini drinkers prefer vodka? Ti Adelaide Martin, coproprietor of New Orleans staple Commander’s Palace, succinctly explains one reason: “Vodka is the more approachable spirit.” Unlike gin, whose botanicals impart noticeable flavors to a martini, vodka doesn’t overpower other ingredients. It’s more of a blank canvas, which is why it’s used for variations like the espresso martini, which is now the fifth-most-ordered drink at bars, according to Drinks International. Instead of strong flavor, vodka provides “texture, weight, and a kind of icy austerity that can be genuinely beautiful when the vodka is good and the drink is properly cold,” says Stephenson.

And, let’s be honest, we also drink vodka martinis for the booze. “Vodka tastes bracing,” says Esquire editor in chief Michael Sebastian. “I want to take a sip of my martini and get the feeling that it’s strong and it’s cold, and it goes through your whole body and you feel better and loose immediately.”

Now I order my vodka martini with a splash of vermouth, just dirty enough to make me thirsty, and shaken so that thin ice shards float on top. You might prefer yours vermouth forward, citrus focused, or dirty as swamp water. It’s all fair game. And that’s the beauty of a vodka martini: It’s a personal drink. My future mother-in-law has her order, and I have mine. What’s yours going to be?

#6912. Before & After: Two Best Friends Turned a Stripped-Down Tudor Into a Colorful, Antique-Filled Dream Home

Designer Anne Hulcher Tollett understood her client’s signature aesthetic better than anyone.

Anne Hulcher Tollett can’t remember a life without her best friend, Van. They met in a playpen as toddlers and quickly became an integral part of each other's lives, with many childhood memories taking place in Van’s house growing up. Tollett has been with her best friend through every milestone, including as her maid of honor at her wedding to her husband, Sean, and when the couple put an offer on a suburbanized Tudor home in Richmond, Virginia.

The five-bedroom house had good bones and an intuitive flow, but it needed help getting some of its charm back. When Van and Sean bought it for their family of four (plus their dog, Jelly Bean, and a small flock of chickens), it was whitewashed and lacked architectural character. In short, it wasn’t up to par with Van’s eclectic style, according to Tollett, but as the principal designer of Hanover Avenue, she reassured her bestie that she could easily take it from suburban chic to warm, layered, and personality-filled without having to completely rework the interior.

“Van’s aesthetic is diametrically opposed to ‘simple’ or ‘builder-grade,’ and although this house had been meticulously renovated and maintained by the previous owners, some of the charm she loves had been removed,” says Tollett. The designer and her team focused on lighter renovations and cosmetic upgrades, walking her best friend through every decision, from wallpaper choices to reworking the staircase to highlight the amazing treasures Van has collected on her travels over the years.

Throughout the renovation, Tollett and Van leaned into their sisterly shorthand, resulting in a project with very few disruptions. “Our biggest challenge was creating unexpected quirky moments without taking down walls or undoing all of the thoughtful and well-built upgrades that came with the house,” Tollett explains. “We found places on tables, walls, and bookshelves to highlight everything Van and her family have collected over the years. It makes the house feel like theirs and not the product of some design firm.”

Walls were covered with grasscloth, patterns were layered on throw pillows and vintage rugs, and anything that wasn’t antique or pre-owned was chosen to add a touch of patina to the space, allowing Van’s collections to truly sing.

Once Tollett was finished with the renovation, the house told the story of their friendship and a life well lived. Because the designer knows Van like the back of her hand, she was able to create a retreat that feels like a true reflection of who she is and how her family lives without editing out any of Van’s eccentricities. “We understood her design sensibility and took something well-built but fairly plain and made it fun, pretty, and interesting at every turn, which is exactly how anyone would describe Van!” Tollett adds. Besides, who better to design your house than the person who knows you better than yourself?

The barrel back and turquoise chairs were finds that Tollett sourced for Van’s previous home, proving that timeless profiles and accessible sizes can work in many different spaces throughout their lifetimes.

“A room like this with a vaulted ceiling and white walls creates a wonderful backdrop for bold pops of color and patterns because it can visually hold its own against furnishings with personality,” she says. “Van had the beautiful sideboard, and we loved pairing it with the tattooed table.”

Possibly one of the most remarkable makeovers in the entire house, Tollett coated the entryway in deep blue paint, topping it off with darling wallpaper on the ceiling for added dimension. “This is a great example of how a can of paint, a roll of wallpaper, and some strategically placed treasures can transform a room,” she says.

Large-scale grasscloth wallpaper instantly gave the once-all-white box character and rhythm, setting a serene backdrop for the rest of the unique finds in this dining room. Above the antique bar (whose natural patina plays so well with the wallpaper), are two framed photos of puppets, which Van found during one of her globe-trotting trips. “We removed the big-box island lighting and reworked the storage, hood, and casework on the range wall,” Tollett explains. “We did the same on the wall opposite the range,” she adds, which was in fantastic shape, so they kept it.

“Open concept kitchens are tricky because they are seen from every angle, so we knew we needed to improve the storage to hide clutter,” she adds. They papered the walls in warm neutral grasscloth and hung relaxed Roman shades to softly filter the natural light, furnishing the room with many of the fabulous pieces Van already owned (and loved). “This is a wonderful example of ‘reimagining what you have,’ and finding ways to remember the ones we love in our own rooms,” Tollett adds. “Van’s mom was the coolest!”

“This room is a grown-up translation of Van’s entire design sensibility that began when she was young and was nurtured in her adulthood during her travels,” Tollett adds.

Dubbed the “dayroom”—an informal spot dedicated to relaxation and reading—Tollett wanted it to feel cozy and as collected as the rest of the home but still a bit private. They washed the walls in a soft gray-green hue and did the same to the custom day bed, finishing off the room with antique bookcases in a complementary gray tone. It’s easy for small rooms like these to feel overcrowded, but the pale color palette takes away some visual weight.

Tollett finds a thrill in uncovering her clients’ design styles and endless joy in creating their dream homes that reflect who they are.

#6895. What to Eat for Healthier Skin and Slower Aging

A dietitian breaks down the foods, nutrients, and habits that may support skin from the inside out.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

On the quest to age smarter and slower, there’s a lot that your skin care can do. But perhaps one of the greatest contributions lies in the foods you consistently eat. Of course, diet and exercise are the pillars of good health, proper body function, and longevity, but a recent study done by scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) also reveals that 10 specific diets are linked to slower aging. Experts unpack the findings for ELLE and break down which foods you should incorporate or ditch from your diet to help improve skin health.

To understand the connection between diet and skin health, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry, says it’s important to define the notion of slowing down aging. She points out that this particular DZNE study didn’t look at wrinkles or how old people feel. “It looked at biological aging, measured with epigenetic clocks. These read chemical marks on our DNA (methylation) that shift as our cells age. In about 7,500 adults, the researchers found that following any of 10 healthy eating patterns more closely was associated with slower biological aging,” Hosmer explains. The 10 eating patterns or diets included in the study results are Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based. Hosmer says that while the effect was modest, this kind of study shows an association, not causation—proof that diet is the reason for the positive outcome—so it is still a meaningful signal.

Additionally, Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Plan, tells ELLE that no single food has been satisfactorily studied and shown to be likely, on its own, to slow aging. “Of all the predictors of a long, healthy life, two of the best, which can be improved with diet, are muscle mass and strength, but this leads to a controversial conclusion,” says Gilbert. “The best nutritional intervention to increase muscle and strength is eating a high-protein diet. However, many scientists argue that high-protein diets have the opposite effect on longevity. So, if we can’t even agree on that, you can see that science has a long way to go to try and figure this out.”

One stat Hosmer found particularly striking was that over 70 percent of the biological pathways affected were the same across all ten diets. “I interpret that to mean that no single superfood is doing the work, and it affirms that [a combination of] food, diet, and nutrition impacts us on the cellular level. The foods these patterns share are what matter,” says Hosmer, who, along with Gilbert, breaks down the standouts:

Alongside individual foods, Hosmer says certain diet components have the greatest impact on aging. For starters, protein is a must. “As we age, our muscles respond less efficiently to the protein we eat. This is called anabolic resistance, and in women, the drop in estrogen makes it worse,” she explains. Building and retaining muscle isn’t only about strength; Hosmer says it’s our largest site for handling blood sugar and a strong predictor of independence later in life. “Many women eat less protein just when they need more.”

Gilbert adds that while protein is an important part of a balanced, longevity diet, there are some considerations to keep in mind before adding it to your meal. “I believe that because muscle and strength are so strongly associated with longevity and that protein, particularly later in life, supports them,” he says. The caveat, however, is that there is a potential mechanism (which has to do with the amino acid methionine) whereby too much protein could have negative effects. “Many scientists argue that, because in insects and some other animals, less protein along with calorie restriction increases lifespan, humans would similarly benefit. Also, some cite the so-called ‘Blue Zones’, alleged to show that plant-based, low-protein diets are superior. In my opinion, all of these have been shown to be invalid,” says Gilbert.

Fiber is another essential nutrient that you’ll reap from plant foods and mushrooms. Meat, fish, dairy, and eggs contain no fiber, and because our enzymes can’t break them down, Hosmer says that fiber travels intact to the large intestine to assist. Once there, fermentable fibers become food for the gut microbiome; other fibers mainly add bulk and keep things moving. “As gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the cells lining the gut, help keep that barrier strong, and keep inflammation in check. Fiber also steadies blood sugar and helps the body excrete estrogen the liver has already processed, supporting healthy hormone balance. Yet more than 90 percent of American women don’t reach the recommended 21 to 25 grams a day,” Hosmer explains.

Plant diversity matters, too, and not just quantity. Hosmer points to research from the American Gut Project that found people who ate 30 or more different kinds of plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. “That’s because each plant brings its own mix of fibers and polyphenols, and some bacteria have preferences, so a varied diet feeds a broader community of microbes. A broader community does more jobs. It makes short-chain fatty acids that protect the gut lining, helps regulate immunity and inflammation, turns plant compounds into forms the body can use, and even helps process estrogen.” The variety of plants Hosmer adds seems to matter more than any single “perfect” plant.

You’ve likely heard of healthy fats, meaning omega-3s from fish and the polyphenol-rich fat in olive oil. Adding these to your diet can support cell membranes and help settle inflammation.

Also on the list for Gilbert is vitamin D, which he says studies have shown to be inversely related to the risk of mortality. “This does not prove that low vitamin D is the cause, but it is compelling evidence. Although some results are controversial, low [vitamin D] levels have been linked to all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and infection-related mortality,” he adds. Vitamin D is intimately involved in immunity, too. It protects T-cell immune response.

Although Gilbert says, “The only thing all good nutritionists agree on is that there is no one ‘best’ diet or ‘best’ food,” he warns that for most people, minimizing the intake of sugar and refined, starchy grains and increasing protein intake is, in my opinion and experience, the best approach, but, again, different people can thrive on different diets. “As in the examples I gave, many of these foods contain phytochemicals—plant chemicals like polyphenols, flavanols, cyanidins, and others – which affect disease risk or support systems in the body that are likely to serve you well in old age.”

Hosmer notes several other red flags as contributing to unhealthy skin: Diets dominated by refined carbohydrates and added sugars, heavily processed meat, and regular alcohol. “Each of these drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation researchers call “inflammaging,” and blood-sugar instability.”

It’s made to help close the protein, fiber, and plant-variety gaps that so many women are carrying,” she adds.

The information can be overwhelming, but instead of purging everything in your fridge, Hosmer suggests first focusing on adding good foods instead of restricting less healthful options. “In my experience, that’s more sustainable, both because it feels less like deprivation and because the “good” foods can displace the ones that speed aging up instead of slowing it down. You don’t need to pick the “right” diet and follow it perfectly.”

She highlights that the DZNE study found few people scored well on all ten diets at once, yet better adherence to any of them helped. What matters is the common ground. Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based diets vary on the plate, but they have a common core: more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and less red and processed meat, and added sugar. “That shared core is where I’d start,” says Hosmer, offering this meal planning guide:

Increase your familiarity with, and use of, plant-based proteins. Plant-based proteins offer an excellent package of beneficial nutrients, including fiber (not found in animal products). If you aren’t already familiar, learn how to include whole soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame) in your diet; add beans and peas and lentils to dishes here and there. Nuts and seeds and nut and seed butters count here also.

Build meals around vegetables and fruit. Fill half your plate with them, and choose deep colors like leafy greens, berries, beets, and cabbage-family vegetables. Those colors come from polyphenols, plant compounds that act as antioxidants and feed beneficial gut bacteria.

Make your grains whole and varied. Oats, rye, barley, brown rice, and whole-wheat bread are staples of the Nordic and DASH patterns, the two most consistently linked to slower aging in this study. Try a grain you haven’t eaten before—quinoa? Farro? Millet or teff? They are all cooked the same way—in boiling water, just with different grain-to-water ratios. Note: raise fiber gradually, with plenty of water, so your gut has time to adjust.

Count your plants, not your calories. Aim toward 30 different kinds of plant foods a week. Veggies, fruits, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans, peas, herbs, and spices all count. A homemade trail mix, an interesting salad, or a smoothie with a handful of fruit and veggies can add three or four at once.

Choose your fats deliberately. Most of these patterns favor unsaturated fats over saturated ones. Use extra-virgin olive oil as your everyday fat, and eat oily fish two or three times a week.

Keep red and processed meat occasional, and keep added sugar low. Every one of these diets limits them.

Quality matters. Note that “plant-based” isn’t automatically healthy. A version of the plant-based diet built on refined grains, sweets, and sugary drinks tended to be associated with faster aging, as did a pro-inflammatory eating pattern overall. Food quality matters, not just food category.

Diet is the foundation the other efforts rest on. Collagen supplements and skin care can help, but they can’t make up for a body that lacks what it needs to build and protect tissue. According to Gilbert, collagen supplements contain very high levels of the amino acid glycine, which he says can balance the potential harms of proteins that are high in methionine.

Hosmer further explains that collagen peptides break down during digestion into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then decides where to use them. “To actually build collagen, it needs enough total protein, plus vitamin C. Vitamin C is a required helper for the enzymes that stabilize the collagen structure. It also needs minerals like zinc and copper. So a collagen supplement taken on top of a protein-poor, micronutrient-poor diet is not going to be optimized,” Hosmer says. “Collagen is also an incomplete protein: it’s low in leucine and lacks tryptophan entirely. It can add to your protein intake, but it isn’t a substitute for complete proteins when it comes to muscle.”

On the surface, what we eat affects the skin from the inside. Hosmer notes that polyphenol-rich foods and healthy fats support the skin’s own defenses against oxidative stress, and high blood sugar leads to glycation, which stiffens collagen fibers. The gut matters here, too. “Researchers call the connection the gut–skin axis. When the gut lining is healthy, and the microbiome is well fed, inflammation throughout the body tends to stay lower, and the skin reflects that. External products can only do so much if we aren’t supporting our skin from the inside,” Hosmer says.

Diet plays a major role in overall skin health and slower aging, but Hosmer argues that it’s not the full picture. To optimize more youthful-looking skin, consider other factors that may be equally as important as diet.

#6890. A New Exhibition Celebrates the Mantilla in Fashion and Art

The garment finally gets its due credit at The Hispanic Society Museum & Library.

Few garments have been as imitated, reinterpreted, and misunderstood as the mantilla. The iconic lace veil with Spanish roots has endured for centuries. What started as an object traditionally worn for ceremonies has had a renaissance via Madonna, Rosalía, and Dolce & Gabbana, just to name a few. That’s just one of the reasons why The Hispanic Society Museum & Library in New York is opening its new exhibition “Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” beginning November 20. “The understanding of the mantilla in all its complexity is often limited, either by a superficial reading of the symbol itself or by its association with an old-fashioned, rather conservative image of Spain,” López tells ELLE. “This exhibition challenges the perception of the mantilla as an anachronistic relic of the past and reclaims its continued cultural relevance and subversive potential. The exhibition is the second installment in the Hispanic Society’s multiyear fashion initiative, which focuses on reinterpreting its own renowned collection through a new lens. The museum was founded in 1903 by American philanthropist Archer Milton Huntington and has many visual examples of the garment, including Francisco Goya’s famed portrait of María del Pilar Teresa Cayetana de Silva y Álvarez de Toledo, the 13th Duchess of Alba (1762–1802). “By bringing together works of art, fashion, and material culture, the iconography of the mantilla activates a collection of such an extraordinary diversity, through unexpected dialogues between the museum’s different departments and international loans,” adds López.

The new exhibition pulls from many cultural moments new and old, including the “Spanish Craze,” which was a period of cultural fascination with Spain in the United States spanning 1890 to 1930. “During this period, the mantilla became part of a highly recognizable visual language through which Spain was imagined and represented in America,” says López. “Spanish performers who travelled to the United States achieved remarkable success: Raquel Meller, for instance, appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1926, while Concha Piquer starred in From Far Seville (1923).” He also points to the reinterpretation of the mantilla in The Devil Is a Woman (1935), starring Marlene Dietrich.

Fashion naturally plays a role in the show, especially through the imagination of emerging designers, like Arturo Obegero. “Throughout the history of fashion, major designers such as Cristóbal Balenciaga and Óscar de la Renta have drawn inspiration from this [Spanish] visual language, including the mantilla,” adds López. “However, it was a curatorial decision to bring contemporary designers, including emerging voices, into direct conversation with the masterpieces in order to make justice to the mantilla’s enduring influence on fashion to this day.”

The mantilla has been loved (and reinvented) in contemporary fashion at least since the 1940s, when it appeared in numerous fashion magazines. Today, the references are endless and people of all cultures and backgrounds are embracing it. “I think there is something particularly beautiful about the fact that those who have historically been excluded from the dominant narrative, who may have felt unrepresented or even oppressed by these national symbols, can now feel encouraged to revisit this folklore and expand its boundaries to make room for others to do the same,” says López. As the exhibition makes clear, the mantilla’s compelling story is still unfolding.

“Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled” is on view at The Hispanic Society Museum & Library from Nov. 20, 2026, through March 28, 2027.

#6867. This St. Bart’s House Was Designed to Look Nothing Like Its Neighbors

Built for maximum family time, a converted boutique hotel puts the emphasis on entertaining, hanging out, and blending into the surroundings.

Few clients ask their designer to make the bedrooms in their vacation home smaller and simpler, but that was just the edit the homeowners made to plans for their house in St. Bart’s, says designer David Ries. “They didn’t want these humongous bedrooms with huge floating beds and massive bathrooms.” Instead, the couple wanted to be able to host as many people as possible, a request that didn’t surprise Ries, who’d already worked with the family on homes in Manhattan, Long Island, and Telluride. “It’s what this client has always asked for. They can’t have enough bedrooms.”

Situated on a steep hillside above one of St. Bart’s famously pristine white sand beaches, the house had initially been a boutique hotel. The plan was to turn it into a retreat for themselves and their adult children and grandchildren. The family are longtime visitors to the island—“the daughter told me it was her ‘happy place,’” says Ries, lead designer of the New York-based interior design firm Ries Hayes—and knew exactly what they wanted. Not the white box look popular with luxury new builds on the island, but instead something reminiscent of the “old St. Bart’s,” says East Hampton-based landscape designer Edwina von Gal, who, like Ries, has collaborated with the clients on other projects.

A Caribbean island is a challenging place to work under any circumstances. Everything has to be brought in from far away, including all the plants, which came by barge from Miami. St. Bart’s in particular requires anyone building to work with a local architect and engineer. In their initial proposal, this French team defaulted to that “slick and modernist” look, as von Gal describes it, with the oversized bedrooms and white color scheme that dominates much of the island’s luxury housing stock.

The design team pushed back—asking for a lot of bedrooms (eight) and generous living spaces. They also had strong opinions on the building materials. Given the island’s climate, the humid, salt-soaked air can be hard on everything. “If we did white paint, which is prominent throughout St. Bart's, it’s constant maintenance, constant painting,” says Ries. The design team insisted on cladding the buildings in Ipe, the durable tropical hardwood, which wears well. It mystified the local architects and set the complex apart from its neighbors. It also makes the buildings disappear into the greenery of the hillside.

But the architects quickly caught on to the new plans, and von Gal is eager to praise their technical accomplishments. Regulations require properties to conserve rainwater, so there are cisterns beneath the buildings, plus technology infrastructure and laundry facilities. “Working on the hill is so complex,” she says. “It's such a tight little package of technology and function. What they did was amazing.”

The team decided to reconfigure the property as a series of pavilions that face the view of the ocean and surrounding hills. The main entry is at the top of the steep hill, along with parking. The next level down is for family bedrooms; below that is a primary suite and study; and finally the fourth level is the main living space, including a playroom. “We made sure this playroom was front and center,” says Ries. “My client loves her grandkids.”

The playroom and study have built-in daybeds that can be used as spare guest rooms in a pinch. But the clients did get their eight bedrooms. “We know how to make compact bedrooms and make them very efficient, make them very livable, which allows us to fit in a lot of space for a lot of guests, and then that puts a lot of emphasis on having main entertaining spaces that are big and gracious,” says Ries. “The idea is that everybody hangs out together.”

Everything is united by generous outdoor spaces, which wind between the bedrooms. “Every room has an amazing view,” says Ries. “It feels almost like your own little treehouse in a way.” The bottom living area opens out to a pool and an expansive deck where the family eats most of their meals.

The house has an elevator, but stairs are the primary way the family moves between levels and finally down to the beach. “We hung on for a really great, gracious, fun set of stairs,” says von Gal. “They really introduce you to how amazing the different levels are and all the wonderful secret places.”

Durable fabrics and materials were chosen throughout and Ries made sure everything could be easily moved around and reconfigured. Outside on the main deck, three long dining tables from Sutherland were outfitted with casters so they could be maneuvered into a single long table or several smaller ones; 26 lightweight dining chairs can be pulled from different parts of the deck. If the infamous “Christmas winds” bring a downpour, the tables can even be moved indoors so a dinner party can proceed in inclement weather. Outdoor seating can be arranged as a 15-foot sofa or several love seats.

“It's so flexible,” says Ries. “We know this from working with a client that their needs for entertaining change every day, or sometimes a couple times a day.”

The kitchen is also efficient, designed to produce easy, clean meals. The family would rather spend time in the pool than over a stove. And no part of the living room is oriented around a TV. “They're readers,” says Ries. “There’s always stacks of new books when you go to their homes. So there’s all these cool little spots you can tuck into.”

Ultimately, the client and design team’s insistence on what they wanted paid off—but it wasn’t without a few moments of doubt. “When so many people are telling you ‘no, surely this is not what you want,’ it does start to erode your confidence a little,” says von Gal. “Then when it does come out well, there’s a real sense of triumph and satisfaction.”

#6846. Are You a Super Ager? This Competition Will Be the Judge of That

The challenge is just the first of eight trials Krepeck will tackle throughout the day alongside his sons, Harrison and Aaron, at the Super Age Games, a fitness and mental competition designed to assess abilities associated with healthy aging. On November 7th, the inaugural day-long event drew 530 athletes from 42 states and 15 countries, and about 70% of participants were between the ages of 40 and 64. Krepeck agreed to let me shadow him to understand this new event through the lens of someone who is, in fact, aging, and would like to do so gracefully.

The name of the Games comes from a buzzword that originated in the longevity world: super ager. The term was originally coined by Northwestern University researchers to describe adults over 80 whose memories rival those of people 30 years younger. These days it’s thrown around on podcasts and in books to describe anyone who defies both the typical trajectory of both cognitive and physical aging.

The inaugural Super Age Games drew 530 athletes from 42 states and 15 countries.

From crawling across a cargo net to reading microexpressions, the challenges in the competition measure longevity markers like functional strength, aerobic capacity, balance, and cognitive skill. At the end, athletes see where they rank among their age group and receive their “Super Age,” learning how their body’s physical fitness stacks up against the general U.S. population’s.

In an era where your average middle-aged midwestern dad is training for Hyrox, and a new, outlandishly extreme challenge is being invented every few months, the Super Age games are a low-key counterpoint to raw, stunty, athleticism, born from the metric-obsessed fitness subcultures who prize longevity above all. And now that fitness trackers like Whoop or Apple Watch can track VO2max, heart rate variability, and resting heart rates, that group continues to grow. Along the way, these stats have become status symbols on par with owning a Lamborghini. And now that overall health is quantifiable, fitness values have become less about just how fast you run or how much you lift, but how slowly aging.

It follows that, despite its name, Super Age Games isn’t just for aging athletes. The crowd there leaned middle-aged, and the youngest person at the competition was 21. As we stood by the dead hang station, Krepak pointed across the room at a brolic young man who looked like he could be an NFL prospect: “That dude’s a serious athlete,” he says, as we watched him run around the track cradling a sandbag on his shoulder.

Unlike competitions like Hyrox, where athletes race through a sequence of workouts against the clock, the Super Age Games allows competitors to rest as long as they need between trials. In fact, Junior Kennedy, the fitness program director for the Games, encourages it. “There's no rush,” he says. “The goal is to get through each event as intentionally as you can. We need to understand what it means to recover.”

Each trial was developed with a certain longevity marker in mind, and developed with input from researchers at the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, a leading nonprofit research center based in Novato, California dedicated to understanding aging. The Over Under, for example, tests agility by judging how fast you can change levels and switch directions. Meanwhile, the Synapse, a challenge where participants track moving balls on a screen and identify them after they've been shuffled around, is meant to measure cognitive processing.

#6844. How to Care for a Thanksgiving Cactus so It Blooms Right on Time

Here's what experts say you need to know about this low-maintenance plant.

Want an easy-care plant to brighten your home through fall and winter? Skip the usual mums and try a Thanksgiving cactus. Though it’s native to Brazil, this plant looks nothing like a desert cactus: Its flat, toothed stems end in pointed tips, and its vivid tubular flowers usually appear in late November. That timing is how it got its name. It’s related to Easter and Christmas cacti, which flower around their namesake holidays.

Thanksgiving cacti can grow outdoors in USDA Hardiness Zones 10–12, but they’re well suited to indoor life in any climate. Their bright flowers come in colors including red, pink, white, and yellow, adding a cheerful burst of color to a room.

Wondering how to help yours thrive? Here’s what to know about growing and caring for a Thanksgiving cactus.

The Thanksgiving cactus loves bright spots that aren't in direct sunlight. Too much sun can cause the plant to turn yellow rather than its beautiful green, so keeping it by a north- or east-facing window or even a shady south- or west-facing window is ideal. For your plant to produce the best flowers, it shouldn't receive more than 12 hours of light. It needs the darkness to produce blooms.

All holiday cacti require a bit more magnesium than other houseplants, so finding your Thanksgiving cactus a magnesium-rich fertilizer you can add to a well-draining soil is essential. The Home and Garden Information Center (HGIC) suggests supplementing its water with Epsom salts—try mixing in one teaspoon of Epsom salts per gallon of water. But avoid doing so during the week you add the fertilizer, which you should do once a month. Stop adding fertilizer in the late summer months for better flower production in the fall.

Thanksgiving cacti should be planted in a pot with drainage holes at the bottom since they won't thrive in soggy soil. Consider adding peat moss or sand to the pot to prevent pooling water around the roots. Thanksgiving cacti are root-bound, so there's no need to repot them for about two to three years.

The soil surrounding your Thanksgiving cactus should never be soaking wet or bone dry. However, the soil should be consistently moist and never fully dry to the touch. Holiday cacti are prone to root rot, so standing water is your main enemy. During the hot months, they can stand to be slightly underwatered.

Like most tropical plants, Thanksgiving cacti thrive in a humid, warm environment. However, the typical household temperature can keep a cactus very healthy too. The temperature shouldn't get above 80 degrees Fahrenheit or below 55 degrees, though ideally you should keep your cactus between 75 and 80 degrees during the day, according to the University of Wisconsin-Madison's Horticulture Department.

Pruning is an important task for plant lovers to keep any houseplant healthy, and Thanksgiving cacti are no different. To promote bud growth, cut the stems in the late spring or early summer, and make sure you have the right gardening tools for the job. Be sure to save the cut stems for propagation too!

If you want more of these tropical cacti around your house, maybe for a huge, flowering centerpiece for the holidays, use cut stems to produce more plants. HGIC suggests using stems with three to five phylloclades (or stem joints) on each to start. Set the segments aside for a day to dry out, then stick them about one inch deep into a pot with moist soil. Do this only in the spring and summer while the plant is actively growing, and avoid doing it in the winter once it has bloomed.

Because the Thanksgiving cactus is native to Brazil, you should try to mimic its natural environment as best you can to get it to bloom, which is near the end of the rainy season. The gardening department at Michigan State University Extension explains that for the buds to develop on your indoor plant, Thanksgiving cacti need at least 13 hours of total darkness each day, bright light when they're not covered up or hidden away, and a consistent temperature ranging between 55 and 65 degrees. You should start this process in late September or early October if you want blooms around the holiday season.

Once the plant has flowered for the season, then you can go back to taking care of it the way you normally would with the help of the guidelines written above.

According to PetMD, Thanksgiving cacti are not toxic to dogs and cats, unlike another holiday plant, the poinsettia. However, that doesn't mean you should let your furry friends munch on it. If large amounts are consumed, your pets can experience vomiting, diarrhea, and a decreased appetite.
