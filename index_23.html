#6856. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#6850. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6844. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6842. SailPoint Intros New Capabilities to Address Agentic AI Identity Security Challenge

That gap could become harder to ignore as organizations give agents more autonomy. SailPoint, which develops identity security and access governance technology for enterprises, is responding with a collection of new identity security capabilities announced at its Navigate 2026 conference. The company is expanding its Agentic Fabric to discover "shadow AI" agents and provide runtime authorization and controls capable of immediately disabling an agent.

The company also introduced what it calls SailPoint Autonomous Agents, specialized AI agents designed to govern other agents. The security agents can monitor runtime activity, intercept threats, and adjust permissions while allowing legitimate agents to continue operating, SailPoint said.

The idea reflects one of the challenges created by increasingly autonomous AI systems: Traditional human approval processes may not be fast enough to govern software capable of taking large numbers of actions on its own.

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. #6802. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

#6779. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

Maybe the urge to put our phones down and experience the real world (and all of its sounds, smells, and feels) is the most compelling thing about what’s coming down the runway right now. It’s fashion that wants to make you feel alive.

#6742. 9 Interior Trends Designers Are Officially Ditching in 2027

Some of this year’s biggest design moments may not age well.

The end of the year is a great time to look back and reflect. When it comes to home design, there’s a lot to cover, from new trends to furniture collaboration drops to specific internet-breaking interiors. One of our favorite things to do around this time is figure out what’s coming next in terms of interiors.

We love predicting what’s going to be hot in home design for the upcoming seasons; however, you can’t talk about what’s going to be “in” without also touching on what’s going to be “out.” We asked a handful of designers to share what design choices and interior trends they saw a lot of in 2026 that they’re more than happy to leave behind in 2027. Keep reading to find out the nine things they said.

We spend so much time making our interiors warm and reflective of our personalities, it would be a shame to ruin the look of these spaces by opting for one-dimensional lighting.

“It's time to move beyond relying on a single overhead fixture or wall-to-wall recessed lighting to illuminate an entire room,” designer Kristen Mendoza of Four Brothers Design + Build says. “Flat, overly bright lighting can make even the most beautifully designed spaces feel sterile and uninviting.”

Instead, incorporating layers of lighting to add dimension and create a warm atmosphere is much better for your home. Sconces, picture lights, table lamps, and thoughtfully placed accent lighting at various heights throughout the room, Mendoza adds, give a space a more intimate touch.

“I also recommend using warm 2700K light sources on dimmers to flatter people, highlight architectural details, and bring out the natural beauty of materials,” she explains. “Lighting should be considered early in the design and construction process, not treated as an afterthought.”

Part of the appeal of “grandma chic” decor is that it’s been collected over time. The pieces show their age, the interior is imperfect, and it’s very clearly reflective of the homeowner’s personality and life. However, “there is a difference between a room that feels ‘collected over time’ and one that feels inherited all at once,” designer Stephanie Kraus explains, and she’s tired of seeing this false presentation on display in people’s homes.

“Heavy Roman shades with lots of fabric, chintz on top of chintz on top of chintz, fringe on every edge, etc.,” she adds. “One of these details in a room can be charming, but if you use them all together, the room can start to feel like a period movie set.”

It’s often clear when this eccentric aesthetic is manufactured rather than effortless. Instead of trying to make the “ideal” interior in the span of a month, be patient and work on your home over a greater span of time. That way, the end product will look authentic.

Though curved sofas are massively popular right now and look gorgeous in a photograph, Kraus is ready to leave them in 2026. “A curved sofa rarely seats more than two people comfortably,” she says. “Once guests settle in, everyone ends up sitting awkwardly, and conversation feels harder than it should.”

She’s all for a sofa or couch being something that invites people in to linger over conversations and stay late into the night, and a kidney-shaped seat isn’t going to do that.

While designer Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios loved seeing the return of decorative, playful details like ruffles and scalloped edges, she thinks we’ve gone a bit overboard in 2026. These touches are being applied to just about everything: upholstery, pillows, lampshades, bedding, furniture—the list could go on.

“These details can be incredibly charming, but when they're repeated throughout an entire room, they begin to lose the very character that makes them special,” she says.

Rather than treat every piece of a room with one of these details, Vollrath suggests opting for a scalloped lampshade or a beautifully tailored ruffled pillow instead to still capture that charm without drowning the space in them.

“Limiting these details to one or two thoughtful applications allows them to feel intentional rather than overly thematic,” she adds. “Sometimes, the most interesting interiors are as much about what you choose to leave out as what you include.”

“I'd love to see us move away from selecting materials simply because they imitate the appearance of something more expensive,” Vollrath explains. This goes beyond marble-printed contact paper and burl wood veneer; the designer means on a larger scale, like mass-produced tile that’s made to replicate the imperfections of handmade zellige.

“Part of what makes authentic zellige so beautiful is the craftsmanship and natural variation that comes from its production,” she says. “When those qualities are artificially reproduced, I think we sometimes lose the very thing that made the original material special.”

She would rather see a greater appreciation for beautiful, honest materials at every price point, and if the exact thing a homeowner wants is out of budget, then her solution would be to pivot the idea altogether rather than find something cheaply made that looks like what they desire.

“A classic Carrara marble paired with beautifully considered cabinetry can create an incredibly sophisticated interior, even when something as dramatic as Calacatta Viola isn't within the budget,” Vollrath explains.

We could probably say this every year, but it bears repeating: Don’t solely follow trends! “Just because something is trending doesn't mean it's right for your home,” designer Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox says. “Polka dots are having a moment right now, but they don't make sense in many homes, or dare I say, most homes.”

He suggests thinking of home trends as prompts that should get you to brainstorm new ideas for your space that actually fit your style. Take polka dots again. If you want to introduce a new pattern to your home, but you don’t particularly mesh with polka dots, try a stripe instead.

“If you try to force something you don't love into your home just because it's trending, you're going to drive yourself crazy,” he adds. Let’s leave this line of thinking back in 2026 once and for all.

While we love a good built-in bookshelf and wooden ceiling beam, those types of architectural details don’t always make sense in every single home. In 2026, as personality-filled interiors became more popular for everyone, a lot of homeowners looked into adding this type of millwork to their spaces as a way to add more visual interest and texture.

“Decorative beams, arches, and elaborate millwork can feel forced when they don't make sense with the architecture of the home,” designer Dani Flax of Pearson Design Group argues. “I think there's a tendency to add these elements to make a space feel more interesting, but the result can actually take away from the architecture itself.”

A home in a modern farmhouse style, for example, would look a bit odd with an intricate ceiling medallion and excessive picture frame molding. Design your interior around the existing architecture and the style of your home, don’t incorporate these elements simply because they’re everywhere right now.

The next year is not about playing it safe with your interiors—designers are tired of the stark white home. While ubiquitous gray and white color schemes had their time, they can often make a home feel generic and devoid of character.

“In their place, we're embracing more intentional, tonal palettes and color drenching,” Mendoza says.

You can still have a mostly white house, of course, but take a little risk and mix in some pops of color and pattern here and there, or choose a beautiful neutral wood tone for your cabinetry instead of a bright white paint.

“Taking a considered color, whether it's a deep green, warm clay, or earthy mushroom, and carrying it across walls, trim, and cabinetry creates a cohesive, enveloping effect,” Mendoza adds. “The goal isn't necessarily to introduce more color, but to use it with greater confidence and purpose.”

This is definitely a hot take, but designer John Maloney McNamara of Coosa Interiors is ready to leave velvet upholstery behind. “I’ve tried, but velvet almost always reads as either a 1990s prom limo or a hotel lobby trying very hard,” he explains. “The sheen shows every crush and bruise, and it ages into something that looks tired rather than loved.”

If you love the look of velvet, he suggests going with a mohair fabric instead, saying it has all of velvet’s depth and none of its shine. It also only gets better with age. And though he knows creamy bouclé has been overdone, Maloney McNamara thinks it deserves a reprise, but only in a rich color that can replace velvet still.

#6716. Maple Bourbon Chicken With Brussels Sprouts

If you, like most people, hear the term “bourbon chicken” and have no idea what it is, let me call it by its more common name: toothpick chicken. Yes, the suspiciously delicious chicken shelled out at mall food courts on toothpicks is most likely bourbon chicken. Popular at American Chinese or Cajun-style Chinese restaurants, the sticky-sweet chicken is the utter definition of delicious, luring weary shoppers into food courts to find refuge from the bubblegum pink sweatpants and ripped denim. This version takes cues from the classic but swaps in some autumnal ingredients to make it fit for the season. Charred brussels sprouts leaves and pecans add bulk, while maple syrup and apple cider vinegar give it that delightful tart-sweet flavor we know and love. Serve it over rice or noodles or all by its lonesome for a hearty dinner that will take you back to the '90s.

More chicken dinner winners here: Sheet-Pan Hot Honey Chicken and Peaches; Basil Chicken Stir-Fry; and Ginger-Garlic Chicken and Vegetable Stir-Fry.

Whisk one 1½" piece ginger, peeled, finely grated, 3 garlic cloves, finely chopped, ⅓ cup bourbon, ⅓ cup pure maple syrup, ¼ cup apple cider vinegar, ¼ tsp. crushed red pepper flakes, 1 Tbsp. cornstarch, ¼ cup low-sodium soy sauce, and ½ cup water in a small bowl to combine. Set sauce aside.

#6706. Mussels With Garlicky Miso Broth

Mussels deserve your attention on weeknights. An affordable and accessible protein, they also happen to cook in less time than it takes to boil water. As the bivalves steam, they release a briny liquid, further perfuming the buttery, sake-fortified miso broth. The seafood counter at your supermarket should sell rinsed and debarred mussels, but it’s easy to do at home. Discard any with cracked shells. Gently tap open raw mussels on a counter to see if they slowly close; if they do, those are okay to use. Swish them in a water-filled salad spinner or colander fitted inside a bowl, draining and refilling until water runs clear (give any especially dirty ones a scrub with a sponge). Use a towel to pull off the fibrous beard and set on a towel-lined tray in the fridge until you’re ready to cook.

More seafood dinners this way: Avgolemono Seafood Stew; Spaghetti alle Vongole; and Gorgeous Green Shrimp.

Place 2 Tbsp. scallions from 8 scallions, thinly sliced on a diagonal, in a small bowl and set aside for serving.

Melt 6 Tbsp. unsalted butter in a large pot over medium heat. Add 8 garlic cloves, thinly sliced, and remaining scallions to pot and cook, stirring often, until softened, about 4 minutes. Add 2 Tbsp. white miso paste and ½ tsp. crushed red pepper flakes (if using) and cook until miso is broken up and incorporated into butter, about 20 seconds. Pour in 1 cup dry sake or dry white wine and stir to combine, then add 3–3½ lb. mussels, scrubbed, debearded. Cover pot and increase heat to medium-high. Cook, shaking pot occasionally, until mussels open, 5–7 minutes (discard any that do not open). Remove pot from heat.

Squeeze 1 lemon half over mussels. Cut remaining 1 lemon half into wedges for serving. Taste broth and season with kosher salt if needed.

#6690. You May Be Thawing Your Turkey Wrong—Here’s How To Avoid Spreading Salmonella

A frozen bird can save you money, but cutting corners could come at a cost.

Thanksgiving is a marathon, not a sprint. And the most involved, time-consuming dish is undoubtedly the turkey. The centerpiece of your holiday table needs hours in the oven and at least a day of brining, but don't forget the time it takes to defrost beforehand, too.

Frozen turkeys are generally the more accessible and affordable option. But they do require a little extra TLC and planning. So if you buy a frozen bird this year, you'll need to know how to work with it.

Making sure it tastes good is one thing, but ensuring that it's safe to eat is something else entirely. Like chicken, turkey can spread Salmonella and other harmful bacteria. You should exercise caution at every stage of the process, including thawing, to avoid making your guests sick. Here’s what you should know.

The USDA says it's also safe to defrost turkey in cold water, but in our opinion, it's not worth the hassle. The best bet is to thaw it in the refrigerator, where the bird can gradually defrost at the safe temperature with less risk of bacterial growth. What you should never do: thaw it on the counter, in the garage, or with warm water.

These environments are less controlled and can expose the turkey to the temperature danger zone (between 40°F and 140°F), where germs grow rapidly. Even if the center of the turkey is still frozen, the thawed exterior can become a hotbed for Salmonella by the time you're ready to roast.

Roasted turkey drippings make great gravy. But raw turkey juice dripping all over your fridge? Not so much. Even shrink-wrapped birds can leak, so you should always store your turkey on a baking sheet or roasting pan while it thaws.

Food safety experts also recommend storing raw meat on the lowest shelf. That way, any leaks are less likely to drip onto other ingredients in your fridge.

We know you're probably keeping a lot of other ingredients in your fridge, so it can be hard to set aside extra room for the turkey. That said, it's a step worth doing. Giving your turkey plenty of personal space minimizes the risk of raw meat or its juices coming into contact with other ingredients.

In the event that your turkey does contaminate another ingredient, here's what to know: Foods that won’t be cooked, like salad greens, should be tossed if they’ve come into contact with raw turkey juices. Cooking contaminated foods thoroughly can kill harmful bacteria, but you’ll also need to clean any surfaces the juices touched.

Turkeys are big, so they take a long time to thaw all the way through. The experts at Butterball say you should allocate a day for every four pounds of turkey if you're keeping it in the fridge. That means if you're working with a 16-pound bird, set aside four days just for defrosting. Once it's fully thawed, Butterball says your turkey will stay safe in the fridge for up to four days.

And on top of that, brining can make your turkey even more flavorful. If your frozen bird didn’t come pre-seasoned, consider setting aside another day or two for that step. (Want some guidance? Check out our brining guide.)

It’s extremely important to keep things clean while you’re handling a turkey. One of the most common ways Salmonella spreads in the kitchen is through cross-contamination caused by poor hygiene.

So wash those hands (including under your fingernails!) with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Make sure you also thoroughly clean any utensils and surfaces that come into contact with raw turkey or its juices using hot, soapy water.

And above all, don’t stress! Americans prepare millions of turkeys every Thanksgiving, so you're not alone in your turkey-roasting journey. Follow the tips above to ensure yours turns out safe to eat and delicious.

#6689. How to Improve Brain Health with 5 Daily Exercises

Brain health isn’t just an issue for the elderly. Maybe you've experienced the first subtle signs of decline. Sometimes it’s a name that’s on the tip of your tongue. Or perhaps you've read a news story in the morning that you can't remember by the evening. It could be that you're feeling increasingly mentally fatigued after a typical day spent dealing with emails, text messages, phone calls, and constant interruptions.

The good news is that you can take steps to keep your mind sharp. “Our brain needs to be stimulated, at any age," says Giuseppe Alfredo Iannoccari, a neuropsychologist, professor at the University of Milan, and president of Assomensana, an association founded in Monza, Italy in 2004 to promote cognitive health. Routines help conserve mental energy, while new experiences force it to work,” says Giuseppe Alfredo Iannoccari, a neuropsychologist, professor at the University of Milan. While working with people suffering from neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s, he came up with the idea to create a gym for the brain. “I asked myself what could be done earlier, when people are still healthy,” he said. His studies led him to develop a protocol that anyone can integrate into their daily routine.

The method is based on the principle of neuroplasticity—that is, the brain’s ability to modify its connections in response to learning and experience. This ability persists throughout one’s entire life, although it changes with age. Today, Iannocari's courses consist of weekly 90-minute sessions in groups of up to fifteen participants. Each session features about ten exercises focused on memory, attention, language, reasoning, creativity, and other cognitive skills. At the beginning and end of the program, twelve mental functions are assessed to measure their progress. “In our tests, we observe an average improvement in the overall cognitive efficiency index of 25–30%," says Iannoccari. "Some functions, such as attention and language, respond particularly well. Others, such as logical reasoning and prose memory, require more intensive work.” These results are based on the association’s experience and should not be interpreted as a percentage reduction in the risk of dementia. The World Health Organization’s guidelines, updated in 2026, acknowledge the potential benefits of cognitive training, while emphasizing that evidence regarding the long-term prevention of cognitive decline remains limited.

When it comes to our ability to concentrate, Iannoccari points to one culprit at the center of our modern lives. “Sometimes we feel tired not because we’ve done too many things, but because we’ve switched what we were doing too many times," he says. “Constantly switching from one task to another is more tiring than focusing on the same activity for an extended period.” A stream of notifications, information to process, and the need to respond quickly to various requests put our attention to the test above all else. “It’s the foundation of all cognitive processes. When it’s fragmented, even the quality of the information we’re able to acquire deteriorates.”

The Johns Hopkins professor recommends some tips you may know—and a few that might surprise you—to keep your brain firing on all cylinders.

And then there’s AI, which promises to free us from an ever-increasing number of tasks. What are the consequences for our brains? “If we ask artificial intelligence to compare two tables, perform an analysis, and write a report, we get the work done in a few minutes that would previously have taken an entire day," says Iannoccari. “Saving work also means reducing some mental activity. But it depends on how we use these tools.”

For Iannoccari, the key is to preserve two abilities: critical thinking and analysis. “We must know how to formulate questions, and this requires familiarity with the subject,” he says. “Then we must evaluate the answers, recognize any inconsistencies, and verify the information. Only a well-trained mind can spot errors.” This issue also applies to seemingly beneficial habits, such as reading books or solving crossword puzzles.“ Reading is invaluable, especially for the emotions it evokes and the worlds it allows us to discover," he says. “But reading doesn’t automatically mean memorizing. If we want to remember what we’ve read, we must do so with the intention of being able to recount it to someone else.” As for puzzle-solving pastimes, “Sudoku trains certain concentration and planning skills, while crossword puzzles stimulate the retrieval of information from memory,” he says. “These are useful activities, but a structured program allows you to work on different skills and gradually increase the difficulty of the exercises.”

In addition to guided sessions, Iannoccari suggests short daily sessions he calls “Brain Breaks”— five-minute breaks for small exercises that counteract brain aging. “If we wait to find five free minutes, we’ll probably never find them.” “We need to make it a habit, just like having a cup of coffee.” Here are five exercises you can try:

Choose a topic—preferably a positive one—read it carefully, and try to recount it to three different people without constantly referring back to the text. “The first time we repeat a piece of information, we’re often uncertain,” says Iannoccari. “The second time, we become clearer and more fluent. The third time, we have another chance to reinforce it in our memory.” It’s an exercise that stimulates what’s known as “prose memory”—the ability to recall and process information we’ve read or heard. “Let’s imagine learning one piece of news a day for 365 days: how much knowledge will we have accumulated?”

For five minutes, write down as many words as possible that start with the letter A, trying to be quick. Repeat the exercise the next day, avoiding words you’ve already used. On the third day, move on to B, and so on. “It’s a way to train verbal fluency and lexical retrieval, particularly useful when we start to experience that ‘word on the tip of my tongue’ phenomenon,” says Iannoccari. The goal is to gradually make the search more challenging by striving to find words that aren’t immediately obvious.

Right-handed people can try performing some simple tasks with their left hand, and vice versa. Open a drawer, move small objects, draw, and gradually try writing. “This new challenge forces the brain to break out of its automatic routines and engage resources we normally use less," he says. “Surprising results can be achieved: people who initially can’t even draw a line gradually learn to write with their other hand.” Of course, it’s best to choose safe activities and proceed without rushing.

How many phone numbers do we still remember? And how often do we rely on the GPS even to get to relatively familiar places? “Before calling someone, I try to remember their number," says Iannoccari. “Before using a calculator, I do the math in my head. Before turning on the GPS, I try to figure out the route.” The same goes for the grocery list. “I write it down so I can be sure I won’t forget anything, but at the supermarket I first try to recall the items. Only then do I check the list.” These are small opportunities to keep practicing skills that we increasingly tend to delegate to digital tools.

Once in bed, instead of focusing on the next day’s commitments, try to mentally retrace the day that just passed. What time did you wake up? What did you eat? Who did you meet? What did you talk about? What details stood out to us? “You have to try to be very detailed,” says Iannoccari. “Often, people who have trouble falling asleep find that this exercise also helps them drift off.”

But there’s another aspect that’s particularly close to his heart. “I find there’s something romantic about it. We live one day after another and often retain almost nothing of them. Reflecting on it means recognizing its value. Every day leaves something behind, like a geological layer deposited on top of the one before it.” This little ritual offers an opportunity to recall the experiences we wish to preserve.

When is the right time to start? “As children,” says Iannoccari. “We encourage them to play sports, but we rarely think about the importance of training mental abilities just as intentionally.” In adult life, of course, exercise, sleep, nutrition, stress management, and social relationships also matter. “But if I had to name three drivers of the mind, I’d choose curiosity, creativity, and emotions."he says. "Curiosity opens us up to new knowledge, creativity allows us to connect that knowledge in original ways, and emotions make experiences more memorable.”

He also notes that, though older age is associated with worse brain function, it's also how we collect the wisdom of lived experiences. “We think an efficient brain is the one we had at twenty, and we lose sight of what we’ve gained by the time we’re sixty or seventy," he says. "When we were young, we may have been faster, but over time we’ve accumulated skills, knowledge, and experience. We possess a much richer worldview.”

The real challenge is to remain present to ourselves and others, valuing who we’ve become.” And perhaps, as the years go by, one of the most important skills is precisely learning to choose where to invest our mental energy. “We meet people who, now that they’re retired, are busier than when they were working. We’ve come to associate efficiency with the number of things we can do, but in doing so, we lose the opportunity to delve deeper, reflect, and let experiences settle. I always say that I don’t pursue nice ideas—I pursue great ideas. Because there are so many nice ideas, and they risk scattering our energy in a thousand different directions.” “With age,” concludes Iannoccari, “we have a great privilege: the ability to choose.”

If you didn’t know better, you’d be rooting for these two off-putting lovebirds to make a go of it.

Boyle’s performance mesmerizes. He seems to have control over his lashes, so completely articulated is his eye acting. His whole posture changes as he takes up different roles in the story: a curious loser, a gifted rhetorician, an anxious boss, an ignored former lover. The show shifts from alienation in New York to crypto adventures in Hong Kong to the office-dorm hybrid of “The Orchid” in the Bahamas. Boyle owns the physical transformations, letting the glee of the Hong Kong era fester into the eyebags and stress-weight accrued (probably one of the more ethical uses of a bodysuit I can recall) in the scheme’s last days.

Garner, meanwhile, has entered rarified air as a TV actor. Over the course of her career she has given us a moody, adrift Gen X student (Kimmy on The Americans), a Millennial lady of rage trapped in her dumpy hometown (Ozark), and now two glimpses of the self-satisfied Zillenial scammer (Anna Delvey in Inventing Anna and The Altruists). Garner’s signature move as a performer is to fail to sublimate anger or distress. Her characters have their own idiosyncratic patterns of twitches and attempts at steely gazes. The brittle darkness inside each of them sings. Her TV career belongs next to those of Elisabeth Moss and America Ferrera and Walton Goggins. It is time that this was generally acknowledged.

The supporting cast is solid, with one particular highlight. In the middle of The Altruist’s eight-episode run, we get an entire episode focused on Sam Trabucco, a college bestie of SBF, a former Alameda Research co-CEO, and an early cooperator with federal prosecutors once the wheels started falling off.

Alex Lawther plays Trabucco and almost walks off with the show. Lawther’s slight frame and self-searching candor give each line of dialogue intimacy. I first saw Lawther on Andor, playing Nemik, idealistic proto-Rebel and truest of true believers, a character based on Lei Feng, the Chinese communist folk hero and soldier, who, like Nemik on Andor, died in a freak accident but left behind a diary of such pure faith that it became a recruitment tool for the revolution. (A recording of Lawther reading that manifesto from his diary—his voice reedy and prophetic—literally haunts Imperial high command at the end of Andor.)

In maybe my favorite scene of The Altruists, Trabucco and Ellison get dinner together. They talk and realize their childhood paths intersected and diverged across metro Boston—the same math competitions, the same tests. But their hometowns were quite different. She was from Newton. He was from Natick. If you’re from New England, that’s the only detail you need. (If you aren’t, I recommend Love Story and/or the novels of Dennis Lehane for background.) At that dinner, Lawther shares a deep, specific childhood memory of his father and Garner’s Caroline flinches without breaking eye contact, like she’s watching a recording of a natural disaster.

Sadly, the Netflix sins are here in full effect. The lighting is smeary and wildly off in many scenes. The music and sound mix could hardly be blunter. Even Oscar-nominated showrunner Graham Moore and talented directors like James Ponsoldt can’t lift dialogue that not-infrequently feels tailored to second-screen viewers: “They’re both off-the-charts math geniuses!”

The braided narrative can turn single episodes into distracting puzzle boxes. The form is designed to function the way a big juicy 10,000-word legacy-media reported feature does: You know the ending, so it takes a memorable reshuffling of moments and incidents to create something new.

But watching this gang of true believers and opportunists and criminally naïve teenage dream brains go from geographic zone to zone does carry emotional import. To them, and to The Altruists, the Bay Area has become a dated country club, a mirror image of The Social Network’s version of Harvard from the 2000s, back when the Bay Area promised something fresh. Post-Recession New York is greyscale normie central. Hong Kong and Macau blur together. The show ends in the Bahamas, a place that rich outsiders have always treated as both Eden and the afterlife.

I think the era of the tech-bro-scammer-titan show and movie is reaching its end. If its dawn was The Social Network, the unrepeatable, mesmerizing You Can See Everything might be the sunset. Gen X icons Aaron Sorkin and David Fincher once tried to get their heads around millennial narcissism and now millennial genius Nathan Fielder has transformed his time with a real-deal apex-predator narcissist into a Möbius strip of content.

The drama of The Altruists depends on the psychology of the people who believe in crypto as a kind of syncretic faith. The show avoids a technical delineation of crypto itself, which makes sense. (I’ve yet to encounter a crypto evangelist who can provide a transparent explanation of the asset class that is superior to this.) ExxonMobil sells you a product and it works. Glock sells you a product and it works. Crypto works in so much as its existence justifies its existence. There are no earnings reports. Value is vibes.

Whenever something is being marketed as the future, it’s usually just the past, accelerated. People made crypto into an alternative financial system because they were “outsiders” who wanted to “disrupt.” Now the crypto system wants to be held close to the bosom of the enduring US financial markets and bulge bracket banks. A bill that would have brought them closer, setting up a regulatory framework, nose-dived in the senate.

It sounds almost cowardly, like running home to mom and dad: We made a brave new thing better and cooler and freer than your corrupt old monetary system. A few years later: Let us back in and give us all the benefits of the old system! Get us in your PNC digital wallet, normie!

Bankman-Fried and Ellison are both the children of elite academics. To its credit, The Altruists pokes these Freudian pains. Bankman-Fried was raised by Stanford Law professors and chose MIT. Ellison was the child of MIT academics and chose Stanford. There’s self-belief in those exact choices. The children of faculty get a significant leg up in gaining admission at their parents’ institution. SBF and Ellison wanted to do something honest on their own, at least once.

My parents are retired professors. You learn early on that you cannot possibly replicate your parents’ careers. A child of a lawyer can have their parent’s career 2.0. Same if you have doctors for parents. But truly successful academics—and given the market today, you either are truly successful or you don’t exist—create niches built only for one.

Successful academics eschew any number of linear career paths and lucrative fields to burrow into zones of learning where they have to be singular, own every network and endure any marginal post (Oregon State, thou hast thy music, too). To make it to a top institution, you need luck and power and timing to dovetail your own research record and professional charisma. Many people make it to powerful banks and hospitals and law firms. But if you manage to get tenure at Berkeley, you might really be that special.

The track record of the children of academics I know is messy: deluded guys floating around Portland or Boston trying to jam their unreadable YA series into the world, an acquaintance who self-sabotaged a promising, morally-clear career as a social worker because they did not get a doctorate and let that arbitrary marker of value smother their self-worth for decades.

SBF and Ellison were expected to perform precocity at all times, but the minute that they stumbled, Dr. Mom or Professor Dad stepped in with the better therapists and better lawyers to set everything right. The last two episodes of The Altruists bubble over with dread. Paul Reiser and Robin Weigert play SBF’s parents, and Jennifer Grey plays Caroline Ellison’s mom. Each performance puts its own spin on enmeshment and infantilization. The parents are not the big bads here. But they are the judgmental elitists who analyzed when they should have listened, and who raised these world-class scammers.

There was a guy a little like me in high school. Like me, he was the child of academics and teachers. Like me, he was clever and capable, which might have been enough in another high school. But such minor skills were door prizes in our grand, old, dizzyingly competitive boarding school. We both loved De La Soul. We were both cut from the same teams. We were both contrarians who trafficked in equal measures of insight and anserine bullshit. I won’t pretend to have been good friends with the guy—it was the classic situation where young men who play similar roles in their respective circles repel each other like the same sides of different magnets. But I sympathized. It’s painful to know exactly why you are not near the top of an elite ecosystem and then having to go on living in it.

I imagined he might have a career adjacent to mine. I imagined that we might see each other in the late 2000s at a Babygrande Records release party or in the bathroom at Santos Party House. It did not play out that way. A few years ago, during the inevitable age-related transition from Facebook to LinkedIn, the guy’s face and name swum across my screen. He’s become a big deal in the crypto space. At one point, he rapped about crypto on crypto podcasts. On a class-wide group chat, as former classmates who had chosen to work in community health settings or on federally-funded research projects shared their pain after losing their jobs in the DOGE cuts, and as they voiced their concerns about what other digits of the American experiment would be severed next, this crypto dude commandeered the chat to brag about taking his company public. No child of an academic has ever missed a chance to make it about them.

One of the side effects of the world-gobbling narratives around A.I. (which gets its own Social Network moment in Luca Guadagnino’s imminent Artificial, with Andrew Garfield as Sam Altman) is that crypto already feels cringe—at best, a chrome decoration on the world-shaking machinery of LLMs and Deep Learning. The brave new world of private money that needed no society and no army behind it has become just another widget. The most vulgar people in the world trade meme coins with Turkish billionaire failsons. That Uber driver for whom you had great sympathy waits forever for a crypto ship that will never come into port. (Bankman-Fried, as it happens, was an early investor in Anthropic; it was reported last week that his shares, liquidated during FTX's bankruptcy, would be worth $35 to $37 billion in the wake of the AI lab's splashy IPO.)

If 2026 is the close of the tech-movie and tech-show era, then how do we square The Altruists? It has arrived at the end of something. It’s not perfect, maybe not even excellent, but it is effective and memorable, and provides a showcase for superb young actors. I wonder how many of these shows about tech founders, disruptors, optimists, hustlers and misanthropes will last. The paranoia of 70s American film evaporated in the desert of 80s escapism and the wry smirk of Ronnie Reagan. The thorny, violent prestige TV era begat the soft-clothes forever-childhood of Stranger Things and of Ted Lasso. Legacy cannot be legislated in advance. I hope viewers check out The Altruists, and appreciate its gumption in allowing love—the least efficient of emotions—to animate the story.

But maybe I’m wrong. Maybe I’ve been hustled by Boyle and Garner’s wonderful acting. Maybe my almost-instinct about the past 15 years of watching damaged young ladder-climbers and their digital dreams on screen is almost true: what will survive of us is coins.

A few helpful notes for pasta success: (1) While any pasta will work, egg-based noodles, like the fettuccine I recommend here, are the best complement to the sauce. The nests (Rummo and De Cecco are great accessible brands) cook far faster than standard straight pasta, almost as fast as fresh. (2) Don’t be afraid to season your mushrooms! Yes, I know this goes against all that we’ve learned about salting them too early in the cooking process, but just a bit of salt will get them going and releasing their water faster for eventual crisp-browned edges. Don’t be alarmed if you see a pool of water at the start of the mushroom cooking—that’s to be expected and just keep going until it evaporates. (3) Don’t forget to reserve plenty of pasta cooking liquid, which is critical to the eventual sauce’s success. I prefer transferring the al dente noodles from the pasta pot to the skillet, letting any residual water come with it (and to ensure you don’t accidentally drain away that liquid gold).

Cook 12 oz. egg-based pasta (such as fettuccine nests) in a large pot of boiling salted water, stirring occasionally, until al dente, about 1 minute less than package directions.

Meanwhile, heat 2 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil in a large skillet over high. Add 1 lb. crimini or mixed mushrooms (such as button, maitake, oyster, and/or shiitake), sliced ¼" thick or torn into bite-size pieces, and ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Remove pan from heat. Add 1 oz. Parmesan, finely grated (about ½ cup) to pasta and toss to combine. Season generously with more pepper; taste and season with more salt if needed.

Divide pasta among shallow bowls and top with more Parmesan and pepper.

It can be satisfying for a moment to pick up the first pretty thing you see, but a crash follows the rush and before you know it, you’ve got a pile of sweaters that are either too warm, too itchy, or just don’t fit right. Don’t make that mistake, here’s what to look out for.

Cotton: We’re not going to belabor the benefits of cotton—the natural fiber is breathable, affordable, washes easily (though careful with the dryer—always read that tag), and gentle on sensitive skin. You can’t go wrong with a cotton sweater any time of the year, but if you’re looking for a transitional layer that’ll keep you warm now and comfortably cool later, it's hard to top cotton.

Wool: Not to mix our species, but wool can be a chameleon. The sheep-borne spectrum runs from airy and light to thick and hefty. But all of it is uniquely adept at regulating temperatures, so you'll stay warm without burning up. While you have to be a little more delicate on the upkeep (almost definitely no dryer), your tough-as-nails wool knit can last you for decades—literally—with some conscientious upkeep.

Cashmere: It used to be that the average “nice” wool sweater was made of merino wool or lambswool, and cashmere was reserved for the rich guys. A few decades of commodifying the luxury fiber has helped bring it to more affordable tiers. Though careful what you pay for: cheap cashmere pills easily, can feel oily, and won't last nearly as long as the good stuff.

Mohair: Shaggier and a hair (sorry) lighter than wool, a mohair sweater isn't afraid to make a statement. Chances are you’ve seen NBA elites sporting the ubiquitous horizontal-striped Marni cardigan, or you've spotted a number of Needle’s eyeball-popping patterned versions out in the wild. Though it's worth pointing out, you can absolutely find more relaxed versions—in both cardigan and button-free variations—if you're not trying to flex for ESPN's cameras.

Synthetics: More often than not, you’ll encounter sweaters that blend man-made materials like polyester, acrylic, and elastane. These materials can help give a sweater desirable qualities like softness, moisture-wicking, insulation, and stretch. They’re also cheaper to produce. While that doesn’t always translate into a low-quality product, it does mean that a brand is cutting costs which often result in an inferior garment.

Beyond the material that’s used to make a sweater, the yarns and gauge of those yarns influence light or heavy the final product will be. The fibers are spun into yarns, which are then knit together to form a sweater. The gauge refers to the thickness of those yarns and can be the difference between an airy spring sweater and a chunky deep-winter cardigan. The thicker the gauge, the heavier and warmer the sweater will be.

The balance between material and fabric weight is a delicate one. So when you’re deciding on a sweater, remember that a heavy cotton sweater can potentially be just as warm as a thin merino wool sweater or that a densely knit cashmere sweater will be warmer than an equally chunky cotton alternative.

Aside from the rarer summer knitwear, sweaters are made for cold weather. So fit is especially important considering you’ll be layering your sweater over and under other garments. Consider whether you’ll be wearing it over a button-up and tie or just a simple T-shirt. For the slightly reckless, slightly sexier among us, also consider whether you’ll be wearing it without any undershirt at all.

Same goes for the outer layers that’ll go on top. A chunky sweater is satisfying and cozy as hell, but can easily become unwieldy if you’re trying to stuff it under a tailored blazer. When it comes to fit, there’s no single best silhouette, but there are a couple of rules of thumb if you can’t make heads or tails of it. Make sure the shoulder seams hit just past your shoulder bones, that the sleeves reach to your wrist bones, and that the hem sits right around your hips.

#6661. Broiled Salmon Teriyaki

Sometimes all it takes to make dinner feel special is a big, bold piece of protein. Case in point: This salty-sweet salmon glazed in a glossy teriyaki-style sauce. Brushed in two rounds with the quick sauce made with nothing more than sugar, soy sauce, sesame oil, and vinegar, the salmon sizzles under the broiler, charring as it takes on a smoky flavor. This recipe calls for one large piece of salmon, but you can use smaller fillets or opt for a white fish such as cod or halibut. Just note that the cooking time will be shorter if using smaller pieces, so keep an eye on them. Add a quick garnish of sliced scallions and toasted sesame seeds, and dinner is done.

More salmon winners this way: Hot Honey-Glazed Salmon; Green Curry-Glazed Salmon; and Slow-Roasted Salmon With Harissa.

Bring ⅓ cup (packed; 67 g) dark brown sugar, ¼ cup soy sauce, 2 Tbsp. unseasoned rice vinegar, and 2 tsp. toasted sesame oil to a simmer in a small saucepan over medium-high heat; cook until reduced by half and syrupy, 5–7 minutes. Let sauce cool until thickened slightly, about 10 minutes.

Heat broiler (if broiler is in your oven, place rack in upper third). Season both sides of one 1½–2-lb. skinless or skin-on center-cut salmon fillet with kosher salt. Place, skin side down, in a skillet or baking dish. Transfer one third of sauce to a small bowl and set aside for serving. Brush about half of remaining sauce over fillet. (If the salmon has a lot of room around it in the pan, line with foil to prevent runoff sauce from burning onto pan.)

Broil salmon until sauce is just starting to burn in spots, 4–7 minutes. Brush remaining sauce over fillet and broil until deeply charred and salmon is opaque all the way through, about 5 minutes.

Remove fish from oven and spoon reserved sauce over. Top with thinly sliced scallions and/or toasted sesame seeds if desired.
