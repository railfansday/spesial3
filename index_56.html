#147. Nigerian youth have shown their power in the past - why aren't they shaping this election?

Bola Tinubu (in red hat) would be 79 at the end of a second term, if he is re-elected

Abdulhadi Dalhatu is something of a rarity in Nigeria politics.

At 38, he is among the young people seeking elected office in a country where about 80% of the population is under 40.

He hopes his age will stand in his favour as he campaigns for a seat in the state assembly in the northern Kano state in January's general elections.

"Younger people are the majority in this country and should be deciding the future," Dalhatu tells the BBC.

But Nigeria's political history suggests he faces an uphill battle.

Since the return to civilian rule in 1999, Goodluck Jonathan has been the youngest person elected president, taking office at the age of 53.

The current president, Bola Tinubu, is 74 and would be 79 by the end of a second term if he is re-elected in January.

His main challengers are also considerably older. Atiku Abubakar, seeking the presidency for the seventh time, is 79, while Peter Obi is 65.

Of the 18 presidential candidates, only one is under 40 - Moses Olusoji Adebisi, a 35-year-old businessman representing the Democratic Leadership Alliance (DLA).

Although he says he is confident of winning, few Nigerians appear to know much about him and he has not been seen much on the campaign trail.

The contrast is striking in a country with one of the world's youngest populations, where young people have repeatedly demonstrated their ability to shape political debate and mobilise for change.

In the previous election, Obi, then 61, galvanised millions of young Nigerians through a social media-driven movement known as the "Obidients".

His campaign promised a break from the established political order, appealing particularly to young people struggling to find jobs and earn a decent living.

But his failure to win the presidency left many of his supporters disillusioned.

"At the time we were so hopeful, but unfortunately the result wasn't what we wanted, which made many lose hope in the system," former Obedient Nonso Chukwudi tells the BBC.

The movement had built on the momentum of the EndSars protests in 2020, which grew into one of Nigeria's biggest youth-led movement in decades.

Initially sparked by anger over police brutality, the demonstrators developed into a wider call for political reform, accountability and greater youth participation in governance.

But the protests were eventually crushed by the authorities. About 56 were reportedly killed, although officials dispute this figure, while hundreds were arrested and some had their bank accounts frozen.

Both EndSars and the Obedients demonstrated the capacity of young Nigerians to mobilise around political causes.

Abdulhadi Dalhatu hopes to be president one day but bemoans the financial obstacles needed to contest

But turning that enthusiasm into sustained electoral participation has proved much harder.

Hajara Musa, 33, is among those who have lost faith in the political system. She has no plans to register or vote in the near future.

"I just feel like things are already determined and I'll just be wasting my time. It is going to be the same people all over again," she tells the BBC.

The electoral register appears to reflect this growing disillusionment.

Although the number of registered voters has increased by 10 million to over 103 million since the last election, enrolment of those under 35 has actually fallen, by about seven million.

Young people now account for just 29% of registered voters, down from 40% in 2023.

Nigeria's electoral commission, Inec, says it has made efforts to encourage more young people to register ahead of the 2027 general elections.

This includes appointing nine ambassadors from the entertainment industry, spanning film, music and comedy, to reach younger audiences.

But the figures suggest these efforts have yet to make a significant difference.

Dalhatu says he understands the frustration but argues that young Nigerians cannot expect change, if they stay away from the political process.

"Unfortunately, many prefer to just post on social media" instead of getting involved, he says.

Of course being young does not mean you are necessarily the best candidate.

For those who want to participate, there is a formidable obstacle to getting into politics - money.

Dalhatu dreams of becoming president one day, but says he has previously had to abandon campaigns because he could not raise enough funds.

The cost of running for office in Nigeria is notoriously high.

Under the Electoral Act, presidential candidates were allowed to spend up to 5bn naira (Â£2.8m; $3.7m) on campaigns ahead of the 2023 elections. That spending limit has since been doubled.

The cost of nomination forms alone puts the presidency beyond the reach of most aspiring young Nigerians.

The ruling All Progressive Congress (APC), along with the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) and the African Democratic Congress (ADC) have each set their presidential nomination fee at 100m naira ($74,000; Â£56,000), making it extremely hard for new challengers to emerge.

Since the return to democracy in 1999, only two parties have governed Nigeria - the PDP from 1999 to 2015 and the APC since 2015.

These parties, alongside the ADC, formed last year by politicians who had defected from the other two, have established nationwide network of branches, supporters and campaigners.

Those aspiring candidates without the financial resources to secure nomination forms and compete in the internal party contests of the main parties have little chances of becoming president.

The barriers are also evident in the National Assembly.

Not one of Nigeria's 109 senators is under 40, while only 14 of the 360 members of the House of Representatives fall into that age group.

"I have been in politics for many years and I will tell you that young people in this country lack the resources to become president," says Dalhatu, who is running under Obi's Nigeria Democratic Congress.

Atiku Abubakar is seeking the presidency for a seventh time

Fatima Zahra Umar, a young politician in the APC who has previously tried to run for a seat in the House of Representatives, agrees.

"Young Nigerians are poor. Nigerian politics is cash-intensive, so participation is guided by money," she says, adding that young people are often content with having a bit of influence as a special advisor to a senior politician.

But political analyst Seun Ajayi believes the problems go beyond the financial barriers.

"We have some of the most brilliant young persons in the world, but they are either not interested in politics or feel satisfied when given a small role in government," he says.

"The old men are not going to sit idle and allow us to push them out. We have to fight for the big positions, but at the moment I don't see the zeal."

Umar believes changing attitudes towards young leadership is equally important.

"Culturally, we are not primed to trust or accept the brilliance of a younger person, so it will be very difficult," she says.

"Old men are still holding on to the system."

Some Nigerians took inspiration from the wave of success of young candidates in the recent midterm elections in the US, some of whom have roots in Africa.

"America Has Done It, Can Nigerians Do the Same?" entertainer Charly Boy wrote on X, in a post that was widely shared.

He said the US results served as a warning to Nigerian politicians that no matter how powerful the political machine, voters can change the direction of government.

He called on young people to interrogate candidates about their past promises: "Has life improved? Where are the jobs, security and affordable living?

"Democracy is not a spectator sport, nor is it about sharing rice," he wrote.

#144. Five takeaways from the failed climate talks COP31

The COP31 summit in Turkey came as evidence of climate change could not have been clearer - 2026 brought glacier collapses, record European heat, and wildfires across Asia - but also when geopolitical divisions could not have been deeper.

The result was the longest-running climate talks in UN history failed to deliver on its central political agreement â the Antalya Pacts - to further climate action.

Despite two countries, Australia and Turkey, leading the charge in their joint hosting of the negotiations, they could not bring the nations together.

After a dramatic and fractious final day where countries walked out over the absence of mention of fossil fuels in the text and money issues, questions are being asked more loudly than ever before about whether the COP process can still deliver.

Here are five, key takeaways from the talks which the hosts hoped would become known as the "implementation COP".

This was the first COP since the UN warned that global temperatures are expected to exceed 1.5C degrees above pre-industrial levels. That is the threshold which countries agreed not to breach as part of the Paris Climate Agreement signed in 2015.

The draft agreement had acknowledged the uncomfortable reality that climate action to date has failed on its most crucial of targets.

Without agreement, that recognition has been lost.

Almost exactly a decade ago a group of the world's climate scientists - the official UN body the IPCC - warned what would happen if temperatures rose 1.5C vs 2C:

Sea level rise would be 10cm higher - that would expose 10 million more people to flooding, external

The ocean would become more acidic risking irreversible damage to some marine life

Extreme heat - that's hot days and nights - in every inhabited part of the world

In a 2C world extreme heat is expected to be experienced in every inhabitated area on Earth

Throughout the talks and during the final day, many countries still acknowledged that the target remained their north star of climate action.

"This COP sought to confront hard truths â that global warming is set to overshoot 1.5C and that we must act with even more urgency to reduce emissions," said UK Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh at the close of the talks.

The draft had called for that overshoot to be kept as small and short-lived as possible â it also suggested something called carbon dioxide removal (CDR).

This is a process which takes greenhouse gases out of the atmosphere and has not been explicitly mentioned in a COP final agreement before.

Carbon dioxide can be removed naturally by planting trees - although there are limits to the scale and permanence of that.

There are other technological solutions, but these are controversial, external and are not well advanced.

This year's negotiations have exposed a troubling problem - consensus does not last forever.

On the central political agreement â the Antalya Pacts â countries could not bridge the divide on including language on fossil fuels.

And yet the very same pledge had been made just three years ago at COP28 in Dubai.

Rich nations also failed to put a figure to how much more money could be given to developing countries to adapt to climate change.

This is despite there already being a commitment from last year to triple the figure by 2035 from $40bn (Â£30bn) - but the interpretation of that pledge remains contested.

If governments cannot even agree to reaffirm commitments they've already made, it raises troubling questions about their ability to work together as the climate crisis intensifies.

Minister Chris Bowen from Australia, who was co-leading the negotiations, pointed out at the end of the talks that the world is a very different place from when these commitments were struck just a few years ago.

"The situation coming into this COP was the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory".

That was made apparent during the final meeting when pointed remarks were delivered. EU representative Darragh O'Brien said the talks had been "hijacked by some oil states".

Russian delegate Sergei Kononuchenko switched from Russian to English to directly address the EU delegation. He accused them of providing climate finance only in the form of loans to developing nations: "This is an old colonial distribution of labour under a new banner".

It is a serious blow not just to climate action, but to multilateralism itself - the idea that countries can put aside their differences on other issues to tackle problems they can't solve alone.

Much of the coverage of COP31 has focused on the failure to get the central political agreement passed.

But much of the work at these summits is putting into action agreements made at previous meetings.

On money that is going towards adaptation for example, countries need to agree how is it being distributed, is it making an impact and how can this be tracked, and which areas are particularly vulnerable.

In fact, there were 164 other items on this year's agenda that were being thrashed out in the negotiating rooms.

And some of those were agreed at the talks.

The funds raised from a global system of carbon credits â money given in exchange for releasing greenhouse gases â will go towards helping developing countries adapt to the impacts of climate change.

They agreed to advance a just transition framework designed to protect workers and communities as global economies shift toward clean energy.

But there were many more that could not be agreed and some will now be picked up at COP32, including crucially the work programme responsible for driving forward action on cutting emissions.

What governments agree in the COP negotiating rooms is only part of what determines the speed of the energy transition.

The International Energy Agency's latest World Energy Outlook, external points to another powerful force: energy security.

It says the disruption to oil and gas supplies through the Strait of Hormuz has been the biggest energy supply shock in history. The longer the uncertainty continues, the stronger the incentive for countries to switch from imported fossil fuels to electricity generated at home.

Solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles have become increasingly competitive.

As clean energy develops its own commercial momentum, formal negotiations may become less important in determining its direction and pace.

Against the headwinds of inflation new investment announcements emerged at COP as part of the "Action Agenda".

This is the part of the COP process that tries to mobilise action outside of formal talks from businesses, investors and cities â this year the focus was on electrification. Turning more of our energy from fossil fuels to electricity.

"We are not entering the age of electricity, we have arrived at the age of electricity," Fatih Birol, head of the IEA, told the BBC. He says energy security and climate policies are now "closely aligned".

Governments still have a crucial role in facilitating the transition, according to Dr Jennie Dodson, a senior director at the World Business Council for Sustainable Development which includes among its members the likes of Amazon and Apple.

"A key message from business is we can go so far, a lot of it is cost competitive, but there are policy barriers both around infrastructure and permitting".

Many are questioning whether the COP process is fit for purpose

COP31 has shown how the role of these annual UN climate conferences is changing.

For the first 30 COPs, the emphasis was on building the international framework for tackling global warming.

The great diplomatic breakthrough came in Paris in 2015, when almost every country agreed to keep global warming well below 2Â°C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5Â°C.

Paris also established machinery designed to turn promises into progress. Every five years, governments assess collective progress in a global stocktake and submit new national climate plans, known as NDCs.

Then came further agreements: a fund for climate damage at COP27, a commitment to transition away from fossil fuels and triple renewable energy capacity at COP28, a new climate finance target at COP29 and increased support for adaptation at COP30.

The international architecture for tackling climate change has, in other words, largely been built.

Now comes the harder business of making it work. That's why this year's conference in Antalya, Turkey, had been billed as an "implementation COP".

The COP summit in Turkey has failed because of a row about even mentioning the cause of climate change. Is there a way the world can move forward with coordinated climate action?

The emphasis now is shifting towards delivering commitments already made. It is not a requirement to have one, and for many years countries managed without. Australia chose to push one for this COP, and it is where they fell down.

On many of the procedural issues Camilla Born spoke of, they did agree things. That may be important. It isn't necessarily exciting.

And there is a fundamental limit to what this machinery can achieve. Under the Paris Agreement countries have no mechanism to force them to meet their emissions targets.

So, when critics berate the COP process for failing to tackle the problem fast enough, it isn't really the system that is at fault, says Alden Meyer, a veteran climate negotiator now at the climate think tank E3G.

The COP process, he suggests, is "like a mirror that we hold up to ourselves every year". If the reflection is disappointing, the problem is the governments, not the mirror.

#143. North Macedonia remains stalled on its EU path after 21 years as a candidate

EU enlargement has regained momentum, with Montenegro and Albania moving closer to membership. But North Macedonia, a candidate since 2005, remains stalled by a politically difficult dispute involving Bulgaria.

The European Union's enlargement drive has regained momentum: Montenegro could become the bloc's 28th member in 2028, while Albania is rapidly advancing through accession talks and could complete accession negotiations by the end of 2027 if it maintains its current pace of reforms.

But North Macedonia, an EU candidate since 2005, remains stalled on the road to accession.

That contrast will be particularly evident at Wednesday's EU-Western Balkans summit in Brussels.

"The Western Balkans' future is in the European Union. Their accession is our priority, our shared ambition and a crucial geopolitical investment in these turbulent times," European Council President Antonio Costa said ahead of the meeting.

North Macedonia formally opened accession talks with the EU in July 2022 but has yet to open the first of the EU's six negotiating clusters, the groups of policy areas through which candidates align their laws and institutions with EU standards.

But the lack of movement does not mean the country is unprepared for membership.

EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos said in November that North Macedonia was "among the countries with the highest level of preparedness" in aligning with EU law and had recently accelerated reforms under the EU Growth Plan.

However, she added, "the handbrake on its accession negotiations is still on."

Kos said that to release this handbrake and open the first cluster, North Macedonia must adopt the constitutional amendment agreed with the EU in 2022.

For years, a dispute with Greece over the country's name blocked North Macedonia's progress along the EU accession path.

Although the 2018 Prespa Agreement resolved the dispute, and the country changed its constitutional name, clearing the way for NATO membership, a breakthrough on EU accession did not follow.

Bulgaria later blocked the process amid disputes over history, language and identity. A compromise brokered during the French EU presidency in 2022 lifted the veto but made further progress conditional on North Macedonia amending its constitution to explicitly include Bulgarians among the country's recognized communities.

The amendment requires a two-thirds majority in parliament, which has so far proved unattainable.

From the EU's perspective, this is no longer a Bulgarian veto, but a condition North Macedonia accepted as part of its negotiating framework.

"It [North Macedonia] is not blocked by a member state. It is blocked because it doesn't want to date to do what it knows needs to be done," Gert Jan Koopman, the European Commission's director-general for Neighbourhood and Enlargement Negotiations, recently told an Atlantic Council podcast.

North Macedonia's government sees the problem differently.

EU membership remains its declared strategic goal, but Skopje wants guarantees that amending its constitution will not simply pave the way for further demands from Sofia.

Those concerns are rooted partly in long-running disputes over identity and history: Bulgarian governments have challenged North Macedonia's interpretation of a centuries-old distinct Macedonian identity, while some Bulgarian politicians have gone further, describing Macedonian as a regional variant of Bulgarian, and Macedonians and Bulgarians as one people.

President Gordana Siljanovska-Davkova says North Macedonia is ready for talks with Bulgaria and the EU but wants certainty that one constitutional change will not simply be followed by demands for another.

"We only want certainty. We only want this not to be yet another amendment for the sake of an amendment, followed by another one," she said.

After more than two decades of waiting, the deadlock has also become a domestic political problem in North Macedonia.

A nationally representative survey by the Institute for Democracy "Societas Civilis" illustrates the contradiction: 71% of respondents support EU membership, but only 24% back the constitutional amendments needed to advance the process. Among ethnic Macedonians, support for the amendments falls to just 11%.

Skopje-based political analyst Petar Arsovski describes the public mood as "passive-aggressive:" Strong support for joining the EU coexists with weak support for making the concession currently required to move forward.

That, he says, helps preserve the status quo.

The dispute is now deeply embedded in domestic politics in both North Macedonia and Bulgaria, where retreat from established positions could easily be portrayed as a national defeat.

But long-standing economic strain, along with August's devastating flash floods and the Nepalese public's high hopes for more jobs, investment and better governance at home, have made Shah's first bilateral visit abroad more than a mere diplomatic box-ticking exercise.

To safeguard Nepal's flood recovery and development, Shah needs to get off on the right foot with India, his country's southern neighbor and largest trading partner, and its long-standing prime minister, Narendra Modi.

While Nepal and India share an open border as well as deep cultural and historical ties, their relationship has also been marked by territorial disputes and a desire in Nepal to be seen as an equal by its much larger and more powerful neighbor.

Constantino Xavier, a senior fellow at the Center for Social and Economic Progress in New Delhi, however, cautioned against expecting too much from a single visit.

"We need a big, long-term ambition coupled with small, short-term steps," he told DW, arguing that economic cooperation should remain the central focus of relations because it directly contributes to the socioeconomic development of both countries.

The young prime minister's test is to turn a strong domestic electoral mandate and political goodwill with New Delhi into tangible economic gain, as well as to address longstanding border disputes and a massive trade imbalance.

Shah's administration sees diplomacy as key to addressing the country's development aspirations, which was reflected in bilateral talks with Modi on Monday.

Economic cooperation featured prominently during the talks as both sides sought engagements in trade, investment, connectivity and infrastructure development.

Kathmandu's priority was securing Indian financial support for reconstruction following the August disaster that claimed the lives of more than 1,450 people, while 5,745 are missing or unaccounted for.

The South Asian neighbors also agreed to deepen cooperation across a wide range of areas, including trade, investment, connectivity and people-to-people ties, and launched a new long-term vision aimed at strengthening economic integration and development.

The talks also yielded commitments to broaden educational and innovation partnerships, boost energy and infrastructure cooperation, improve market access and trade links, and enhance landlocked Nepal's connectivity through additional air and port access via India.

Shah's visit offered an opportunity to break the ice in a high-level exchange visit and improve a relationship periodically strained by political mistrust, territorial disputes and differing strategic interests.

Nepal and India both have competing territorial claims over Kalapani, Lipulekh and Limpiyadhura, a strategic Himalayan territory in northwestern Nepal.

As mayor of Kathmandu, Shah raised the border disputes with India, which helped boost his nationalist image and set his broader political agenda. However, his India visit steered clear of such contentious issues.

#121. Jensen Huang isn’t out of the woods yet

Monday morning, Jensen Huang returned as CEO of Nvidia, after resigning the position following news of multiple affairs, including one with a subordinate and one with a woman tied to Chinese intelligence services. With so much of the AI industry built on top of Nvidia’s AI infrastructure, Huang’s departure looked like an imminent threat to the business — and the mood on his return has been ecstatic. Share prices are rocketing back up, particularly for neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius, and the Nasdaq rose 7% on the news.

Still, tech leaders should be clear-eyed about all the problems likely to come with Huang’s reinstatement. President Trump is already telling reporters that the U.S. government found no national security concerns resulting from the affair, but the broader national security concerns aren’t so easy to dismiss, and Trump’s involvement may do more harm than good for Nvidia and its partners. This isn’t the end of the problems caused by Huang’s affairs; it’s likely just the beginning.

Most importantly, serious questions remain about how Nvidia chips were smuggled into China, and how much Nvidia knew about it. Among Jensen Huang’s defenders, it’s become common to question whether Alice Huang was really a spy, but it’s clear that a lot of restricted chips were flowing into China through her company. Just this weekend, The New York Times reported sustained contact between Alice Huang and a member of China’s Ministry of State Security during the same period she was having an affair with Jensen Huang.

With Democrats in control of Congress, including investigative bodies like the House Oversight Committee, there are plenty of ways to force the issue. Nvidia’s role in violating U.S. export controls would likely have been the subject of prolonged uncomfortable investigation, whether Huang returned or not. But with him back in the CEO’s seat, there will be a lot more riding on any revelations that come up — and any new revelations will damage Nvidia as a company alongside Huang himself.

Then there are the questions raised by Trump’s intervention. It’s unprecedented in American history for a sitting president to intervene to clear a CEO accused of being a national security risk. Simply pressing the Department of Justice to exonerate Huang would have been a scandal, but calling the Nvidia board goes significantly further. Huang has already donated $25 million to Trump’s ballroom project, so it’s easy to make the case that Trump was intervening on behalf of a donor, setting national security risks aside for his personal benefit.

All of this is happening just as Democrats retake Congress, with all of its associated investigative powers. Even if they can’t fire Huang or remove Trump, there are lots of uncomfortable questions that are still unanswered. Was there any Department of Justice inquiry into whether Huang had committed a crime, or did Trump make the call on his own? Did the intelligence services make any official assessment that could be subpoenaed into the congressional record? Investigating Democrats will be particularly interested in Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who oversees export controls and retains significant financial ties to the industry through his firm, Cantor Fitzgerald.

If all of this sounds exhausting, you’re getting a sense of what the next year will be like for anyone following national news. Congress will keep a steady drip of embarrassing revelations, milking as much political juice as possible out of Huang and everyone associated with him. Trump will keep lashing out, often making things worse for everyone involved. (Trump’s statement to CBS, which defends Huang in racist terms while also accusing him of treason, is an early taste.) It’s going to be ugly and sad, and the most successful tech company of the last decade will be right at the center of it.

I have to assume that this was the scenario the board was concerned about when it decided to remove Huang two weeks ago. Since then, the cost of removing him has gone up: an open letter from Nvidia’s employees and partners, a tanking stock market, and, ultimately, direct intervention from the president of the United States.

But nothing has lowered the cost of keeping Huang, and the ugly scenario the board foresaw earlier this month is still very much in place. We’re about to see it come to life.

#117. Meta throws out its net-zero goal — its competitors should be furious

Meta’s new sustainability report buries the lead: The company is almost certain to miss its own goal to reach net zero by 2030, and it’s refusing to commit to a new timeline.

“We are re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing, and interim milestones — including the 2030 timeframe for our net zero emissions goal and our near-term intensity targets — so our approach reflects current operating realities while keeping progress transparent year over year,” wrote Rachel Peterson, vice president of data centers at Meta, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net-zero and sustainability at Meta, in the introduction to the company’s 2026 sustainability report.

That’s a lot of words to say that Meta will continue to pollute if that means it can continue its AI rollout.

Such is the risk of voluntary targets. Companies can make and break them with little to lose, betting — often rightly — that they can ignore them with just a small hit to public opinion, which is fickle anyway. (Remember the Cambridge Analytica scandal? Most people don’t.) There’s almost no risk to the bottom line.

“We remain committed to net zero, but are re-evaluating the 2030 timeline and interim milestone. We’ll continue investing in emissions reductions and reporting our progress annually,” a Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch.

A few years ago, Meta was arguably one of the better corporate stewards of the climate. The company has been one of the largest purchasers of clean power, and it set ambitious goals for water conservation.

More recently, though, its multibillion-dollar bet on natural gas at its Hyperion data center in Louisiana has undone much of that progress. Natural gas may burn cleaner and more efficiently than coal, but its impact on global warming can be just as bad when the leaky infrastructure needed to burn it is factored in.

Despite years of clean power procurement, Meta’s greenhouse gas emissions have more than doubled in the last four years. Between 2024 and 2025, its data center emissions increased more than 12 times, and Hyperion isn’t online yet.

That commitment to natural gas led to the company’s departure from RE100, a corporate renewable energy initiative, in July. The company had been part of the group for a decade, though the two split after RE100 started requiring more rigorous reporting toward renewable energy targets.

“When we set our net zero emissions goal, we anticipated steady growth, falling clean energy costs, and changes in the broader system that would enable progress at the pace we envisioned,” they added. “The reality has been far more complex. Demand for AI infrastructure has grown faster than almost anyone projected, arriving on a timeline measured in quarters rather than decades, and meeting it has meant building at a pace and scale the industry has never attempted.”

That’s all true, but when put that way, it sounds like Meta has little agency in the matter. But the company’s decision to chase AI was a conscious one.

You could argue that Meta has a fiduciary duty to chase after its peers, which have also dived headfirst into AI. But there’s precious little data to support the idea that AI will be a profit driver for a company like Meta, which is almost entirely consumer-focused.

CEO Mark Zuckerberg is optimistic that improving Muse’s energy efficiency will turn that around, but how much will everyday people be willing to pay for software that translates your offhand thoughts into surreal memes? Or monitors your emails so you won’t forget to reply to your mom? Meta is betting that it’ll be enough to support a $200 billion-plus data center, and probably more.

The choice of the fuel to power those AI ambitions was also a conscious one. Natural gas power plants, besides being able to run 24/7, are actually not a great fit for AI data centers. Turbines like to run at constant speeds, while AI data centers’ energy requirements surge and sag depending on the load. They need batteries as a buffer, which begs the question: Why not just connect to the grid and use batteries to bridge any gaps during times of peak demand? That’s what one expert recommends.

Meta’s bet on natural gas appears mistimed. Long-duration energy storage is coming into its own — and geothermal energy appears poised to become the next major power source on the grid in the U.S. Companies like Fervo, Mazama, and Quaise have been logging milestones at a rapid clip, potentially unlocking tens of gigawatts of capacity. The technology isn’t competitive with natural gas today, but it very well could be in the mid-2030s, just a few years after Meta completes Hyperion. Geothermal is also not vulnerable to supply chain shocks, like what happened with natural gas and the Iran War.

Beyond that, the company’s decision to abandon its 2030 net-zero goal risks fomenting the sort of backlash that leads to regulations with real teeth. Such regulations won’t just apply to Meta, and the broader industry would be justified in their frustration.

In that light, Meta’s timing couldn’t be worse. Surveys show the general public doesn’t hold AI or data centers in high esteem, rightly sensing that the infrastructure development is driving a rapid and often-unchecked expansion of polluting infrastructure.

The tech industry has spent over a decade trying to prove that it would be different from previous industrial juggernauts like oil and gas or automobile manufacturing. In a single PDF, Meta might have undone much of that goodwill. After this, who will trust another tech company’s voluntary net-zero goals?

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

#93. After Japan's APEC snub, what's next for its China ties?

Japan has long-running concerns over Chinese military expansionism, and Beijing has bristled at Sanae Takaichi's comments on Taiwan. The frosty ties were on full display at the APEC summit.

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi received a cold shoulder from China during the recent Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in the tech hub of Shenzhen.

After Takaichi arrived in Shenzhen on November 18 for the two-day summit, there was an immediate issue when the mobile stairs to the door of her aircraft were some way short of the threshold, requiring her to hold on to the door frame to step down around 40 centimeters (about 15 inches) to the top step.

It may have been completely inadvertent, but upon safely reaching the airport tarmac, her official welcome was decidedly low-key. There were no musical performances, no cheering crowds or traditional dances, which were laid on for many other APEC leaders as they arrived.

Japan's Yomiuri newspaper reported on November 20 that when Takaichi met Chinese leader Xi Jinping at the dinner reception, "his reaction was cold." And while many other regional leaders managed at least a brief one-on-one with Xi on the sidelines of the main event, the Japanese delegation was not granted such access.

"The level of frostiness from the Chinese side was on full display from the moment Takaichi's plane landed," said William Yang, a Taiwan-based analyst for the International Crisis Group.

"I cannot think that the issue with the plane steps was anything other than deliberate and there was a huge contrast between the welcome she received and virtually everyone else," he told DW.

Yang added that the ice didn't break when Takaichi tried to speak to Xi at the dinner reception and the entire occasion "just reflects the state of bilateral relations."

China and Japan share a troubled history, marked by the Japanese invasion of China in the 1930s and the often brutal occupation of cities that were captured and subjugated.

Chinese schoolchildren learn about the horrors of the Nanjing Massacre and the use of chemical and biological weapons by Japanese forces.

Japan maintains that its repeated post-war apologies and massive economic aid that helped China to become the economic powerhouse it is today are overlooked amid the criticism.

Diplomatic relations were normalized in 1972 and Tokyo pursued a policy of engagement over the following decades, although that came to a halt in the mid-2000s, points out David Adebahr, a professor of international relations and Asia-Pacific security policy at Kobe University.

"China's rising military spending, the breakdown of talks over the East China Sea in 2006, and a resurgence of nationalism raised concerns even among Japan's more China-friendly diplomats, while defence analysts noted an increase in Chinese submarine activity near Japanese waters," he told DW.

The nationalism on display during the 2008 Beijing Olympics, coupled with China's more assertive stance over the Senkaku Islands, which Japan has sovereignty over but which Beijing claims, accelerated this shift.

Ties took a sudden nosedive in November 2025 when Takaichi responded to a question in Japan's parliament, the Diet, by saying that any "contingency” involving Taiwan would also inevitably threaten the national security of Japan.

According to Adebahr, the Japanese leader was merely saying out loud what "had long been the internal logic of Japanese security planning." Nevertheless, Beijing has reacted with fury.

China has demanded that Takaichi retract the statement and raised pressure on Japan by restricting exports of rare earth minerals critical to Japanese industry, banning imports of Japanese seafood and telling tourists to take Japan off their destination list.

And while ruffled national feelings have been smoothed in the past with shows of reciprocal goodwill, China is standing firm on its demands this time.

"From Tokyo's perspective, it is clear that Beijing believes it holds more cards than in the past," said Adebahr.

"Today, Beijing has considerable leverage: Japan's dependence on Chinese rare earths; an increasingly visible military presence around Japan, sometimes in conjunction with Russia; and a US administration that has urged Tokyo to de-escalate rather than firmly backing it."

Adebahr added that this left China with little incentive to offer Takaichi a face-saving compromise. However, he pointed out that Beijing's reliance on coercion could backfire by prompting Japan to increase defence spending and seek closer ties with other partners.

As a committed conservative who has talked tough on Beijing's rapid military buildup, its seizure of islands and atolls in the South China Sea and the inexorable pressure on Taiwan to capitulate and become one with mainland China, Takaichi cannot give in to Xi's demands for fear of losing face with her hardline base.

Analyst Adebahr said that while he expects diplomatic relations to remain frosty, there are too many interdependencies for a full decoupling between China and Japan.

"I would not be too pessimistic about the relationship, because in the medium term, trade, technology and critical minerals are now seen as security issues," he added. "Therefore, complex interdependence will prevent a return to the old pattern of keeping politics and economics separate."

"While economic interdependence alone will not be enough to stabilize the relationship, full decoupling is highly unlikely, as transaction costs would be far too high for both sides," he said.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

#82. Supertake raises $20M to let AI agents turn your market predictions into stock portfolios

AI investing startup Supertake has an interesting premise: What if your “takes” on the market could make you money without you having to pick stocks yourself? The service lets people turn their ideas into real investment portfolios, using frontier AI models and trading agents.

Now, the company has raised $20 million in seed funding to grow its business. The round was led by venture firm Adjacent (Nico Wittenborn), with participation from Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures (USV). Michael Mignano originally incubated the startup at USV. He is the co-founder and former CEO of the podcasting platform Anchor, which Spotify acquired in 2019 for over $150 million. He also co-founded Oboe, an AI education startup, and joined USV as a general partner in April.

“We really believed there was an opportunity to make investing more democratized…and give everyone and anyone the ability to invest in their own insights and beliefs and ideas,” Mignano said. While he will continue in his role as founder and executive chairman, he’s now brought in a team to run the company day to day.

The team includes co-founders Lincoln Murr (CEO) and John Peterson (CTO). Both joined from Coinbase, where, Mignano notes, they were the heads of AI product and engineering, leading launches like Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents, agentic wallets, and others.

The startup arrives as AI continues to make inroads into financial services, but allowing AI to take over people’s actual investments requires a new level of trust. Supertake is betting that people will ultimately be willing to take that leap, given recent improvements in frontier AI models.

Currently, Supertake works with Coinbase and Robinhood, and the company intends to expand to more brokerages over time. The idea is that you can explain, in broad, natural language, what you believe about how the world is going to change, and Supertake’s AI will iterate with you back and forth to turn that idea into a “take” that’s investable.

Under the hood, the startup is leveraging frontier AI technology and tools, combined with its own proprietary software that directs and limits what the AI can do.

Supertake’s users’ ideas are tracked on a leaderboard on the site, so others who agree can invest alongside them. For instance, there’s currently an idea on the leaderboard about looksmaxxing and longevity becoming more of a trend, where the portfolio includes certain pharma and cosmetic companies that could potentially benefit.

For now, these portfolios consist of stock picks, but the team wants to expand the service over time to handle other types of transactions, like shorts, derivatives, perpetuals, long exposure, or even prediction markets — the latter handled through partnerships with companies in that space.

Despite these “takes” seeming like bets on what could happen next in a given market, Murr stresses that this doesn’t make Supertake itself a prediction market.

“We think it’s a novel and very much improved way to create what was previously considered the robo-advisor experience,” he says. “When that came out, it had this amazing vision and direction of allowing people to have digital asset management. We think now is actually the time for that, with AI agents being so widely proliferated.”

“I don’t think this is necessarily a prediction market by any means. The idea of natural language translation into an underlying set of assets feels different in that regard,” he says.

Still, the startup could wade into short-term predictions if a user’s take involves something they believe could happen in the near future, like who might win an election.

“We’re primarily focused on the longer, medium-term holdings today. But that being said, we don’t want to preclude people from building out very hyper short-term strategies that might exist,” Murr adds.

To address people’s concerns over AI managing their money, Supertake’s team says it’s implementing certain guardrails and spend limits. For instance, the AI has no ability to deposit or withdraw funds on a user’s behalf without their explicit consent. There are also drift limits in place so the portfolio stays balanced, plus allow and deny lists (meaning which assets the AI may and may not trade), real-time monitoring for anomalous activity, tools to set trade size limits, and a restriction that keeps agents from seeing the user’s brokerage credentials.

Access to Supertake is still behind a waitlist while in beta, but the founders said they’ve put their money where their mouths are. Murr and Peterson are letting it manage “tens of thousands” and “thousands” of dollars, respectively.

The report says 99% of Gaza shows indicators of potential environmental contamination

Oxfam and the UK-based Conflict and Environment Observatory (CEOBS) used satellite imagery, remote sensing, spatial analysis and other open-source data to draw their conclusions in the most comprehensive report to date on the severe environmental impacts caused by three years of intense war in Gaza.

The report details how munitions used in Israel's intense bombardment, as well as the debris from destroyed buildings and infrastructure, all have an impact on the environment. #27. What failure of COP31 reveals about a divided world

Some have hailed the lack of consensus as a sign of a positive shift, despite the Turkey summit failure

Some may have found it ironic that the world's biggest conference on climate change should collapse over the very mention of fossil fuels - the main cause of global warming.

Yet that is what happened in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday.

The failure tells us something about the state of climate diplomacy. But it may tell us even more about the increasingly divided world in which the negotiations take place.

Chris Bowen, the Australian minister who led the negotiations, spoke of a backdrop constituting "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory". The competing demands, he said, had become "unbridgeable red lines".

Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister and the summit's president, was blunter. Governments should not disappoint people desperate for climate action, he suggested, by getting "hung up on one word or phrase".

And Kurum had a point. Three years ago, at COP28 in Dubai, there was agreement to transition away from fossil fuels. The proposed Antalya Pact failed to mention them at all.

Was it really worth sacrificing the agreement over two words, then?

The countries that rejected it said the distinction was fundamental. They argued that there was a world of difference between promising to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and committing to move away from the fuels responsible for most of them. Without that explicit commitment, they feared the new agreement would weaken the one made in Dubai.

Co-leader of the negotiations Chris Bowen blamed the failure to reach an agreement on "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory"

Normally, it is difficult to establish exactly who has said what at these conferences. Much of the bargaining takes place behind closed doors, and governments are often reluctant to admit which proposals they have opposed.

This time, some of the opposition was public - mostly from oil-producing nations.

Mohammad Ayoub, a Saudi negotiator speaking on behalf of the 22 Arab League countries, argued that Arab nations had contributed less than 2% of historical greenhouse gas emissions. It was wealthy industrialised nations, he said, that bore the overwhelming responsibility for the problem.

He defended the Arab countries' right to choose how to tackle climate change according to their national circumstances.

Russia's delegate Sergei Kononuchenko went further. Cutting oil and gas supplies before demand fell, he warned, would cause shortages, higher prices and energy poverty.

"For that reason, we cannot agree to any formulation on fossil fuel," he said.

The wider political backdrop was still more revealing.

The US, which in previous rounds of talks had helped broker compromises - sometimes working alongside China - had no official delegation in Antalya. President Donald Trump has rejected the entire international climate process.

And while delegates argued over fossil fuels in Turkey, Trump, China's President Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin issued a joint declaration in Shenzhen that included a pledge to increase production and supply of refined petroleum products.

The priorities of some of the world's most powerful governments appeared very different from those being discussed in the negotiating rooms.

The EU's representative, Irish Minister Darragh O'Brien, said a deal that did not mention fossil fuels was "unconscionable"

Yet, perhaps, the picture is more optimistic than it first appears. About 130 governments backed a demand for explicit language on moving away from fossil fuels - roughly two-thirds of the countries represented in Antalya.

Christiana Figueres, who led the UN negotiations that delivered the Paris Agreement, argued that countries were right to walk away rather than endorse a weaker political commitment that might undermine the progress already made.

Looked at this way, the collapse is not evidence that the world has lost its appetite for climate action. Quite the opposite - a substantial majority of governments were demanding more ambitious action.

So why not simply find a way for them to move ahead without waiting for the countries determined to block them?

The difficulty becomes clearer when you look beyond the dispute over fossil fuels.

Kononuchenko had another target in his sights: climate finance. Much of the money wealthy countries provide to poorer nations, he complained, comes as loans rather than grants. He said that developing countries ended up repaying more than they received.

"This is an old colonial distribution of labour under a new banner," he declared.

Then, in a striking moment, he leaned towards the microphone and switched from Russian into English to address his criticism directly to the Europeans: "First of all distinguished representatives of the EU."

Russia has its own reasons for attacking Europe, but the complaint about finance is one that many developing countries share. They argue that they are being asked to pay for damage they did little to cause while receiving too little help to adapt to climate change or build cleaner economies.

The EU had ruled out significant new funding before the negotiations began. Mohamed Adow, the Kenyan campaigner who heads Power Shift Africa, accused Europe of rejecting a flawed deal while refusing to acknowledge its own responsibility for the failure on finance.

The UN's climate chief, Simon Stiell, said he would seek advice on potential reform to the COP process

So is it possible for a majority of countries to outvote those blocking progress?

The UN climate chief, Simon Stiell, has acknowledged the need to "improve, modernise and reform" the process. Some governments have proposed introducing voting when consensus proves impossible. Others want countries prepared to move faster to make their own agreements outside the formal talks.

But there is a reason the UN climate process depends on consensus. International climate co-operation is largely voluntary.

Voting might produce more ambitious agreements on paper, but would it persuade the governments that had been outvoted to act? Consensus also offers some protection to smaller and poorer nations that might otherwise find themselves overruled by the most powerful countries.

Meanwhile, the failure to agree a summit declaration does not mean climate action stops. Solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles are becoming increasingly competitive, while the upheaval in global oil and gas supplies is giving countries another reason to generate their own electricity rather than depend on imported fossil fuels.

The energy transition is moving forward, whatever happens in the UN negotiating rooms. The problem is that it is not moving nearly fast enough for the climate.

Perhaps the most poignant aspect of the failed Antalya Pact is what the countries had apparently been prepared to accept.

The final draft expressed "deep concern" that global warming was expected to exceed 1.5Â°C in the next few years. It warned that further warming would cause increasingly serious damage, some of it irreversible, and called for urgent action to limit how far temperatures rose above that threshold.

This remarkably strong language had survived into the final version, apparently without provoking the opposition that sank the agreement. Even countries bitterly divided over fossil fuels and finance seemed prepared to acknowledge the scale of the danger.

Yet they couldn't agree on how to respond.

And that, perhaps, is what Antalya tells us about the state of international climate co-operation. The problem isn't simply finding the right words or changing the voting rules. It is persuading countries with very different interests to take action that no international authority can compel them to take.

That was always going to be difficult. In today's divided world, it looks harder than ever.
