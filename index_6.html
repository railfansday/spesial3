VAST Data says petabyte SSDs will outgrow RAID in today’s parallel file systems, according to a simulation of its own 900 PB system that puts the odds of data loss at 1 in 11 over 10 years. At Supercomputing 2026 (SC26), the company explains how.

Bigger drives take longer to rebuild after a failure, leaving more time for further failures to lose data, but operators will still want those large drives because many sites are power-constrained and storage power scales with bandwidth rather than capacity. VAST’s recommended fix: system-wide erasure coding with at least three parity strips.

The company’s principal technical strategist, Glenn Lockwood, walked through the simulation, described as a “discrete event simulation” using real failure data, in an Exhibitor Forum talk during SC26’s HPC System Hardware and Architecture session. With petabyte-scale SSDs swapped in, enclosure-local declustered RAID — the approach used by DDN EXAScaler and HPE Cray ClusterStor, per Lockwood — has those exceptionally high odds of data loss. With global declustering and extra parity, operators can get this down to 1 in 54 million, a significant improvement, but VAST’s own scheme gets to about 1 in 62 billion, three orders of magnitude better.

Lockwood’s case for petabyte SSDs rests on how storage power scales: at about 28 W per SSD, regardless of capacity and including its share of the surrounding infrastructure, plus 20 W per GB/s of bandwidth. Getting 900 PB of flash from 15 TB SSDs would take more than 63K drives, each adding its own bandwidth, for 190 TB/s at 5.6 MW, or “the equivalent of 3,100 or so of Blackwell Ultra GPUs.” Lockwood says, though, that “real AI applications in our experience have never approached needing anything close to that level of performance” from the storage.

With 122 TB drives, at almost an 8K count and much lower 8 TB/s of aggregate bandwidth, the power draw is less than a tenth of that, at about 400 kW. Within that budget, 15 TB SSDs top out at about 250 PB, even with no power left for performance. That power could be better spent on compute and, anyway, 245 TB-class drives are already arriving, with much larger drives expected.

Everything is a series of trade-offs, though, and larger drives come with their own caveats. Lockwood acknowledges the usual reaction to a 245 TB drive: that it’s “a big blast radius,” since a single failure leaves much more to rebuild. And it does take longer: at the same per-drive speed, a 122 TB drive takes twice as long to rebuild as a 60 TB drive, leaving a longer window in which a second failure in a traditional RAID 5 set loses data. Per-drive capacity is potentially outpacing bandwidth increases and, at least within a generation, “the performance of each individual drive does not change,” so this is something that must be accepted or overcome.

The simulated system involves 7,920 SSDs at 122 TB each, using 180 enclosures with 44 drives each. With a RAID 5 single parity solution, there is a 100% chance of data loss over 10 years, but “no one uses single parity anymore.” Double parity still has 1 in 150 odds of data loss over a decade and is considered “state of the art for many open source file systems,” and he explicitly names Lustre and BeeGFS within this context.

For proprietary parallel file system implementations, declustered RAID within an enclosure is the state of the art, instead. Declustered RAID spreads each drive group’s data, parity, and spare space over more drives than usual, allowing for faster rebuilds. Keeping it within an enclosure means that the spread still stays within a single box of drives. Swapping petabyte SSDs into that enclosure-level declustering is what gets to the headline 1 in 11 odds of data loss.

As for the mentioned rivals, DDN’s DCR, in one published configuration, uses RAID 6 in an 8+2 array for a 24-NVMe SSD pool. That means eight data strips plus two parity strips per stripe, allowing any two drives in the pool to fail without losing data, and each stripe is spread over 10 of the 24 drives. DDN has conceded in the past that larger systems may need more than DCR. HPE’s declustered RAID solution is known as GridRAID, with declustered parity per enclosure half, meaning two 12-drive pools per 24-drive enclosure.

Perlmutter, the supercomputer at Berkeley Lab’s National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center, where Lockwood once worked, used GridRAID with 8+2+1 RAID 6 per 12-drive object storage target, Lustre’s name for a storage unit. The +1 is one spare drive’s worth of space, spread across all 12, so a rebuild writes to every surviving drive rather than to a single replacement. Either way, protection stops at two failures in a pool of one or two dozen drives, and only those drives can assist with a rebuild. This is the scenario in VAST’s simulation with the catastrophic failure rate.

Declustered RAID across the whole system, plus extra parity

Several solutions exist, with limits. Adding a parity strip for triple parity reduces the odds of data loss. Global declustering, on its own, can also reduce the odds a little bit, with faster rebuild times. However, stripes are spread over every drive in the cluster, so two random failures are more likely to hit the same stripe, meaning a larger fault domain.

If both approaches are used, which is the minimum bar for him, you get down to getting struck by “lightning on your birthday” odds. VAST already publishes quadruple parity, which is beyond this bar. Using VAST’s own “special magic,” undisclosed but beyond the minimum bar, drops the odds even further, specifically for the petabyte SSDs, which is the real target of the talk.

Then we get to the metadata, the data about data, which in this case is the index of where every piece of every file dwells. Every write necessarily updates it synchronously, so VAST keeps it on separate, fast SSDs as three full copies for replication, not parity. This means it can survive two failures, which is less than the data’s four parity strips, and the index becomes the weakest link. With 122 TB drives, he puts the data drives’ odds at 1 in 78 trillion over 10 years, against 1 in 58 million for metadata. To Lockwood, this illustrates that the surrounding infrastructure is the real risk, given the failure gap between the index and regular data.

Rebuilding in the index area starts about 12 seconds after a failure, on average, versus about two minutes for data drives, Lockwood says. VAST’s procedure more generally, for when a drive drops out, is to power-cycle it and hope, so there is always some delay before a rebuild is forced. This slight delay barely matters for data drives with four parity strips but is more impactful for the three-copy metadata index drives. For example, waiting an hour for data drives “wouldn’t make a big difference” while just 20 minutes or more on a metadata drive has “extremely high” risk.

Earlier this year, in May, Micron began shipping the 245 TB 6600 ION, which pulls up to 30 W at maximum load. Kioxia has the LC9, used in a Dell 2U server with 40 drives for a total of 9.8 PB. SanDisk also has its 256 TB UltraQLC, which was due in the first half of this year, and Solidigm is targeting about 245 TB later this year.

Meanwhile, multiple roadmaps reach petabyte-class drives, including SanDisk’s UltraQLC, a target by SK hynix for QLC SSDs, and Samsung at some time in the future. This strengthens VAST’s case and, while at least the power math adds up, data still needs to be compared openly and fairly to make a firm conclusion about the best pathway forward for what is clearly an unavoidable problem.

#10388. What Is the Best UI design Tool? 8 Tools Compared by the Job They Do (2026)

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

The ideal UI testing tool is the one that discovers what your users discover first: broken layouts, usability issues that no longer work, and new accessibility issues in all the browsers your users use before a change goes into the repository. In 2026, most frontend teams don't rely on a single tool to do all of that. The best setups involve two layers: a test runner layer that launches the browser and monitors behavior, and a UI testing and review platform that visually checks the outcome and routes changes to the right stakeholders for sign-off.

Even with the right stack, it still relies on what your team needs to catch. Different teams will focus on different points: a QA team trying to automate checkout flows, a design system team defending shared components, and a single developer working on a tight budget will all be somewhere different. This guide is categorized by the job these UI testing tools do, then connects them.

Most "best UI testing tool" lists rank test runners and visual testing platforms against each other as if they were interchangeable. They aren't. UI testing covers four distinct jobs:

Functional testing: Does the UI respond correctly when a user clicks, types, or hovers?

Visual testing: Does the UI still look right across layout, color, typography, and spacing?

Accessibility testing: Can people using keyboards and screen readers use it, and did this change introduce new violations?

Cross-browser and device coverage: Does it work in Safari on mobile, not just Chrome on a developer's laptop?

There are also other levels of UI tests. Unit tests test the logic but don't interact with the UI. End-to-end (E2E) tests represent the full user journey in a browser but are difficult to set up, hard to troubleshoot when the bug is a visual detail, and can't practically test every state. Between the extremes of unit tests and E2E tests, component tests simulate each component's behavior in a real browser using fixed inputs, offering speed closer to unit tests and user-level coverage closer to E2E. Many teams maintain a thin layer of E2E tests for critical journeys, and they have component tests for the dozens of states a component may be in.

These tools are organized by job so you can stack them rather than choose one winner

These platforms sit on top of your test runner. They capture rendered UI, compare it to approved baselines, and give your team a place to review what changed.

Chromatic is a UI testing and review platform made by the Storybook team. It catches visual, interaction, and accessibility bugs in components and pages before they merge, and it gives developers, designers, and product managers one place to review UI changes and sign off. Chromatic's team is the core maintainer of Storybook, which has about 90,000 GitHub stars and 17 million weekly npm downloads, and a Vitest core maintainer on the team built Chromatic's Vitest integration. Chromatic is used by 45 Fortune 100 companies, including GitHub, Shopify, Microsoft, Adobe, the BBC, and monday.com.

Pricing: Free plan with 5,000 snapshots per month (Chrome only), unlimited projects and users. Starter is $179/month for 35,000 snapshots and adds Safari, Firefox, and Edge. Pro is $399/month for 85,000 snapshots. Enterprise is custom, with SSO, SCIM, SLA support, and data retention. TurboSnap snapshots count as 0.2 of a regular snapshot.

Trade-offs: Chromatic is strongest when your UI is built from components. Teams with no component tests can start from Playwright or Cypress E2E tests, but they'll get the most value once components are covered too. Snapshot usage also grows with each browser and viewport you add, so plan coverage before you scale it.

Best for: Design system teams, product teams that want designers and PMs to approve UI changes, React Native teams, and Vitest-first teams that want visual testing without baselines stored in the repo.

Percy is BrowserStack's visual testing platform. It captures screenshots from your existing tests, renders them across browsers and responsive widths, and highlights pixel differences for review.

Strengths: Framework-agnostic SDKs for Playwright, Cypress, Selenium, and Storybook. App Percy covers native mobile apps. The free plan includes 5,000 screenshots per month with unlimited users and projects.

Trade-offs: Percy is page-oriented, and each page rendered in each browser at each width counts as a separate screenshot, so usage climbs quickly with cross-browser coverage. Percy compares feature branches against a base branch, which teams with complex branching should test early. Reviewers see rendered screenshots rather than an inspectable copy of the code under test, so debugging a diff usually means reproducing it locally.

Pricing: Free tier, then paid plans with fixed screenshot allowances and overage.

Best for: Teams already paying for BrowserStack that want visual checks on page-level E2E tests.

Applitools Eyes uses Visual AI to compare screenshots the way a person would, with match levels that can ignore content changes and focus on layout. Its Ultrafast Grid re-renders captured page states across many browser, viewport, and device combinations.

Strengths: Mature AI matching, broad browser and device coverage, Figma and Storybook testing, accessibility testing, and an MCP server. Higher tiers add AI-driven functional testing and plain-English test authoring.

Trade-offs: Applitools is priced and packaged for enterprises. There's a free trial but no free plan, and the Starter plan is $667/month billed annually for 100,000 component checkpoints or 1,000 page checkpoints. Professional and Enterprise are custom-priced.

Best for: Large QA organizations with big cross-browser matrices and budget for a dedicated visual AI platform.

BackstopJS is a free, open-source visual regression testing tool released under the MIT license. You list the URLs, viewports, and CSS selectors to capture in a config file, and BackstopJS renders them in a headless browser through Playwright or Puppeteer, then compares the results against approved reference screenshots. The core workflow runs on three commands: backstop init, backstop test, and backstop approve.

Strengths: Completely free, with no screenshot limits. A built-in browser report shows reference and test images side by side with a visual diff inspector, and changes can be approved from there. Optional Docker rendering keeps screenshots consistent between laptops and CI; Playwright adds Firefox and WebKit, and click, hover, and keypress selectors capture interactive states. JUnit reports plug into existing CI pipelines.

Trade-offs: BackstopJS is self-hosted, so there's no cloud dashboard, shared team review, or pull request status checks out of the box. Reference screenshots are usually committed to your repository, and every page or component state you want to test needs its own URL and scenario in the config. Someone on the team also has to own the setup, CI integration, and maintenance.

Best for: Developers and small teams with no budget who are comfortable running and maintaining their own visual regression setup.

Test runners click, type, and assert. They're free and open source, and every platform above builds on them. On their own, they offer limited visual testing: built-in screenshot assertions store baselines in your repository, rendering can drift between laptops and CI, and designers have no review interface.

Playwright is Microsoft's open-source browser automation framework. It runs tests across Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit, with auto-waiting, trace viewer debugging and code generation, and supports JavaScript, TypeScript, Python, Java, and .NET.

Visual testing: Playwright's toHaveScreenshot assertion compares screenshots against baselines stored in Git. It works, but baselines must be regenerated per operating system, and accepting a change means rerunning with an update flag.

Best for: Teams automating critical user journeys such as sign-up, checkout and login. Pair it with a platform like Chromatic for visual review across real browsers.

Cypress is an open-source testing tool that runs inside the browser, with time-travel debugging that shows the app at each step of a test. It supports both E2E and component testing.

Visual testing: Cypress has no built-in visual comparison and relies on plugins or a platform such as Chromatic, Percy or Applitools. Cypress Cloud, its paid service, adds recording, parallelization, flake detection and add-ons for accessibility and UI coverage.

Best for: Frontend developers who want fast feedback and an interactive test runner while they build.

Storybook is the open source workshop for building UI components in isolation. Each story captures one component state, and those stories double as test cases.

Testing features: Interaction tests with play functions and Testing Library, accessibility checks with the a11y addon, and a Vitest integration that runs stories as component tests.

Visual testing: Storybook doesn't diff screenshots on its own. Chromatic, built by the same team, is its visual testing and review layer.

Best for: Design system and component library teams, and any team practicing component-driven development.

#10380. Build Live Video Chat With WebRTC: From First Call to Production Debugging

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Adding real-time video to a web app used to mean plugins, proprietary SDKs and a lot of money. Today every modern browser ships with WebRTC, a set of APIs that lets two browsers exchange audio, video and data directly. The catch is that WebRTC gives you building blocks, not a finished feature. This guide walks through those blocks in the order you actually need them, with the exact API calls you need, and then covers the production issues that tutorials usually skip.

A browser-to-browser call has four moving parts:

Start with the local preview. Browsers only allow camera access on HTTPS or localhost, so serve your page accordingly.

1.     Request a stream with navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia(), passing video constraints (an ideal width of 1280 and height of 720 is a sensible default) and audio constraints with echoCancellation and noiseSuppression enabled.

2.     Assign the returned MediaStream to the srcObject property of your local video element.

3.     Keep a reference to the stream, because you will add its tracks to the peer connection later.

Set the muted and playsinline attributes on the local video element. Without muted users hear themselves, and without playsinline iOS Safari opens the video full screen.

The peers need a way to find each other. A tiny Node.js server using the ws package is enough for development. Its whole job is to relay messages between the peers in a room:

1.     Create a WebSocket server (for example with the ws package) listening on its own port.

2.     Keep a map of room names to the set of sockets in each room.

3.     When a client sends a join message, store the room name on the socket and add it to that room's set.

4.     For every other message type, forward the raw message to the other sockets in the same room, and never echo it back to the sender.

5.     On disconnect, remove the socket from its room so stale peers do not receive messages.

In production you would add authentication, limit room size to two for one-to-one calls, and validate message types, but the shape stays the same.

Both sides create an RTCPeerConnection, add their local tracks and listen for the remote ones:

1.     Create a new RTCPeerConnection with an iceServers list that contains at least one STUN server.

2.     Loop over the local stream's tracks and call addTrack() for each one.

3.     In the ontrack handler, attach the first remote stream to your remote video element.

4.     In the onicecandidate handler, send every new candidate to the other peer through your signalling channel as a candidate message.

One peer creates an offer, the other answers, and both apply each other's descriptions. Candidates arrive asynchronously in the background.

1.     The caller runs createOffer(), applies it with setLocalDescription() and sends the description as an offer message.

2.     The callee applies the offer with setRemoteDescription(), runs createAnswer(), applies it locally and sends it back as an answer.

3.     The caller applies the answer with setRemoteDescription().

4.     Both sides pass every incoming candidate message to addIceCandidate().

If both peers might start a call at the same time, look up the "perfect negotiation" pattern on MDN. It assigns one side as polite and resolves offer collisions cleanly instead of leaving both peers stuck.

On a home network, STUN alone often works. On corporate Wi-Fi, mobile carriers and symmetric NATs it frequently does not, and the call silently fails to connect. A TURN server relays media when a direct path is impossible. The open-source coturn server is the usual choice.

In practice, your iceServers list should contain your own STUN URL plus a TURN URL with a username and credential, so the browser can fall back to the relay automatically when direct connectivity fails.

Generate short-lived TURN credentials on your backend rather than hard-coding them, and budget for bandwidth: relayed calls pass every frame through your server.

Users deny camera access, unplug headsets and switch from Wi-Fi to 4G mid-call. Catch NotAllowedError and show a clear explanation, listen for devicechange events, and use RTCRtpSender.replaceTrack() to swap cameras without renegotiating the whole call.

Listen to pc.onconnectionstatechange. When the state becomes disconnected, wait a few seconds before giving up, because it often recovers. On failed, call pc.restartIce() to try new network paths.

Use RTCRtpSender.setParameters() to cap the bitrate on weak connections, and read pc.getStats() to monitor packet loss and round-trip time. Showing a small "poor connection" indicator does more for perceived quality than any codec tweak.

Mesh calls, where every participant connects to every other, work for two or three people. Beyond that, upload bandwidth explodes. Group calls need a Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU) such as mediasoup, LiveKit or Janus, which receives each stream once and forwards it to the others.

Tutorials end at a working demo, but the fastest way to understand what production WebRTC looks like is to watch a real call from the inside. Chrome ships an internal diagnostics page that shows every peer connection the browser opens, and it works on any site that uses WebRTC.

1.     Open chrome://webrtc-internals in one tab and keep it open.

2.     In another tab, start a call on your own demo and find its RTCPeerConnection entry.

3.     Expand that entry and look at the ICE candidate pairs: you will see whether the call went direct (host or srflx) or through a TURN relay (relay).

4.     Open the stats graphs for the outbound video stream and watch the bitrate, frame rate and resolution adapt when you throttle the network in DevTools.

5.     Check the negotiated codec and how quickly the connection recovers when you switch Wi-Fi off and back on.

Once you can read your own call, repeat the exercise on a production service. Any browser-based product works, whether a meeting tool or a live video chat that pairs two visitors, because webrtc-internals shows the same data for every site. Expect differences from your demo: more candidate pairs, a relay candidate in the list and a bitrate that adapts faster. Seeing them makes the earlier sections concrete, from why TURN matters to how bandwidth estimation reacts to packet loss.

The one-to-one call above is the core of many products, from telehealth consultations and tutoring to customer support. The engineering challenges grow with the use case: random matching needs queueing and presence on the signalling server, public platforms need moderation and reporting, and global audiences need TURN servers in several regions.

Start with the minimal version, get it working across Chrome, Firefox and Safari on both desktop and mobile, and add TURN before real users arrive. Once the call is reliable, every other feature becomes a much easier problem.

#10365. Announcing Long-Context Training on TPUs with MaxText Context Parallelism

Long-context training — including pretraining and post-training, has become a foundation of frontier AI models. Longer native sequence lengths give large language models (LLMs) the working memory to read a whole code repository or ingest hours of video, and reason through multi-step agentic tasks. In three years, context windows have grown from 128K–200K tokens to 1M–2M, and models trained toward 10M tokens are arriving. Training at those lengths, not just serving them, is where the system limits show up.

In particular, efficient training at multi-million-token sequence lengths runs into the following bottlenecks:

Today, we are excited to announce long-context training support in MaxText, Google Cloud's open-source JAX training framework. MaxText enables this by supporting four context-parallel strategies on Cloud TPU: All-gather, A2A (DeepSpeed Ulysses), P2P (Ring Attention), and the A2A + P2P hybrid (Unified Sequence Parallelism, or USP), combined with load balancing and sequence packing. All four use the Tokamax Splash Attention kernel. Combined with the 3D inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ( supported on TPUs such as TPU 5p or TPU 7 (Ironwood) ), this lets one sequence span an entire slice while communication overlaps compute and memory per chip stays flat. In our benchmarks, Llama 3.1 8B (25 head dim, 16 query heads) demonstrates exceptional long-context efficiency, achieving 70% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) on TPU 5p for sequence lengths up to 4 million tokens. Across both TPU 5p and TPU 7, context can be seamlessly extended to 14 million tokens—with TPU 7 consistently maintaining 50% MFU across all sequence scales below 6 million tokens.

This functionality is available today in MaxText v0.2.4(doc).

Context parallelism splits a long sequence across chips, so no chip holds the activations of the whole sequence. Context parallelism can be combined with data and tensor parallelism, and the smallest context-parallel size that fits your sequence in HBM is usually the right one. This also holds for sparser attention variants which cut attention compute but not the activations of every other layer.

Attention is the one global operation where every token needs the keys and values of every preceding token, so each context parallel strategy has to bring those keys and values back together. Each makes a different tradeoff between memory (how much KV a chip holds), communication (how much moves per layer, and whether it overlaps compute), and model shape (how many attention heads to split).

All-gather: Each chip keeps its chunk of the sequence and, at every layer, gathers the keys and values from every other chip. It is the simplest option. However, because causal masking causes later tokens to attend to larger prefixes, All-gather requires balancing compute load across devices.

All-to-All (Ulysses): Gathering keys and values makes every chip hold the whole sequence, so attention memory never shrinks no matter how many chips you add. All-to-All changes what each chip owns instead. Attention is independent across heads but not across tokens, so the head axis is free to shard and the token axis is not. An All-to-All before attention rotates the sharding from one onto the other: each chip gives up the heads it is not responsible for and receives the tokens it was missing for the heads it keeps, ending up with the full sequence for a few heads instead of a few tokens for all heads. This is also why Ulysses does not need token load balancing. Attention then runs as ordinary single-chip attention with standard kernels, and a second All-to-All rotates the output back. KV per chip shrinks by the context-parallel factor, but you cannot split across more chips than the model has heads — and tensor parallelism shards that same dimension, so TP × CP is capped by the head count (under GQA, by the KV heads: eight for Llama 3 8B). The all-to-all itself, though, does not overlap with compute — attention cannot start on a partial sequence, so every chip waits for data from every other chip before any of them begins.

P2P (ring attention): Ulysses' ceiling is the head count; ring attention removes it by never leaving the token axis. Keys and values never materialize in full. KV chunks travel around a ring of chips one hop at a time, neighbor to neighbor. Each chip attends its queries to the chunk it holds while the next one is in flight, and merges the partial results as it goes. A chip holds its own chunk and one visiting chunk, never the full sequence, and the communication overlaps compute. However, dividing attention computation into smaller chunks can reduce local kernel efficiency, and unlike All-to-All Ulysses, ring attention requires explicit load balancing.

Unified sequence parallelism composes them on two axes of the chip mesh, so the context-parallel degree becomes the product of the two: All-to-All along one axis, sized at or below the KV-head count, and a ring along the other to extend past it. Because each All-to-All group has already pooled its tokens, a ring step now carries a chunk larger by the All-to-All factor, and the ring is correspondingly shorter — more compute per hop to hide the transfer behind, and fewer hops to accumulate latency and causal imbalance. On Cloud TPU the two map onto two axes of the slice's chip mesh.

These strategies cover the attention variants in today's major models, from multi-head and grouped-query attention to DeepSeek-style multi-head latent attention.

Splitting the sequence only pays off if the attention compute on each chip stays efficient. All four strategies run on Splash Attention, the block-sparse flash attention kernel from Tokamax, Google's open-source library of TPU kernels. It skips fully masked blocks and handles packed sequences without padding. P2P calls it once per ring step, on the local queries and the KV chunk that has just arrived, while the transfer for the next step runs underneath, in the forward and backward pass alike. Each chip does the same fused, block-level work it would do without context parallelism, with the communication hidden behind it.

P2P (Ring Attention) is the primary workhorse for very long-context training, whereas All-Gather is the standard baseline for most other cases where the KV can fit. These are the primary CP strategies to use in practice. We compare how far each can push context length on a fixed 64-chip TPU 5p slice with Llama 3.1 8B (256 head dim, 16 query heads) , and measure their MFUs. In ideal settings where both strategies can have large enough chunks (S/CP) and local batch size - the two major variables driving MFU on TPU 5p, we observe that P2P can outperform All-Gather almost every context length from 64K to 14M tokens, where communication becomes mostly hidden with the Ring circular transport schedule.

#10324. 5 Things Most Developers Get Wrong When Turning Images into HTML

Turning a design image into a working webpage can look simple.

You have a picture (a mockup, a screenshot, a Figma export), and your job is to turn it into working HTML in a browser.

But here is the catch. A design image is static, while a webpage is dynamic and responsive across different screen sizes and devices. A webpage needs to load quickly, adapt perfectly to different screen sizes, and allow touch interactions on mobile devices.

Most mistakes in this process happen when the conversion is treated as copying a picture instead of rebuilding it.

This section walks you through the mistakes developers often make, and more importantly, how to fix each of them.

Whether you are slicing a client mockup or converting a screenshot into code, avoiding these mistakes can save you from hours of rework. Let’s get into the details.

A screenshot reflects how a page appears at a particular display. But it doesn’t show how the layout will behave when the content changes or the screen gets smaller.

Developers sometimes try to copy the exact positions and dimensions of every element. To match the screenshot, they may use fixed widths, large margins, and absolute positioning.

This can work at one screen size, but the layout may break on smaller screens or when a heading wraps onto an extra line.

The better approach is identifying the layout rules behind the design. CSS Grid can arrange the cards in columns. Flexbox can help align elements within each card. And media queries can adjust the layout for smaller screens.

Pixel-level comparison can be useful in some cases, but it should not come at the cost of a flexible layout.

When developers recreate a design, it’s easier for them to focus on shapes and spacing rather than the purpose of each element. So, they may use generic elements for headings, buttons, navigation areas, and content sections instead of choosing elements that reflect each element's purpose.

This may produce the right appearance, but it often makes the code harder to maintain and understand. And when developers fail to use the right HTML elements, it can also create accessibility problems later.

So don’t put everything inside several unnamed containers. Instead, use a clear structure with appropriate headings, meaningful alt text for informative images, and real buttons for actions.

Semantic elements (e.g., header, nav, main, section, and footer) can also help organize a large page when they match the content’s purpose.

A design image may have headings, descriptions, prices, labels, or button text.

Reusing the entire image to reproduce the original design feels quick. But it can create problems when that text has to work as part of a responsive web page.

For example, there’s a promotional banner that contains a heading, a short description, and a CTA button. If you use the entire banner as one image, you can’t easily change the heading, translate the text, or make the button work as a real element.

So, don’t treat the image like that. Instead, use the original image where appropriate and recreate the text and button in HTML. CSS can control their position, size, color, and spacing.

Make sure you distinguish between visual elements that should remain images and text that should be recreated as HTML.

Not every part of turning images into HTML needs to be done manually.

Developers sometimes spend too much time on recreating basic elements when there are free online conversion tools available. These tools can help them get an initial version much faster.

An online conversion tool may be helpful when you need to extract text or generate a rough starting layout from a design image.

For example, you can upload a design image into an online image to html converter to get a starting point (rather than starting from a blank file). Review the tool’s output. Identify the parts you can reuse (as they are) and tweak the parts that need adjustments.

Keep in mind that the first result generated by the tool isn’t the final code. You may need to reorganize the HTML, refine the CSS, and adjust the layout to make it responsive.

Use automation where it saves time and your own judgement where it matters.

A screenshot shows just one view of a page. It doesn’t show how the layout may change on a phone or how people using a keyboard/screen reader can interact with it.

There’s also a chance of accessibility problems when images lack suitable alt text/interactive elements.

Let’s say a page has three cards in a row. On the desktop, this arrangement may work well. But on a phone, you may need to stack the cards vertically so their content remains readable.

That’s why accessibility should be considered during the same process. Use meaningful headings and provide appropriate alt text for images. Also, maintain sufficient text contrast and make sure interactive elements can be operated with a keyboard.

Lastly, don’t forget to test the page on both desktop and mobile screen sizes before finalizing it.

HTML and CSS handle the structure and appearance of a webpage, but JavaScript makes it possible to add interactive features.

#10316. Retail Merchandising Principles for Web Designers and Developers: Display Design, Ranking Rules and Measurement

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Every e-commerce grid, product page and checkout screen is a shelf. Merchandisers have spent decades working out what to put at eye level, how many choices to show and how to tell whether a display works, and web teams are now rebuilding the same logic in code: sort orders, pinned products, badges, filters and A/B tests. Knowing where those ideas came from helps you build them with fewer mistakes.

This article maps physical retail merchandising onto digital product design. It covers visual hierarchy and choice load in listing UIs, a small rule-based ranking function you can test, accessible price and badge design with a contrast checker, and a significance test for layout experiments. It also covers why good plans fail at the handoff between design, engineering and operations. The numbers in the examples are illustrative, so use your own data. The code was run with Node.js 22.

Merchandising shapes how products are displayed, promoted and made accessible across sales channels. A well-planned strategy considers visual hierarchy, packaging, promotional materials, inventory timing and consistency between locations. For example, a skincare product may struggle to convert if testers are missing, shelf information is unclear or displays arrive after a campaign begins. If you build for the web, nearly every one of these problems has a direct counterpart:

Customers scan before they read. Nielsen Norman Group defines visual hierarchy as the arrangement of elements so the eye moves through them in order of importance, and names color and contrast, scale and grouping as the main tools. On a shelf, a larger facing or a contrasting background makes the hero product visible first. In a product grid, the equivalents are a larger card for a featured item, a clear boundary around a bundle, and a restrained use of badges so that only one or two items carry emphasis.

A common mistake is giving every card a badge, a color and a call to action. When everything is emphasized, nothing is, and the page reads like a crowded table in a store. Treat emphasis as a limited resource and have the design system say how many emphasized cards one screen may show.

A premium item placed beside entry-level alternatives may lose perceived value, and a crowded display can make fresh products look less appealing. Hick’s Law states that the time to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices, and its practical advice is to reduce options when speed matters and to highlight recommended ones. In a listing UI that suggests a curated first screen with a clear sort label, a few high-confidence recommendations and filters that handle the long tail, rather than an unbroken wall of products. Hick’s Law is a guide, not a rule, so treat it as a hypothesis to test on your own audience.

Merchandisers often ask for things like “pin the new range to the top, boost this collection, never show more than two items from one brand in a row, and push sold-out items down”. Implemented as scattered CSS order values and one-off queries, these requests become hard to audit. A small, pure function keeps the rules explicit and testable.

A real system would also log which rule moved each item, so that someone can answer why a product appears where it does.

Price cards and shelf labels are text, and text needs contrast. The WCAG contrast requirement asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Product cards are a place where this slips, because grey secondary prices and pastel sale badges look refined in a mockup. A useful display respects the realities of the sales environment: floor space, customer movement, staff workload, replenishment access and how much information shoppers can absorb at a glance. The digital version is the gap between the design file and what ships. Test the setup with the people who will use it, not only with the team that created it, because their feedback reveals practical problems before a wide rollout.

A polished plan can fail in practice when materials arrive late, displays are assembled incorrectly or teams miss replenishment. On the web, the matching failures are a promotion that goes live before its product data is synced, a badge that stays on after a sale ends, and a hero banner that links to a discontinued item. Execution needs coordination across marketing, suppliers, logistics and engineering, with clear instructions, realistic schedules, display standards and checks. Some companies outsource this kind of field merchandising. One provider’s services page, for example, lists planogram and retail resets, end-cap displays, signage placement, on-shelf availability, in-store audits and visit reporting. Whether or not you hire help, that list is a useful checklist of what execution involves, and most items have a digital twin you can automate: availability checks, expiry dates on promotions and audit reports. Treat any performance figures on vendor pages as the vendor’s own claims and ask for evidence before relying on them.

Timing matters most for seasonal promotions and limited windows. A campaign that goes live after peak demand has passed cannot deliver the same value, so give promotional content a start and end date in the data model and have a scheduled job remove it, rather than relying on someone to remember.

Sales alone cannot tell you whether visibility, placement, stockouts, confusing information or poor setup caused a result. For a web listing, the answer is a controlled experiment. A strong merchandising operation does not require a large budget. A simple guide for product priorities, layout, pricing, replenishment and approved materials keeps execution consistent, and it is the physical version of a design system: a small set of approved components and rules that make the correct result the easy path. For a product card, that means a documented component with a fixed set of badge types, a defined maximum of emphasized items per screen and tokens that already pass the contrast check.

Consistency also protects trust. Damaged fixtures, inconsistent packaging, missing prices and outdated signs make good products look poorly managed, and the digital equivalents are broken images, stale prices and mismatched labels between the listing and the product page. Aim for consistency, not identical pages: a mobile grid and a kiosk screen can differ, but they should share product priorities, visual identity and core messages.

Temporary and in-store selling spaces demand quick communication. Visitors move fast, attention is limited, and interfaces must be clear from a distance and forgiving of mistakes. If you build kiosk, tablet or point-of-sale software, these constraints shape the interface:

When results disappoint, audit the real experience before commissioning a redesign. Think like a first-time customer: is the offer clear, are premium items distinct and can the team maintain the page easily? Government small-business guidance offers concrete analogues. The Queensland Government’s retail display guidance recommends showing essential information and prices so customers can decide quickly, placing popular or profitable items at eye level, and refreshing window displays every two to four weeks. The Westminster City Council guide similarly links visual merchandising, layout and planned promotions to attracting customers and encouraging them to spend more time and money in a business.

Prioritize visibility, clarity, stock accuracy and consistent execution before investing in a full redesign. Small operational improvements can make a meaningful difference, especially when they remove obstacles that repeatedly interrupt the buying process.

The public guidance cited here was written for general small-business use, and UX principles such as visual hierarchy and Hick’s Law come from interface research, so they are useful analogies rather than guarantees. The code is a teaching sketch: it ignores personalization, pagination, caching, search relevance and many other concerns of a production ranking system. Results vary with category, audience and device, so test changes in your own setting and treat any benchmark as a starting point.

What is merchandising in e-commerce? It is how products are displayed, ordered, promoted and made accessible across a site or app, including layout, labels, badges, sort rules and inventory handling.

How does visual hierarchy apply to a product grid? The same tools apply: contrast, scale and grouping decide what the eye sees first and which products read as related.

What should I measure first? Start with conversion, add-to-cart rate and stock availability for the listing, then add average order value and revenue per visitor once the basics are tracked.

How do I know a layout test is reliable? Fix the sample size in advance, randomize users, run it long enough to cover weekly patterns and check significance, for example with the two-proportion test above.

Do small teams need a big design system? No. A short component guide with a few badge types, spacing rules and accessible color tokens is enough to keep pages consistent.

Merchandising is applied interface design with a long history. Clear hierarchy, a curated first screen, readable prices, explicit ranking rules and controlled experiments carry over directly from the shelf to the screen. For designers and developers, the practical lesson is that a good layout only counts if it ships correctly, stays current and is measured. Audit the basics, put the rules in code, test one change at a time and record what you learn.

#10301. Sizing GPU Memory for a RAG Service: KV Cache, Context and Concurrency

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) app is usually drawn as a language model plus a vector database. When it goes live, the part that fails first is rarely the retrieval. It is GPU memory on the generation model, because a model that loads fine for one request can run out of memory when several users send long questions at once.

This article shows how to estimate that limit before you deploy, with a short script you can run, and then covers what else in the pipeline can slow responses down.

Serving a model takes more than its weights. The GPU also holds:

the key-value (KV) cache, which grows with the length of every active request

memory used by the inference runtime itself

SitePoint's guide to running a local LLM on 10GB of VRAM makes the same point for a single desktop GPU: the usable budget is the total VRAM minus overhead, and the KV cache grows with the context window, so the real model budget is smaller than the number on the box.

For a RAG service, the KV cache matters even more. Retrieved passages are pasted into the prompt, so every request carries a long context, and every concurrent request holds its own cache.

The cache size per token depends on the model's architecture: two tensors (keys and values) for every layer, for every KV head. This script turns those numbers into a rough limit on concurrent requests:

def kv_bytes_per_token(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, dtype_bytes=2):

# Keys and values, for every layer, for one token.

return 2 *layers *kv_heads *head_dim *dtype_bytes

The numbers are estimates. Real runtimes add their own overhead, and the architecture values come from the model's configuration file, so read them from the model you actually use. The pattern is what matters: doubling the context length halves the number of requests that fit. A RAG prompt that stuffs in ten retrieved chunks costs real capacity.

Serving runtimes do not simply crash when the cache is full. The vLLM documentation on optimization and tuning explains that when KV cache space is insufficient for all batched requests, the engine preempts some of them and recomputes them later, which can hurt end-to-end latency. Its suggested remedies include giving the cache more memory, reducing the number of concurrent sequences or batched tokens, or sharding the model across GPUs.

So the symptom of undersizing is often not an error. It is slow, uneven responses under load. Test with realistic prompt lengths and several simultaneous users, not a single request.

Embedding and ingestion have a different profile from generation. Embedding is often lighter than serving a large language model, and some teams run it on CPU. Document parsing, chunking and tokenization also run on CPU, so a fast GPU cannot make up for a slow CPU upstream. SitePoint's tutorial on a local RAG system for private documents walks through chunking, embeddings, vector storage and reranking, and is a good map of which stages run where.

RAM: vector indexes, document loaders and caches all live in system memory, so large collections need more of it.

Storage: model files, indexes, logs and backups add up. Fast storage shortens model loading and re-indexing.

Network: if the vector database, application server and model server sit in different places, latency adds to every request.

Two layouts are common. A single instance runs everything and is simplest for prototypes and internal tools. Separated services put ingestion and retrieval on smaller machines and keep the GPU for generation, which scales better as traffic grows.

Memory estimates only hold if the GPU memory is really yours. On a shared or time-sliced GPU, other workloads can take memory and compute away from your service, which makes capacity hard to plan. Hosting providers describe this in different terms, such as passthrough, vGPU, MIG and time-slicing. For a view of how one GPU hosting provider explains those allocation methods, GPU Mart's GPU virtualization guide compares them. It is a hosting company's page, so confirm any provider's claims about isolation and performance with your own load test.

Whatever provider you choose, ask these questions:

Is the physical GPU assigned to you exclusively, or partitioned or time-sliced?

Do you have root access to install your own drivers, CUDA libraries and containers?

Which minimum commitments, regions and support terms apply?

A small stack can run as a handful of containers: an API service for request handling and prompt assembly, an ingestion worker, a vector database and a model server. Keeping them separate lets you monitor each one's memory and CPU use and move the heavy one to bigger hardware without rewriting the rest.

For a service that stays up all month, a fixed monthly term is easier to forecast than hourly capacity. Hourly capacity suits testing quantization settings or processing a one-off document batch.

Is GPU memory always the bottleneck for RAG?

Not always. Generation usually hits memory first, but slow parsing, a small CPU or a distant vector database can dominate response time.

Does quantization solve the memory problem?

It shrinks the weights, but the KV cache still grows with context length and concurrency. Test with your real prompt sizes.

It depends on volume. Small workloads can run on CPU, while a large ingestion job may benefit from a GPU for a limited time.

Size a RAG service for the worst realistic moment, not the single-request demo. Estimate the KV cache from the model's architecture, remember that long retrieved contexts reduce how many requests fit, watch for preemption under load, keep CPU, RAM, storage and network in the plan, and find out whether the GPU is exclusive or shared. The script above is small, but it turns a vague worry about memory into numbers you can check.
