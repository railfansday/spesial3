Most Kafka deployments in the industry plan to survive the loss of a single Kafka broker or an availability zone. But that's not enough for critical Tier-0 infrastructure where downtime or data loss of even a few seconds could have a massive business impact.

In such scenarios, the failure model should consider not just the loss of a broker or a zone, but the loss of an entire region. The business is expected to operate without interruption even when a whole region goes down, and it should be possible to also roll the cluster safely while operating in that state, to apply any break-glass changes.

But what actually happens inside Kafka when an entire region goes down? What happens to the replicas, producer writes, and KRaft quorum? And what happens when that region comes back?

Let's walk through these scenarios using a Tier-0 multi-region Kafka architecture.

For a Tier-0 multi-region Kafka cluster, we need three different regions with isolated failure domains and a minimum of two brokers in each region.

Brokers in each region should be configured with rack awareness to indicate their failure domains as follows:

For Tier-0 workloads, each topic's partitions should be configured to have a replication factor (RF) of 6. Kafka's rack-aware replica assignment will spread the replicas across the three regions. If the automatic replica assignment doesn't allocate two replicas per region, we have to explicitly reassign replicas immediately after topic creation to meet our availability guarantees.

This way, if one region goes down entirely, we still have four replicas surviving and the cluster would still continue to operate.

But just running brokers in multiple regions is not the solution here. In fact, that's the easy part. The hard question is, what guarantee remains when one of those regions disappears, and does it solve the business needs?

The two brokers in that region become unavailable, leaving four surviving replicas across Regions B and C.

For any partitions whose leaders were in Region A, Kafka needs to elect new leaders from the surviving in-sync replicas.

But having four surviving replicas alone does not determine whether Kafka will continue accepting writes.

The next step is to define what is considered a successful write in this Kafka setup. For this, we need two configs:

min.insync.replicas determines the minimum number of replicas that should be present in the in-sync replica set (ISR) of a partition for Kafka to accept writes when producers use acks=all.

acks=all is a producer-side config that ensures that the producer will get an acknowledgment only when the write has been successfully replicated to the required in-sync replicas.

These two settings work together to satisfy the durability guarantees we want.

When all three regions are healthy, ISR would include all six replicas. Let's say one region goes down, then the ISR would drop down to four.

Since minISR is three, writes will still continue to work.

There may be temporary interruptions while Kafka detects the failure, elects new leaders, and clients reconnect to them. But once the surviving leaders are available, the cluster can continue accepting writes as long as the ISR requirements are met.

The goal isn't availability at any cost, but rather the system should be available as long as the durability guarantees that we designed for can be maintained.

Now let's make the scenario more interesting.

Region A is still down. We have four replicas surviving across Regions B and C.

What if we need to roll the cluster one broker at a time, to apply any break-glass changes?

During that time, the ISR can go down from four to three. But since minISR is three, writes will still continue to work.

Let's look at how the cluster behaves under different failure scenarios.

Kafka will stop accepting writes only when the surviving ISR drops below three.

This is an important distinction for Tier-0 workloads.

It's not sufficient to design a cluster that survives a region failure. We also need to consider whether the cluster can continue operating safely while already in that degraded state.

So far, we focused on the data plane. Now switching gears, the KRaft controllers have a completely different availability model.

They form the metadata quorum, and a majority is needed to continue operation.

For our setup, five controllers could be distributed as 2 + 2 + 1 across the three regions, requiring a majority of three to maintain quorum.

If either of the regions containing two controllers goes down, three controllers remain. If the region containing one controller goes down, four remain.

So, the controller quorum survives the loss of any one region.

But, unlike brokers, we cannot necessarily roll the controllers at that point because taking one controller down could cause the quorum to drop below three.

With a static quorum, one way to solve this is to use more controllers, and a 3 + 3 + 3 distribution would provide this. Losing one region would leave six controllers with room to still roll the controllers, as we only need five to maintain quorum.

On the flip side, an increase in quorum size also increases the coordination overhead. The official Kafka documentation typically recommends three or five controllers, which tolerate one or two concurrent failures respectively.

Alternatively, with dynamic KRaft quorum (KIP-853), introduced in Kafka 3.9, we can add or remove controllers from the voting quorum without restarting the cluster.

For example, with our 2 + 2 + 1 setup, if a region containing two controllers goes down, the remaining three controllers can still maintain quorum. We can then remove the failed controllers from the voting membership, one at a time, reducing the quorum size to three. This allows us to roll one of the surviving controllers while still maintaining the new majority of two.

However, dynamic quorum reconfiguration is not automatic. The surviving controllers must be able to maintain quorum and successfully commit the membership changes before we can proceed with the controller restart.

You need to consider whether you want the same availability guarantee for the controllers as that of the data plane, and whether dynamic quorum reconfiguration during a regional outage is acceptable for your Tier-0 workloads.

So far, we assumed that a region either works or goes down completely.

But the WAN itself becomes a failure mode.

In this setup, a partly working WAN between two regions can be more dangerous than a link that's completely down.

Increased latency, packet loss, or intermittent connectivity issues between regions can cause replication to lag, ultimately resulting in replicas falling out of ISR.

The three regions might be independently healthy, but if the connectivity between them is not, then the availability of the cluster might be impacted.

Going back to the CAP theorem, to tolerate a network partition, we must choose between consistency and availability.

So, if a partition's ISR falls below the configured min.insync.replicas of three due to a network partition, Kafka stops accepting writes with acks=all rather than weakening those guarantees.

Similarly, if the KRaft controller quorum is lost, then no metadata changes, like leader elections, can happen in the cluster, even though existing data-plane traffic may continue for some time.

The availability guarantees we discussed also come with their own costs.

The first cost is client latency. With cross-region replication and acks=all, the network latency between these regions directly affects producer latency. Consumers can also pay the WAN latency cost when consuming from partition leaders located in a different region.

The second cost is replication overhead. With RF=6, there is additional storage, network traffic, and replication-related coordination cost in the brokers compared to a smaller number of replicas.

So, in an architecture like this, monitoring the health of the network paths between these regions is as critical as monitoring the Kafka brokers and controllers.

Metrics like round-trip latency (RTT) between regions, packet loss, TCP retransmissions, connection errors/timeouts, and network throughput/bandwidth utilization are a good start.

When one region goes down, all the partition leaders in that region would move to the two other surviving regions.

This implies that those two regions would have to handle more producer and consumer traffic, network throughput, and disk I/O.

Even if the cluster is designed to satisfy its ISR and controller quorum requirements, if it's not sized to handle these failures when one region goes down, the surviving regions would be overloaded, resulting in higher latencies, timeouts, and operational instability.

So, for Tier-0 infrastructure, capacity planning should always be done for failure states and not stop at normal operations.

Now let's say Region A comes back online.

The brokers in the recovered region might have to catch up on a significant amount of data before they become fully in sync again.

This can result in a sudden burst of cross-region network traffic and disk I/O across all regions.

Region recovery needs to be controlled so that it doesn't overwhelm the healthy part of the cluster.

This can be achieved by throttling the replica catch-up, with the trade-off of longer recovery time to minimize the impact on live production traffic.

Replication throttling needs a deeper discussion of its own, so we won't go into details here.

The important point is that bringing a failed region back online is not simply a matter of restarting its brokers. We need to make sure that the recovery process itself doesn't impact the business.

There is no shortage of ways to deploy Kafka across multiple regions. The challenge here is to pick the right architecture that addresses all our durability guarantees.

For Tier-0 workloads, this means designing the architecture around failures first.

What happens when a region goes down? Can we still roll the cluster? What continues to work, and at what point do we deliberately stop accepting writes?

Lastly, can we safely bring back the region that was down without impacting the business?

That is ultimately the difference between simply running Kafka across multiple regions versus actually supporting a mission-critical Tier-0 workload.

Every gold calculator comes down to three inputs: how much the item weighs, how much of that weight is actually gold, and what pure gold is trading for today.

Purity is measured in karats, out of 24. A 14K ring is 14 parts gold and 10 parts other metals, so it's 14 ÷ 24 = 58.3% gold. You'll also see 14K stamped as "585", which is the hallmark many manufacturers use for the same alloy. For a calculator, the karat fraction is the safer default:

The second detail most tutorials miss is the unit. Gold spot prices are quoted in USD per troy ounce, which is 31.1035 grams, not the 28.35-gram ounce on a kitchen scale. Jewelers also use pennyweight (dwt), which is 1.5552 grams. If your calculator multiplies grams by a per-ounce price without converting, every result is wrong by a factor of 31.

That gap is why good production tools separate the two numbers. We sent 1,000 streaming calls at the same moment through three self-hosted LLM gateways: the Langfuse AI Gateway, the LiteLLM proxy and AcruxCore. Each gateway was limited to 2 CPUs. As shipped, Langfuse finished 261 of the 1,000 streams and LiteLLM finished 403. Neither failure came from the gateway's own code.

Langfuse's failures came from Linux's open-file limit, which Docker sets to 1,024 by default. LiteLLM had that problem too, plus a second one: an empty key cache sent hundreds of database lookups at once, and the ones that waited longer than 10 seconds were rejected. This article shows how each failure looked, how we traced it to its cause, and the settings that fixed it.

This benchmark measured the resource consumption of three LLM gateways under scaling concurrent streaming loads.

Full latency, memory, and feature results are available in the Langfuse vs LiteLLM vs AcruxCore benchmark.

First-token times are medians of the streams that succeeded. Up to 250 streams, every gateway succeeded. Then the errors started, and they did not look alike:

A 502 and a 503 point at different layers, so we expected two different causes. Two of the three turned out to share one cause.

Linux counts every network connection as an open file, and it caps how many open files one process may hold. The cap has two values. The soft limit is the cap that is in force now. The hard limit is the ceiling: a process may raise its own soft limit up to that value, and no further.

A streaming gateway holds two connections for each stream: one from the client, and one to the model provider. That makes about 1,000 open files at 500 streams, and about 2,000 at 1,000 streams. A gateway also needs a few more files for its database, its logs and its own libraries.

Docker on our machine gave every container a soft limit of 1,024 and a hard limit of 524,288. All three gateways received the same two numbers. What differed was how each program treats them when it starts:

Node.js raises its own soft limit to the hard limit at startup. The Rust and Python services keep 1,024, and neither project's Compose file sets a higher value. So the two gateways ran out of open files near 500 streams, which is the point where the errors started.

The out-of-files error appeared as a failed connection, not as a clear message. The Langfuse gateway's logs showed failed connections to OpenAI, and also failed connections to Langfuse's own web service, which the gateway asks to check each key. Both come from one cause: a process that cannot open another file cannot open another socket. Its client then receives a 502.

Raise the limit for the gateway service in its Compose file. We used 65,536 for all three gateways:

The same ulimits block works on the LiteLLM service. On Kubernetes or another platform, check /proc//limits inside a running container rather than assuming a value.

With the open-file limit raised, the Langfuse gateway finished all 1,000 streams. LiteLLM still returned 503 errors. To separate the two causes, we ran LiteLLM with one worker and only the open-file limit raised. All 500 streams succeeded in that ladder, but 595 of 1,000 failed with 503.

The 503 message names the authentication database, so we read LiteLLM's logs and its source. The log gave the same reason for all 840 failed streams in these runs, 595 in that ladder and 245 in the cold-cache runs below:

When 500 calls arrive while the key is not in the cache, each call looks the key up in Postgres. All of those lookups queue for 10 connections. The worker was also using its whole CPU allowance at this point, which slows every query it sends and receives. We infer that this CPU load lengthens the queue, but we did not measure it separately. Whatever the share of each, the lookups at the back of the queue miss the 10-second deadline.

If that explanation is right, the failures should depend on whether the key is already in the cache. So we ran 500 streams four more times. Three runs started after 75 seconds with no calls, which is longer than the 60-second cache. One run started right after a single warm-up call:

The warm run had no failures. The cold runs failed by different amounts, 161, 84 and 0, because the result depends on how many lookups miss the deadline, and that changes from run to run. This probably explains why the earlier ladder succeeded at 500 and failed at 1,000. A run is a race between the lookup queue and the deadline, so a single ladder does not show where the limit is.

The warm-up result is the one we measured. Send one call with each virtual key after LiteLLM starts, and avoid quiet periods longer than 60 seconds before a traffic spike. A steady flow of traffic keeps the key in the cache.

We raised the open-file limit to 65,536 for all three gateways and gave LiteLLM 2 workers, one per CPU, as its production guide recommends for a single machine. Then we ran the full ladder again:

The open-file errors were gone for every gateway, and the Langfuse gateway gave the fastest first token of the three. LiteLLM still returned 120 × 503 at 500. That level started about 60 seconds after the first call of the ladder, so the cached key had probably just expired. All 1,000 succeeded at the next level, which started within 60 seconds of the 500 level.

Once nothing failed, CPU decided the first-token times. LiteLLM used its whole 2-CPU allowance from 100 streams on, and its median first token took 19.2 seconds at 1,000 streams. AcruxCore also used both CPUs from 100 streams on, and its first token at 1,000 streams was 3.8 s at the median and 9.7 s at p95. The Langfuse gateway peaked at 1.65 CPUs and 168 MiB, with a first token of 1.7 s at the median and 9.6 s at p95.

Each gateway also needs the rest of its stack, and that stack uses CPU too. While the Langfuse gateway served 500 streams, its whole stack used up to 3.1 CPUs, because the web service checked keys and the worker and ClickHouse stored usage. AcruxCore's whole stack peaked at 4.0 CPUs, because its API writes a trace for every call to Postgres. Size the stack around the gateway, not only the gateway container.

#10421. The Lenovo Legion Tab Gen 5 would be my perfect Steam tablet if it didn’t cost $900

For every Android-powered slate I’ve used, only Nvidia’s Shield Tablet really stands out in my memory. Part of the company’s occasional (and all-but-dead) dip into hardware products, the Shield Tablet — and its revitalized K1 follow-up, which I also owned through a recall program on the original — carried me through the back half of college, frequently keeping my Chromebook in my backpack in lieu of a mini Bluetooth keyboard or the Shield Tablet’s built-in stylus. Its front-firing speakers made it great for movie-watching, and while I’m not much of a mobile gamer, I did find it to be a great device for emulation, especially when paired with an external controller.

Even if Nvidia hadn’t abandoned the hardware game at the end of the last decade, it’s found new and much more destructive ways to make piles of cash at the expense of your easy access to RAM. And considering the market for Android tablets in general, it’s not all too surprising to see very few companies trying to make headway into offering a miniature, gaming-focused tablet that can easily be thrown in a backpack. Lenovo, however, has managed to fill its spot quite well. 9to5Google’s own Ben Schoon found himself smitten with the Gen 3 model nearly two years ago, and now, its Gen 5 successor has found its way into my nostalgic hands.

But expectations around mobile experiences have come a long way since 2016. Whether the Legion Tab Gen 5 can win over your heart really depends on your patience with some old-school Android jank — and whether your wallet can withstand an exceptionally-high asking price. Still, with recent advancements in PC emulation, it’s hard to say Lenovo isn’t onto something.

Considering the decade between their respective releases, I’m actually quite surprised just how much this tablet reminds me of the original Shield Tablet. Its 8.8-inch 16:10 LCD panel combined with a boxy matte-black design and a near-identical weight meant that I immediately felt at home holding this thing in my hands. You lose out on a couple of clutch features — most notably the built-in stylus — but largely speaking, this feels like the correct size and shape for a miniature gaming tablet.

Still, it’s not all perfect. Take, for example, the completely unnecessary camera bump that prevents this slate from properly laying flat on a desk. No one is buying this thing to use the camera, and judging by my very limited experience trying it out, I’m fairly confident whatever potato sensor is shoved in here would fit fine flush with the chassis. I’m pretty fed up with OEMs leaning on camera bumps to make any device look or feel more premium than it actually is, and that goes double quadruple for tablets like this.

Just like its predecessor, this thing still picks up smudges and fingerprints at an unreasonable level, its matte-black finish unable to hide anything. 360 grams is also a little heavier than I’d expect for a slate of this size, though to be fair, it’s much more noticeable in portrait than landscape. Holding the tablet with two hands to watch a movie or make my way through another round of Balatro made its top-heaviness much less noticeable.

This display is sharp and fast — with one minor caveat — and reasonably bright in all circumstances except direct sunlight thanks to its 600 nits of brightness (and 800 in HBM). Really, it’s the LCD of it all that gets me here; this thing is nearly $1,000, and the lack of OLED alone makes it difficult to recommend at this price. It’s not an outright bad panel, but I found its contrast levels to be pretty lacking, with most blacks only ever showing up as dark grays on-screen.

Oh, and that caveat: On two different occasions, I caught the tablet stuck in 60Hz mode, regardless of what refresh rate option I selected in settings. Switching back and forth between modes a few times eventually unstuck it, but it’s definitely a bug worth being aware of.

I think I’d feel a lot better about recommending this to someone looking for a content consumption device if this screen looked a little more premium. Your mileage may vary though — if you can get your hands on a demo unit before buying one, if only to see the display, I’d recommend it. For everyone else, this price tag might have you waiting for the OLED panel rumored to be arriving on next year’s successor.

At $900 — or potentially more, considering this tablet actually launched at $850 — the Legion Tablet’s hardware leaves something to be desired. Biometrics are the most obvious weak spot; camera-based face unlock is the only biometric option provided here, without a fingerprint scanner in sight. I wouldn’t blame some if they saw this as a dealbreaker, because even if you’re willing to put up with the security concerns that a basic front-facing camera brings to the table, I can promise you you’ll be typing the code in all the time. Whether you’re trying to unlock in a darker environment or it’s just been four hours since you last unlocked the slate, make sure you have your pin memorized.

If you’re primarily buying this as a content consumption machine, I think you’ll mostly have a great time. Lenovo built stereo sound into this slate, but did so in one of the smartest ways I’ve ever seen, ensuring all speakers are located to the right side along the bottom and top of the device. This might sound a little unbalanced in portrait mode, but in landscape, it guarantees you’re never actually covering up the speakers while holding the slate in your hands, something practically every other equivalent tablet is guilty of. No headphone jack here, unfortunately, something I would’ve liked to have seen on a gaming-focused device.

I find battery life genuinely difficult to judge on tablets, especially if you don’t pick it up every single hour of every single day, but I’ve been impressed overall with the Legion Tab’s longevity. Some weekend YouTube binge watching sessions at relatively high brightness settings left me with plenty of juice in the tank, and its standby time between sessions has been excellent. Obviously, if you’re really pushing your graphical limits on this thing — or if you’re emulating non-native software through something like DroidDeck — you’re going to see a bit of a dip, but combined with fast charging and two USB-C ports for both portrait and landscape access, battery life gets a big thumbs-up from me.

Lenovo’s packed a couple of accessories in the box with this thing, but I wouldn’t get too excited. While it’s nice to see a 68W charger included for those aforementioned fast top-offs, the included folio case is just awful. Not only does the triangle-shaped flap barely stick together to form a proper stand, but one of the corners of my case was loose enough to frequently flex back and forth. Holding the tablet while wrapped in its case just felt bad, in the way that so many bundled cases often feel, and a quick look at Amazon shows that basically no recognizable brand — or unrecognizable, for that matter — has stepped up to offer something better.

The Legion Tablet also includes a screen protector in the box, if that’s how you roll.

Unsurprisingly, the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 in here can handle effectively anything you dare to throw at it. While my mobile games might be limited to more casual, indie fare like Balatro — and, by the by, if you’re still enjoying Balatro a couple of years later, the size and shape of this device makes it perfect — graphically-intense games played fine. Asphalt Legends or the HoYoverse games might not be my cup of tea, but they perform excellently on this device, and compared to the 12-inch tablets companies like Samsung and Amazon have become obsessed with, the display size here actually feels perfect.

We definitely have to talk about emulation, though, because it’s increasingly becoming the main focus of mobile gamers everywhere, and this feels like a tablet perfectly designed for something like DroidDeck. Getting Steam emulation — complete with the Deck’s usual Big Picture mode UI — available as an application on the Legion Tab Gen 5 takes all of two minutes and a handful of taps on your display, and I have to say, I’m pretty shocked at just how impressive the overall experience is right now.

DroidDeck gave me the option to bring my mini gaming tablet experience full-circle. While my Steam library has no shortage of games I think could run without stressing this slate, I wanted to push the envelope a little bit. Persona 3 Reload felt like as good a candidate as any: a modern title that doesn’t necessarily require quick response times and fits virtual controls quite nicely. Plus, most of my gaming time on Nvidia’s tablet a decade ago was spent working through Persona 3 Portable; like poetry, it rhymes. I installed the game, ran it on the Legion Tab, and to my surprise and delight, it was absolutely in a playable state.

Now, there are some caveats here. I did drop the game’s graphics preset to low, and even still, there were some occasional hiccups while running around an early dungeon. The game’s initial intros and disclaimers actually gave the impression the title wouldn’t run at all, but I was able to sneak up on a shadow, use a slash attack to begin the battle — basically the only thing in P3R that remotely requires accurate timing — and issue commands to my player character and party mates.

Obviously, your experience emulating Steam titles on the Legion Tab will vary on what you want to play, and with such wide variables between experiences, I probably wouldn’t push most gamers to run out and pick one up right away. It might be early days for DroidDeck, but considering how we’ve already seen it progress, I wouldn’t be surprised to see some buyers picking this up as a Valve-esque take on a gaming tablet.

With all of that power under the hood, though, it’s Lenovo’s take on Android that leaves a lot to be desired. It’s not just that this feels like a rough-and-ready take on AOSP, an unrefined package that would feel more in line with devices from a decade ago, though, yes, that’s half the struggle here. But at its worst, the Legion Tab Gen 5 simply feels unoptimized in daily use.

Scrolling through web pages or various apps often felt sluggish and heavy, like the UI elements on screen never actually match the movement of my finger, and it’s unclear how much of that was due to the refresh rate bug I described above. Auto-rotate was both super finnicky and super sluggish, alternating back and forth between one state or the other, and the auto-rotate button that pops up when it’s disabled would frequently fail to appear. Occasionally, I’d run into app issues, such as choppy playback on a 1440p YouTube video that got so bad — regardless of what resolution I selected — that I had to fully force close the app in settings to fix it. Even getting the slate to reliably wake up (or not wake up) when picking it up was annoying, as it’s simultaneously sensitive enough to frequently turn on when I don’t want it to, or to stay asleep when I’m trying to unlock it. Inevitably, I turned that setting off.

You might think that shelling out $900 for an Android tablet would buy you an out from pre-installed bloatware; you’d be wrong. During setup, the Legion Tab attempts to install a whole host of apps and games, including Perplexity, TikTok, Temu, several match-three games that I’ve truly never heard of, and no less than two mahjong and solitaire titles each. In fact, there might actually be three solitaire titles depending on what “Jigsawcard Solitaire Puzzle” is, but I unchecked all of these apps during setup and didn’t deem the title worthy of bothering to check.

While Lenovo has been trying to improve its software update policies on some recent devices, this slate isn’t among that group. To my knowledge, there is no official update schedule for this device, but based on past performance, I’d expect somewhere around 2 OS upgrades and 4 years of security patches, give or take.

I would’ve loved nothing more than to recommend this year’s Legion Tab to small tablet fans and gamers everywhere, but when this thing is $200 more than Apple’s latest iPad Mini — which in and of itself is absolutely overpriced — and $100 more than the base OLED Steam Deck post-price hike, I just have a hard time telling anyone to buy it. Really, its Steam performance was one of the few areas that made me feel truly excited by this thing, but it’s simply too unpredictable an experience at this point (and price) for me to recommend.

Even setting aside the price, there’s simply too much here that feels unfinished or rough around the edges for my liking. A handful of bug fixes and a couple of additional optimization passes could fix a lot of my more minor quibbles, but despite what I just said, you can only ignore the $900 price tag for so long before it bites you in the ass.

We have seen a post-iPad Mini discount bringing this slate to an Apple-matching $700 ahead of the holidays, but even that price is $150 more than its predecessor was at launch without a whole lot to show for it. If you’re looking for a gamer-friendly tiny tablet, I do think Legion is a brand worth keeping your eye on, especially as DroidDeck and other Steam emulation apps continue to evolve. But at this point — and for this price — your best bet is waiting for whatever model follows this one. It might still be stupid expensive, but at least you’ll be getting OLED.

A decade ago, Nvidia’s Shield Tablet felt like a no-brainer addition to my everyday carry. But in this economy, gamers and tablet fans alike are simply better off dropping $900 elsewhere.

Sprint estimates usually fail because development teams heavily rely on memory and assumptions instead of evidence. They assume similar tasks will take the same amount of time. Based on these half-baked assumptions, most managers plan around ideal sprint working hours. The backlog then fills with commitments that look realistic on paper but often end up delayed.

Actual development rarely follows that plan. There are always unexpected bugs and task dependencies that consume more time than teams account for in their estimates. Even the buffer time set aside for unexpected work may not be enough. This causes tasks to take longer than expected and pushes sprint commitments behind schedule.

Developer time tracking helps teams make better estimates with historical data. It shows how long previous tasks actually took, which provides managers with an evidence-based reference when they are planning similar work.

Reviewing this data before committing to a sprint scope turns estimation from guesswork into an evidence-based process.

Historical time data offers deeper insights, depending on how you collect and analyze it. Most importantly, it explains why planned work took longer than expected.

Before sprint planning, the following records should be reviewed.

Completed task duration: Check the time spent on finished tasks. Managers should compare similar work to understand typical development effort.

Actual versus estimated hours: Management should observe and identify differences between planned and recorded hours. If there are repeated delays, to some extent, it indicates inaccurate estimates.

Carryover work: Review unfinished tasks from previous sprints. Determine whether poor estimates or external delays caused the spillover..

Interruptions: Measure time spent on urgent requests and production issues. These activities reduce the development capacity that you had planned earlier.

Meetings and ceremonies: Review the time being spent on planning sessions and sprint reviews.

Bug-fix time: Examine hours spent resolving defects. You can also compare it to the time for developing new features.

Blocked tasks: Managers should account for the tasks delayed by missing approvals or technical dependencies.

Capacity differences: Compare team availability across sprints. Include leave and staffing changes in that time as well.

These records provide the most value when reviewed together. For example, a task may finish three days late even if the development work stayed within its estimated hours. The actual issue could be an external dependency.

Teams should understand these differences before using historical data to estimate future work.

Historical records provide a useful starting point for estimating new tasks. However, teams must interpret the data rather than simply copy previous estimates.

The following approaches can improve estimation accuracy.

The first step is to consider completed tasks that resemble upcoming work. Managers should compare their requirements and technical complexity. Moreover, the development environment should be considered.

For example, suppose a team needs to estimate a new API integration. Three previous integrations required 12, 15, and 18 hours of active work.

The average is 15 hours. This gives developers a reference point for the new estimate.

However, the average doesn't tell the entire story. The new integration may involve unfamiliar authentication methods or more complicated testing.

Review the reasons behind differences in previous task durations. Check whether developers encountered unexpected bugs or unclear requirements.

As a project manager, I'd consider these details before estimating for the next project. In my experience, they provide more context than an average alone.

Inaccurate estimates can happen; it doesn’t mean that there is a definite planning problem. However, repeated overruns across similar tasks may reveal an underlying issue.

Research suggests the pattern is widespread. A review of software estimation surveys reports that 60–80% of software projects encounter effort overruns, with the average overrun falling between 30% and 40%.

Start by calculating the difference between estimated and actual effort. Here is the formula for it.

Estimation variance (%) = [(Actual hours − Estimated hours) ÷ Estimated hours] × 100

Suppose a task was estimated at 10 hours but needed 14 hours. The actual effort exceeded the estimate by 40%.

You can compare the results across similar tasks.

For example, database migrations may repeatedly require more time than expected. This means that the team might be overlooking data validation or additional testing. Once the cause is clear, adjust future estimates accordingly.

Avoid increasing every estimate by the same percentage. Different types of work involve different risks. Teams can also break large tasks into smaller parts. This makes hidden development activities easier to identify.

The aim is to correct recurring estimation errors without unnecessarily increasing the entire sprint backlog.

Task duration doesn't always reflect the hours spent developing a feature.

Choose a planning assumption that reflects the task's risk and available capacity.

Sprint velocity measures how much work a team has completed during a sprint. It is usually measured in story points. Contrary to this, time-based capacity planning calculates the hours available for planned work. It accounts for meetings and other responsibilities.

Time tracking data shows how much time developers spend on assigned tasks and other responsibilities. Managers can review these records to identify uneven workloads. They can then distribute upcoming tasks based on available capacity.

We've heard your concerns about the speed of A2UI and generative UI experiences: people wait while the model generates each screen. This release gives you levers to make that faster, so you can choose the trade-off that fits each screen.

With this release, we’re happy to introduce swappable inference formats and share examples on leveraging decision models with A2UI, which help building faster and more reliable generative UI experiences. Using our new A2UI Express as an inference format you can reduce latency by up to 3x, and up-to 80x using decision models like Jev to make layout decisions. Express requires up to 3x fewer tokens to generate the same kinds of UI compared to Direct JSON. We'll dive into the details in this blog post.

A2UI is an agent-optimized protocol for generative UI, where a client advertises a catalog of trusted components as a contract, an agent sends A2UI messages describing a bespoke user interface composed from that catalog, and the client's renderer draws the UI (see Introducing A2UI).

Initially agents generated A2UI JSON transport messages directly, every curly brace and nested element. It turns out that generating JSON trees constrained by a complex JSON schema is slow and error-prone for LLMs. This coupled A2UI to LLMs, which output text, and made it difficult to integrate faster System One decision models.

Now agents can generate A2UI Express, an intermediate format optimized for inference speed and accuracy, and rely on deterministic code in A2UI's Agent SDKs to translate it into the JSON-based transport protocol. Agent builders are also free to swap in any other DSL and its associated compiler for any special use-cases, and our A2UI Agent SDK(s) make it compatible with transports and renders.

The format the model generates is what we call inference_format. You set it with one argument when you create the generator. The SDK generates the prompt instructions for that format and compiles the model's reply into standard A2UI messages. This post covers how the swap works, why we developed A2UI Express as the new default inference format, and how a decision model can build screens from layouts you define in advance.

An inference format has two parts, and the SDK generates both from your catalog's JSON schema. A prompt section teaches the model the format's syntax, and a compiler compiles the model's reply into validated A2UI messages. Because both come from the schema, a format can't describe a component or property that your catalog lacks.

A UDP port through which peers communicate.

Separate production and staging network boundaries.

The exact implementation depends on the infrastructure. The key point is that VPN membership and application authorization are different controls.

There are situations where running WireGuard yourself is the more practical option.

You already operate infrastructure. If your team routinely patches servers, manages secrets, monitors services, and responds to incidents, maintaining a VPN may add relatively little work.

If your team already has mature infrastructure practices and needs custom network control, self-hosting may be a sensible choice.

If the team is small, access changes frequently, and nobody has time to own another server, a managed service may be more practical.

The bottleneck didn't disappear. It moved. And the workflow needs to move with it.

When shipping code was slow, the existing design workflow made sense. Multiple rounds of revision, user testing, iteration, all of that fit naturally into the window while engineering caught up. That window is now gone, and most design workflows were never updated to reflect it.

"Founders are asking design teams to move at the same speed as an AI-assisted dev pipeline, and design doesn't compress the same way code does," Khomenko says. The result is a workflow that was built around a constraint that no longer exists, applied to a pipeline that has fundamentally changed around it.

The forced choice between polish and speed that product teams are now navigating isn't new as a concept. What's new is how stark it has become, and how directly it traces back to a workflow problem rather than a people problem. "Because development is cheap and fast now, there's constant pressure to ship the next thing immediately," he explains. "But the products that actually retain users are still the ones where the details were considered carefully. Fewer revision rounds isn't a process improvement if it means shipping avoidable friction."

Khomenko has watched this play out across enough teams, at enough stages, to be specific about where the existing workflow breaks down. The issue isn't design judgment. It's where that judgment sits in the process, and what format it's expected to produce at the end.

To understand Khomenko's proposed fix, it helps to understand exactly where the current workflow stalls. For most product teams, the end of the design process looks like this: a designer produces polished, dev-ready screens inside Figma, and hands that file to engineering. Engineering then interprets those screens, translates the visual decisions into code, and builds from there.

That translation step, Figma file to working code, is where the pipeline now breaks down. AI-assisted engineering has made the building part dramatically faster. But the translation step hasn't changed. An engineer still has to read a Figma file, interpret spacing, extract assets, make judgment calls about component structure, and write the implementation from scratch. That process takes the same amount of time it always did.

"The manual workflow inside Figma simply isn't keeping up with AI-boosted engineering," Khomenko says. "The design handoff is quietly becoming the thing everyone is waiting on."

The result is a pipeline where everything around the handoff has accelerated, and the handoff itself has become the single point of drag. Figma, in other words, was built for a world where engineering was the slow part. It wasn't designed to feed a pipeline where engineering moves at AI speed.

This is the core of what Khomenko is proposing, and it's a more significant structural shift than it might first appear.

In his workflow, Figma doesn't disappear. It moves. Rather than serving as both the exploration environment and the final delivery artifact, Figma becomes the visual exploration layer exclusively: the place where brand identity is established, layout logic is worked through, and design direction is validated. It remains the right tool for figuring out what something should look and feel like.

But the final delivery, the artifact that actually goes to engineering, is code. Specifically, components built into prepared libraries that engineering can work from and scale directly, without any translation step in between.

The difference in implementation speed is the point. When a designer delivers a coded component library rather than a Figma file, engineering doesn't need to interpret anything. The decisions are already expressed in the language they're building in. What previously required an engineer to read, interpret, and re-create from a visual reference now arrives ready to use. In a pipeline running on AI-assisted development, that difference compounds fast.

"Figma is still the right place to explore what something should look and feel like," Khomenko says. "But handing over a Figma file and asking engineering to implement it is now the slow path. Delivering in code is the fast path. That's the shift."

Executing this workflow requires a different kind of designer, and Khomenko is direct about that. The UI/UX designer becoming a Design Engineer is, in his assessment, not a distant possibility. It is a matter of time, and the teams treating it as a future consideration rather than a present reality are the ones most likely to find themselves stuck at the handoff.

But Khomenko is equally clear about what this shift doesn't mean. Delivering in code is one part of the role, not the whole of it. The full design cycle isn't going anywhere. Applying branding to imagery, producing animations, building marketing materials, these remain essential creative tasks that require a broad and varied toolkit well beyond code. "The designer of the near future isn't just generating code," he says. "They're moving across the full cycle, brand, motion, marketing, product, with design engineering as an important and growing part of that stack, not a replacement for it."

What changes is that code-ready delivery becomes a core expectation within that stack. The designer who can move fluidly from visual exploration in Figma to component delivery in code, while also carrying the brand thinking, motion, and marketing creativity that differentiates products at the surface level users actually experience, is the profile that the new workflow demands.

Khomenko is candid that no single version of this workflow has fully standardized yet. Every team is figuring it out differently. The tools are evolving, the handoff expectations are shifting, and the processes teams are building around all of it vary considerably. There is no off-the-shelf playbook.

But this is precisely where his 250+ engagements become more than a credential. They're a dataset. Having watched enough teams, at enough stages, through enough cycles of tooling change, Khomenko can see through the noise of individual variation and identify what's directionally true even when the specifics are still in flux. "Each team's workflow is really custom at this stage," he says. "But some general patterns of how this is going to look in the near future are already visible."

Those patterns consistently point in the same direction: design judgment moving earlier, final delivery moving into code, and the designer's role expanding in technical range while staying broad in creative scope. The exact tools and sequences will keep shifting. The underlying logic of the workflow Khomenko is describing is already taking shape in the teams paying closest attention.

For founders and product leads, the workflow Khomenko is describing has direct implications for how teams are staffed, budgeted, and structured.

Front-load design judgment. The biggest leverage in the new workflow isn't faster execution, it's better problem definition before generation starts. Bring senior design thinking in earlier, when it can shape what gets built rather than polish what's already done.

Separate exploration from delivery. Figma and the visual exploration phase serve a distinct purpose from the final delivery layer. Treat them as separate stages with separate expectations, rather than collapsing them into a single step expected to produce everything.

Shift the delivery expectation to code. Whether that means hiring for Design Engineer range, developing it internally, or restructuring handoffs, the end of the design pipeline needs to arrive in a form engineering can work from directly. The translation step is where time goes to die, and it's now the most avoidable part of the process.

Protect the full creative stack. Design engineering matters more than it ever has. It is not, however, the whole job. Teams that reduce design to code generation will lose the brand thinking, motion, and marketing creativity that differentiates products at the surface level users actually experience.

Stay flexible. The workflow is still forming. Teams that build rigidity into their process now will find themselves redoing that work within a year. The teams getting this right are the ones building enough adaptability into their structures to absorb what's still coming.

"The Figma handoff made sense when engineering was the slow part," Khomenko says. "Engineering isn't the slow part anymore. Delivering in code isn't the future of design workflow. For the teams paying attention, it's already the present."
