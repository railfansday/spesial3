#10245. TPU Model Design Considerations and Optimization Guide

Google designed TPUs from the ground up to be exceptionally efficient for machine learning workloads, delivering top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Because TPUs trade general-purpose hardware scheduling for wide systolic arrays, dedicated SparseCore engines, and custom interconnect fabrics, adhering to specific architectural best practices is essential to achieve maximum performance. To help developers get the most out of TPUs, we are launching the open-source AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign guide. This post highlights its key principles—spanning shape alignment, architectural choices like LatentMoE, and parallelism strategies—to accelerate LLMs, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, and other hardware-intensive workloads on TPUs.

Performance tuning starts with model definition. Architectural choices made before the first training step set permanent ceilings on MFU (Model FLOP Utilization), cost, and serving latency. This guide gives concrete, hardware-grounded design rules for teams building or adapting models for TPUs, organized into three groups:

How to use this guide. The shape rules apply to anyone designing a new model. If you are adapting an existing model rather than designing one, the Expressing Parallelism section applies without touching the checkpoint. The general rules hold for any large-matmul architecture (dense LLMs, multimodal, and world models), and the companion AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign repository grows model- and workload-specific sections over time.

Eighth-generation TPUs. These principles carry forward to TPU 8t and TPU 8i and to the newly announced TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max. The reasoning stays the same and the thresholds move with the hardware. TPU 8t and TPU 8i both add native FP4. For training, TPU 8t also brings more balanced Vector Processing Unit (VPU) scaling. For serving, TPU 8i adds the Boardfly topology, a Collectives Acceleration Engine (CAE), and 3x more on-chip SRAM. We note the effects inline in Principles 5–8, and follow-up posts will cover eighth-generation designs in depth.

Measured evidence. Alongside the published sources, notes marked Measured carry results from our own Ironwood profiling program: open 20–30B MoEs (a Qwen3-30B-derived LatentMoE variant; GPT-OSS-20B) plus dense controls, single slice (except for the 512-chip TPU v7x and 1,024-chip TPU v4 ablations in Principle 4), BF16, training shapes up to 32K sequence, all against tuned-systems baselines. Treat the trends and rankings as transferable, the exact percentages as config-specific.

TPUs are purpose-built custom ML ASICs designed from the ground up to deliver state-of-the-art performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). While their architectural choices—such as wide 256×256 systolic arrays, software-managed VMEM, dedicated SparseCore engines, and direct multi-dimensional ICI fabrics—enable exceptional efficiency, they inherently create specific structural constraints and programming patterns that must be carefully followed to unlock maximal performance. Reaching peak efficiency requires aligning model shapes, routing, and parallelism directly with these underlying hardware characteristics:

Make d_model, FFN and expert intermediate widths (d_ff), latent widths, attention projections, and padded vocabulary sizes (used as contracting or output dimensions) multiples of 256 to match the 256×256 MXU systolic array. Token/batch axes and counts such as heads, layers, and experts are exempt. The most frequently missed dimension is head_dim: at 64 or 128, flash-attention QK products leave the MXU ≥ 50% idle. Reshape to head_dim = 256 with proportionally fewer Q and KV heads, preserving total projection widths and logical KV cache bytes (iso-quality in our ablations).

Measured: Reshaping Qwen3-30B MoE attention from 32 × 128 to 16 × 256 (identical parameters and FLOPs) sped up end-to-end training, with gains widening at longer context:

These rules apply for non-quadratic attention as well, notably GDN ablations from Yang et al. 2025 also noted at par or better quality for head dimension 256 for their Gated DeltaNet Attention.

XLA compiles each program for exact tensor shapes, so every buffer has a fixed address and every loop a known trip count, and a new shape means a recompilation and a host stall. What about dynamic shapes? Dynamic workloads fit this model: the trick is to separate the shape of a buffer from the extent of the data inside it.

Instead of padding to the largest group or the longest sequence, we keep the buffers at fixed shapes (tokens sorted by expert and padded only at the tail, the KV cache as a fixed pool of pages) and describe the ragged structure with a few small integer arrays (group sizes and offsets; KV lengths, the page table, cumulative query lengths). Pallas copies these into the TensorCore's scalar memory before the grid starts (PrefetchScalarGridSpec), and the kernel reads them to choose the next tile and bound its loops, with a mask covering a tile that straddles a group boundary or a partly filled last page. The data dependence becomes an address and a trip count instead of a shape, so the compiled program is the same for every routing and every batch composition and the kernel runs at the unpadded FLOP count.

Standard EP dispatches every routed token at full d_model width, making EP + FSDP slower than plain FSDP in our Ironwood benchmarks. LatentMoE projects routed tokens from d_model down to latent width ℓ (d/ℓ ≈ 2–4×) before dispatch, executes experts in latent space, and projects back up after combine (introduced by Nemotron 3 and adopted by 962B MAI-Base-1 and 2.8T Kimi K3):

Measured: On a 30B / 128-expert MoE, an ℓ = 768 (2.67×) LatentMoE cut on-device permute time 2.8×, lifted end-to-end training throughput +14% tokens/s (322 vs 270 TFLOP/s/chip, or +19%, since the latent projections add ~5% FLOPs per token; tokens stay ℓ-wide past the down-projection, so on-device permute and combine shrink alongside EP all-to-all), and freed ~25 GB/device of activation HBM when remat checkpoints were anchored on the compressed latent. While current MoE recipes in tpu-recipes run FSDP (+ TP), LatentMoE + FP8 dispatch makes EP roofline-viable when per-device batch is too small to hide FSDP weight all-gathers. Under both FSDP and EP, avoid over-fragmenting experts: small tokens × K / N group sizes starve MXU tiles.

When candidate MoE layouts match on quality, pick the one with higher compute intensity at a similar active-parameter budget. A larger shared expert (wider d_shared) shifts work into dense, communication-free GEMMs that overlap EP dispatch/combine in training and amortize weight reads across the decode batch. Fewer, wider routed experts (halving N and K while doubling d_ff at fixed sparsity K/N) cut dispatch traffic in half and double per-expert GEMM fill. Optimal (N, K) grows with model size, and over-coarsening hurts routing diversity. Ablate expert granularity and the shared-to-routed split at your target scale rather than copying GPU co-designed models, since the TPU optimum is more shared-heavy.

Low-concurrency decode streams model weights every token (~300 concurrent tokens per weight shard are needed in dense FP8 to reach compute roofline). Combine two architectural levers with explicit concurrency sizing:

Norms, activations, masking, routing math and quantization scale applies run on the Vector Processing Unit (VPU), which is roughly two orders of magnitude below the MXU in throughput and executes in sequence with the matmuls unless XLA fuses it into an adjacent GEMM or we move it off the TensorCore. Therefore we drop reshapes and transposes that carry no quality, standardize on fusable RMSNorm and SwiGLU so the elementwise work folds into the neighbouring GEMM, and offload the MoE sort, gather and scatter to the SparseCore.

Note that fused VPU work is free only while its cycle count fits inside the MXU window it is fused into. A quick illustration of this concept is block-scaled fp8 GEMM.

With a scale per block of B elements along the contraction axis, the MXU emits one partial sum per output element per block and the VPU rescales each one (about three vector ops), so per output element the MXU does 2K flops and the VPU does 3·ceil(K/B) ops. The rescale hides when B ≥ 1.5 × r_mxu / r_vpu, which on v7x (fp8 peak 4,614 TFLOPS against a 19.2 TFLOPS vector peak, a ratio near 240) puts the threshold near 340. In simulation on a DeepSeek-V3 expert shape, block 512 runs at the per-tensor time, block 256 is VPU-bound at 1.2x and block 128 at 2.4x. Achieved MXU efficiency is lower than peak, which lowers the effective ratio and can make the smaller blocks workable; the way to decide is to measure the GEMM at each block instead of using peak ratio. This threshold is specific to Ironwood. TPU 8t is designed with specialized support for scaling so quantization for MXFP4 and MXFP8 formats has no overhead. Re-measure the block-size threshold there instead of reusing 340. The rule itself carries forward: keep vector work hidden inside a matmul window.

For workloads dominated by BF16-sensitive matmuls (most notably long-context quadratic attention where direct FP8 degrades accuracy), native single-pass BF16 leaves MXU capacity unused whenever hardware low-precision MXU throughput exceeds native BF16 throughput by more than the pass count. Use the multi-pass BF16 algorithm to execute BF16-accurate matmuls via multiple passes over the higher-throughput lower-precision MXU pipeline without losing numerical fidelity. This matters more as low-precision throughput pulls ahead of BF16. TPU 8t adds native FP4, which doubles MXU throughput. On Ironwood the FP8 to BF16 ratio is exactly 2:1, so two or more passes cannot beat native BF16.

#10228. Agents are sessions, not requests: size GKE node pools for AI agent sessions

Sorry, your browser doesn't support playback for this video

Your company wants to move its coding agents from developer laptops to a shared GKE cluster, for persistence, scale, and governance. Each of those agent sessions stays open all day but works only part of it, and how you run them decides how much of your node bill pays for waiting.

By the end of this article, you will know why an agent session needs a different runtime than a web request, how Agent Substrate and AX run sessions on GKE, and how to identify cost optimization opportunities in your GKE node pools. This article assumes you know Kubernetes Pods and node pools. If not, check out the GKE overview.

A web request lasts milliseconds. The server answers it, keeps nothing, and serves the next one, so Kubernetes scales a web app by its traffic: more requests, more Pods.

An agent session works another way. A coding agent's workspace can stay open for the developer's whole workday: it keeps its memory and its files between prompts, and it works only after a prompt arrives.

How much of that day does the agent really work? Much less than it waits. In a July 2026 study of agent traces, Banruo Liu and colleagues found that in a typical session with several prompts, the agent waits for the user about 80% of the time. A turn, one prompt and the agent's whole response to it, takes about 6.5 minutes of agent work on average

Put those numbers on one developer's day. With 2 prompts an hour over an 8 hour workday, which is our assumption, the agent works 22% of the day and waits for nearly 78% of it. If each session holds a machine, you pay for that waiting time

Teams usually pick one of two answers, and both work well for web apps.

The first is to give each session its own Pod. The session keeps its state, but its Pod holds 1 vCPU and 1 GiB all day. For example, a c3-standard-4 node fits 3 such Pods after GKE's reservations, so in our cost model 100 coding agents need 34 nodes and about USD 1,288 a month, for agents that work 22% of the time.

The second is to scale the Pod to zero between prompts. The node's capacity is released, and files on a persistent volume survive, but the process and its memory do not, so the next prompt starts a new agent process.

Based on that, you want a third option: keep the session, and free the machine while the session waits.

Agent Substrate and AX are built for that: the developer's session stays open, and the sandbox that serves it gives its machine back while the session waits. Together they cover the parts of an agentic platform that are the same for most teams: the control plane, the runtime, and the sandbox. Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. AX answers what the agent is.

Following the CNCF donation of gVisor, Agent Substrate, and OpenRL in November 2026, you can run Agent Substrate on GKE or on other Kubernetes clusters. Agent Substrate 1.0 is generally available on GKE.

One distinction helps from here on. The session is the multi turn interaction between the developer and the agent, with its conversation history. The compute that serves it is an isolated sandbox: an actor in Agent Substrate, or a Task in AX. Parking acts on the sandbox, and the session stays open.

Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. It separates the stateful agent from the compute it runs on. An actor is the sandbox for one agent, and it holds the agent's running processes, files, and identity. A worker is a reusable sandbox drawn from a warm pool of Kubernetes Pods, and one worker hosts several actors.

An agent runs code that a model wrote, so anything in its environment can leak through a prompt injection or a bad command, API keys included. Gateways control the actor's inbound and outbound traffic and add credentials on the way out, based on the actor's identity, so credentials stay out of the agent's environment.

SuspendActor snapshots an idle actor to object storage and frees its worker, and the next request restores the actor on a warm worker with room for it. Agent Substrate snapshots memory by default and falls back to files when it cannot capture memory. Many parked sessions share a small warm pool, so in our cost model the same 100 agents need 16 nodes instead of 34.

AX answers what the agent is and what it knows. You declare a Task, which says what runs (image, command, and limits), and a Workspace, which says what the agent knows (repositories and files). AX runs each Task as an actor. ax suspend parks the task and keeps its /workspace. Today AX asks for a volume snapshot, so the files come back on resume and the agent's processes start fresh (runner guide).

AX passes suspend, resume, and delete to Agent Substrate. The AX roadmap plans to add budgets, policies, and gateways, which limit what an agent may do, and a record of what each task did.

Two details decide the bill before you size anything.

First, you decide when a sandbox parks. Agent Substrate does not suspend an idle actor by itself (#424), so your agent harness, or the service in front of it, calls the suspend API after an idle timeout. Until then, an idle actor keeps its worker. When idle actors hold every worker, a request for another actor waits up to 5 seconds by default, then fails with HTTP 503 (Agent Substrate docs).

Second, you decide how much CPU each sandbox is promised. An actor is sized by its CPU and memory limits, and each worker books those limits against the capacity you declare for its pool (API guide). Our cost model sets that capacity to what the node really has, so every actor keeps its full limit. You can declare more capacity than the node has, and then you overcommit CPU at the pool instead of in each Pod.

Our cost model prices parking for the same 100 coding agents, at Google Cloud on-demand list prices, as of November 2026, in us-central1.

With a 2 minute idle timeout, each session holds a worker nearly 29% of its open time. A warm pool sized for bursts (the 99th percentile of sessions holding a worker at once, if sessions act independently, plus 15% headroom) needs 15 nodes. Add one node that runs around the clock for the Agent Substrate control plane and its PostgreSQL database, and a job that scales the pool to zero after hours:

Parking saves nearly 40% of the node bill. The pool is full about once in 3,000 turns, so a prompt rarely meets that 503. In an hour at 2.5 prompts instead of 2, the same 15 nodes are full for 3% of turns, so size the pool for your busiest hour.

Keep in mind that parking pays most when your agents wait the most. At 2 prompts an hour, the saving is nearly 40%. At 4 prompts an hour, the agent works 44% of the hour, the pool grows to 27 nodes, and the saving is 4%. Above about 4 prompts an hour, one Pod per session becomes the cheaper option, so measure your prompt rate first.

Also, the pool size is yours to set. The autoscaling option in the GKE install guide follows worker Pods, not suspended actors, so scale the pool to zero after hours with a scheduled job: a pool that runs all 730 hours of the month costs nearly twice the one Pod per session bill.

Finally, the fleet size matters. The Agent Substrate control plane costs the same for 10 sessions or 1,000, so parking pays from about 37 coding sessions, or 13 for an assumed chat assistant that works about a minute an hour.

Overcommit changes the comparison. If your Pods request less CPU than their limit, Kubernetes places them by their requests. A Pod that requests 0.25 vCPU with a 1 vCPU limit packs 12 to a node (its 1 GiB of memory caps it there), so the same 100 coding agents fit on 9 nodes, fewer than our parked pool. Agent Substrate can overcommit too, through the capacity you declare on the worker pool, and our cost model does not price that case.

Overcommit has the same cost on both sides: agents share CPU and slow down when their neighbors are busy, during builds and tests. Compare like with like before you decide to park: Pods and actors with the same CPU guarantee.

Back to the shared cluster. Agents are sessions, not requests: size the warm pool for the sessions that hold a worker, not the sessions that are open.

We would park long lived sessions that wait most of the hour, and keep one Pod per session when developers prompt more than about 4 times an hour. Size the warm pool for working time plus a short idle timeout at your busiest hour, and compare with your current Pods at the same CPU guarantee before you park.

#10224. Sizing GPU Memory for a RAG Service: KV Cache, Context and Concurrency

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) app is usually drawn as a language model plus a vector database. When it goes live, the part that fails first is rarely the retrieval. It is GPU memory on the generation model, because a model that loads fine for one request can run out of memory when several users send long questions at once.

This article shows how to estimate that limit before you deploy, with a short script you can run, and then covers what else in the pipeline can slow responses down.

Serving a model takes more than its weights. The GPU also holds:

the key-value (KV) cache, which grows with the length of every active request

memory used by the inference runtime itself

SitePoint's guide to running a local LLM on 10GB of VRAM makes the same point for a single desktop GPU: the usable budget is the total VRAM minus overhead, and the KV cache grows with the context window, so the real model budget is smaller than the number on the box.

For a RAG service, the KV cache matters even more. Retrieved passages are pasted into the prompt, so every request carries a long context, and every concurrent request holds its own cache.

The cache size per token depends on the model's architecture: two tensors (keys and values) for every layer, for every KV head. This script turns those numbers into a rough limit on concurrent requests:

def kv_bytes_per_token(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, dtype_bytes=2):

# Keys and values, for every layer, for one token.

return 2 *layers *kv_heads *head_dim *dtype_bytes

def max_concurrent(vram_gb, weights_gb, ctx_tokens, per_token, reserve_gb=2.0):

budget = (vram_gb - weights_gb - reserve_gb) * GIB

return int(budget // (ctx_tokens * per_token))

per_token = kv_bytes_per_token(32, 8, 128)

print(per_token // 1024, "KiB per token")

n = max_concurrent(24, 16, ctx, per_token)

print(ctx, "tokens ->", n, "concurrent requests")

The numbers are estimates. Real runtimes add their own overhead, and the architecture values come from the model's configuration file, so read them from the model you actually use. The pattern is what matters: doubling the context length halves the number of requests that fit. A RAG prompt that stuffs in ten retrieved chunks costs real capacity.

Serving runtimes do not simply crash when the cache is full. The vLLM documentation on optimization and tuning explains that when KV cache space is insufficient for all batched requests, the engine preempts some of them and recomputes them later, which can hurt end-to-end latency. Its suggested remedies include giving the cache more memory, reducing the number of concurrent sequences or batched tokens, or sharding the model across GPUs.

So the symptom of undersizing is often not an error. It is slow, uneven responses under load. Test with realistic prompt lengths and several simultaneous users, not a single request.

Embedding and ingestion have a different profile from generation. Embedding is often lighter than serving a large language model, and some teams run it on CPU. Document parsing, chunking and tokenization also run on CPU, so a fast GPU cannot make up for a slow CPU upstream. SitePoint's tutorial on a local RAG system for private documents walks through chunking, embeddings, vector storage and reranking, and is a good map of which stages run where.

RAM: vector indexes, document loaders and caches all live in system memory, so large collections need more of it.

Storage: model files, indexes, logs and backups add up. Fast storage shortens model loading and re-indexing.

Network: if the vector database, application server and model server sit in different places, latency adds to every request.

Two layouts are common. A single instance runs everything and is simplest for prototypes and internal tools. Separated services put ingestion and retrieval on smaller machines and keep the GPU for generation, which scales better as traffic grows.

Memory estimates only hold if the GPU memory is really yours. On a shared or time-sliced GPU, other workloads can take memory and compute away from your service, which makes capacity hard to plan. Hosting providers describe this in different terms, such as passthrough, vGPU, MIG and time-slicing. For a view of how one GPU hosting provider explains those allocation methods, GPU Mart's GPU virtualization guide compares them. It is a hosting company's page, so confirm any provider's claims about isolation and performance with your own load test.

Whatever provider you choose, ask these questions:

Is the physical GPU assigned to you exclusively, or partitioned or time-sliced?

Do you have root access to install your own drivers, CUDA libraries and containers?

Which minimum commitments, regions and support terms apply?

A small stack can run as a handful of containers: an API service for request handling and prompt assembly, an ingestion worker, a vector database and a model server. Keeping them separate lets you monitor each one's memory and CPU use and move the heavy one to bigger hardware without rewriting the rest.

For a service that stays up all month, a fixed monthly term is easier to forecast than hourly capacity. Hourly capacity suits testing quantization settings or processing a one-off document batch.

Is GPU memory always the bottleneck for RAG?

Not always. Generation usually hits memory first, but slow parsing, a small CPU or a distant vector database can dominate response time.

Does quantization solve the memory problem?

It shrinks the weights, but the KV cache still grows with context length and concurrency. Test with your real prompt sizes.

It depends on volume. Small workloads can run on CPU, while a large ingestion job may benefit from a GPU for a limited time.

Size a RAG service for the worst realistic moment, not the single-request demo. Estimate the KV cache from the model's architecture, remember that long retrieved contexts reduce how many requests fit, watch for preemption under load, keep CPU, RAM, storage and network in the plan, and find out whether the GPU is exclusive or shared. The script above is small, but it turns a vague worry about memory into numbers you can check.

Penguin Solutions showed off its MemoryAI Photonic Appliance remote memory system at Supercomputer 2026 (SC26), with customer samples scheduled for the second half of 2027. The device supports up to 576 GB of HBM3E with an aggregate bandwidth of up to 7.2 TB/s, and connects to systems using optical interconnects via the CXL.mem 3.1 protocol. HBM memory capacity is usually one of the major bottlenecks of contemporary AI accelerators, which is why, when developers face it, they tend to add or upgrade their accelerators. Penguin Solutions' new device aims to solve that, but there are drawbacks.

The MemoryAI Photonic Appliance is designed to accommodate up to 576 GB of HBM3E and 16 TB of DDR5 memory, with overall bandwidth targeting 7.2 TB/s. The external memory box supports three memory access configurations: HBM Direct Mode, HBM-DDR5 Cached Mode, and DDR5 Direct Mode. The first provides access to external HBM3E, the second combines HBM3E and DDR5 in a tiered memory sub-system with HBM3E acting like a write-through cache for DDR5, while the third provides access to DDR5 without an intermediate HBM cache. Meanwhile, hardware semaphores (HSEMs) enable software-managed memory consistency across multiple host processors (i.e., if more than one host processor accesses the same memory location, software can use semaphores to ensure that only one host processor can modify data).

The Penguin appliance relies on Marvell's architecture that consists of Photonic Fabric Memory Modules (PFMMs) and Photonic Fabric network interface cards (PF-NICs). The device uses eight PFMM modules; each integrates a memory controller, eight DDR5 DIMM interfaces, 72 GB of HBM3E cache, and a photonic interposer with 16 transmit and 16 receive channels operating at 224 Gbps. This provides approximately 7.2 Tbps of aggregate bidirectional optical bandwidth per module, or 57.6 Tbps (7.2 TB/s) for the entire appliance, equivalent to approximately 3.6 TB/s in each direction.

Marvell offers two ways to connect processors to the external memory pool. The first uses a Photonic Fabric network interface card (PF-NIC) with PCIe 6.0 and CXL 3.1 connectivity, which provides up to 2 Tbps of bandwidth and memory access latency below 350ns, excluding PCIe/CXL overhead. The second relies on a Photonic Fabric chiplet integrated into an XPU, which offers up to 14.4 Tbps of bandwidth and latency below 200ns. Both approaches enable processors to access shared memory over optical links across distances of up to 30 meters. Meanwhile, the chiplet approach requires accelerator designers to install the chiplet, while transparent memory expansion using the PF-NIC requires the host to support its memory mapping at both the hardware and software levels.

Marvell's architecture is designed to enable compatible host systems to access external memory through ordinary load/store memory operations rather than RDMA data transfers, but this requires specific hardware capabilities on the host side. Most modern CPUs support PCIe as well as the CXL.mem protocol for memory expansion. However, the situation is different for AI GPUs.

Nvidia's data center GPUs support PCIe, NVLink, and various memory management mechanisms, but they do not support a general-purpose CXL.mem host interface for memory expansion. Similarly, AMD's Instinct accelerators use PCIe and Infinity Fabric for connectivity, but they do not generally support CXL.mem. In theory, accelerators without native CXL.mem support could theoretically access the appliance through a CXL-capable CPU, provided their coherent interconnect supports access to CXL-attached memory. However, this would introduce additional latency, which will extend well beyond the advertised sub-350ns figure (which already excludes PCIe/CXL overhead), which could greatly reduce the actual performance benefits provided by extra capacity.

As a result, PFMMs and Penguin's MemoryAI Photonic Appliance can extend usable memory capacity only for select host systems without major performance penalties. These could include custom XPUs equipped with Marvell's Photonic Fabric chiplets, CPUs or other PUs that support CXL.mem and PF-NIC with memory mapping mechanism, and potentially future AI accelerators that support CXL.mem or integrate Marvell's Photonic Fabric. Obviously, this means that such systems will get an indisputable advantage over machines based on conventional accelerators from AMD and Nvidia in the form of memory upgrades.

Speaking of advantages, we must note that while an aggregate bi-directional bandwidth target of 7.2 TB/s (3.6 TB/s in both directions) seems formidable (it is only a bit lower than memory bandwidth of B300 SXM that comes at 7.7 TB/s), it is set to be shared between multiple accelerators (eight accelerators in a hypothetical scenario), which will provide limited bandwidth to each one. Although Penguin promises transparent access to external memory without modifications to applications, the appliance will require compatible memory management software (in addition to host hardware), which will treat external memory as another memory tier. While extra memory is always welcome, performance benefits of complex multi-tier memory subsystems depend heavily on workloads and efficient data placement. Consequently, the appliance's practical advantages remain to be demonstrated.

Penguin showcased the appliance this week; however, customer samples are scheduled for the second half of 2027, which likely means that the device and/or underlying hardware, firmware, and software need polishing. That said, practical performance and economic advantages of Penguin's MemoryAI Photonic Appliance remain to be seen when everything works as intended. In any case, do not expect deployments of such systems before 2028. This begs the question of where Penguin Solutions plans to source HBM3E in volume in 2028, when the industry will have transitioned to HBM4 and will be in the midst of transition to HBM4E.

#10204. From Script to Screen: A Developer's Workflow for Long-Form AI Video

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Generating a single AI video clip is easy now. You send a prompt and get a few seconds of footage back. The trouble starts when the goal is three, five, or ten minutes of video where the same character looks the same in every scene, the style does not drift, and the captions line up with the picture.

Most guides stop at the clip. This one covers the part after it, treating a video as a small build pipeline: a storyboard that is data, prompts generated from that data, clips normalised and stitched with ffmpeg, and captions derived from the same source. If you want to wire a generation model into your own application first, SitePoint's guide to integrating generative video APIs into web applications covers the request and webhook side. If the finished video is headed to a YouTube channel, how to get a YouTube API key and use the Data API is a good starting point for the publishing side. Everything between those two is below.

Long-form AI video fails at the seams between clips, not inside them. Plan for continuity before you generate anything.

A storyboard stored as JSON can be validated, versioned, diffed, and turned into prompts by code, which a pile of loose prompts cannot.

Repeat the full character description in every prompt. Models do not remember the previous clip.

Clips from different generations rarely share resolution, frame rate, or codec settings. Normalise them before joining.

Captions are cheapest when generated from the script and measured clip durations, not transcribed afterwards.

Current video models generate short clips, commonly a handful of seconds each. A longer video is therefore many clips, and every clip is an independent generation. The model has no memory of what the character wore in the previous one.

That produces three kinds of failure. The character drifts, so hair, clothing, or proportions change between scenes. The style drifts, so lighting and colour grade shift from cut to cut. And the technical specs drift, so one clip is 24 frames per second at one resolution and the next is something else. Each of these is fixable, but only if the project is organised so the fixes can be applied consistently.

Start with a structured storyboard instead of a document full of prompts. One file holds the style, a "character bible", and an ordered list of scenes.

"style": "soft watercolor picture book illustration, warm evening light, 16:9",

"mira": "a nine year old girl with short curly black hair, a yellow raincoat and red rubber boots",

"lantern": "a small round paper lantern with a gentle orange glow and a tiny smiling face"

"setting": "a narrow cobblestone street in light rain",

"action": "walks slowly past closed shops, looking up at the sky",

"camera": "slow push in from a medium shot",

"narration": "It had rained all afternoon, and the street was quiet."

"action": "finds the lantern glowing on a wooden crate and kneels beside it",

"narration": "Then she noticed something glowing under the awning."

Because it is data, a script can check it before you spend any generation credits. Two checks pay for themselves quickly: every character named in a scene must exist in the bible, and no scene may be longer than the clip limit of the model you use.

const known = new Set(Object.keys(board.characters));

errors.push${scene.id}: unknown character "${name}");

${scene.id}: ${scene.duration}s is over the ${MAX_CLIP_SECONDS}s clip limit, split it into shots

Treat MAX_CLIP_SECONDS as a setting. Limits differ between models and change over time, so check the one you are using rather than trusting the number in this example.

Never write the per-scene prompt by hand. Generate it, so the style line and the character descriptions are identical every time.

The character description appears word for word in every scene that includes that character. This is the single most effective consistency tactic that needs no special tooling. Changing a detail, say the colour of the boots, becomes one edit in the bible instead of a search through twenty prompts, and the diff shows exactly what changed between versions.

Put the style line first and keep it identical across all scenes.

Describe one action per clip. Two sequential actions in one prompt often produce a blend of both.

Keep camera directions simple. A single move per clip is more reliable than a combination.

Avoid naming real people, brands, or existing characters in prompts. Beyond the rights issue, it makes results harder to reproduce.

Clips from separate generations often disagree on resolution, frame rate, pixel format, or codec parameters. Joining them directly can produce stutters, green frames, or an outright error. Convert every clip to one agreed format first.

-vf "scale=1280:720:force_original_aspect_ratio=decrease,pad=1280:720:(ow-iw)/2:(oh-ih)/2,fps=24,format=yuv420p" \

-an -c:v libx264 -crf 18 "norm/$(basename "$f")"

The filter scales each clip to fit a 1280 by 720 frame, adds padding if the aspect ratio differs, sets 24 frames per second, and forces a widely compatible pixel format. The -an flag drops any audio the model generated, since narration will be added as one track later. Name clips with zero padded numbers such as s01.mp4 and s02.mp4, so a shell glob sorts them in story order.

Once every file shares the same parameters, the concat demuxer can join them without re-encoding:

for f in norm/*.mp4; do echo "file '$PWD/$f'"; done > list.txt

ffmpeg -y -f concat -safe 0 -i list.txt -c copy stitched.mp4

Hard cuts are the safe default. Crossfades are possible with ffmpeg's xfade filter, but they require careful offset arithmetic and a re-encode, so add them only when a cut genuinely looks wrong.

Captions are easiest when they come from the script, not from a speech recognition pass over the finished video. The storyboard already holds the narration text and each scene's length, so an SRT file is a few lines of code. Use measured durations rather than planned ones, because a generated clip is often a little shorter or longer than requested.

const { execFileSync } = require('node:child_process');

const ms = Math.round(totalSeconds * 1000);

const h = String(Math.floor(ms / 3600000)).padStart(2, '0');

const m = String(Math.floor((ms % 3600000) / 60000)).padStart(2, '0');

const s = String(Math.floor((ms % 60000) / 1000)).padStart(2, '0');

const rest = String(ms % 1000).padStart(3, '0');

const end = cursor + scene.duration; // set from measureSeconds() after generation

${blocks.length + 1}\n${toTimestamp(start)} --> ${toTimestamp(end)}\n${scene.narration}\n

Update each scene's duration from measureSeconds on the normalised clip before calling buildSrt. If a narration line is long, split it across two caption blocks so it stays readable. Then combine picture, voiceover, and captions in one final step:

ffmpeg -y -i stitched.mp4 -i narration.mp3 -i captions.srt \

-c:v copy -c:a aac -c:s mov_text -shortest final.mp4

Soft captions like these can be switched off by the viewer and read by assistive technology, which is better than burning text into the picture. Keep the .srt file as well, since most platforms accept it as a separate upload.

Everything above is a good fit when you need repeatability: many videos from templates, versioned storyboards, automated publishing, or tight control over the model. The price is that you own the glue code, the ffmpeg settings, and the failures.

For one off projects, or when the people making the video are not developers, a managed platform is often the more sensible choice. Tools such as magiclight.ai bundle story to video generation, character handling, voice, and an editor in one interface, and advertise videos of up to 50 minutes. Check the specifics yourself, because claims like that are marketing, and results depend on your content. One point matters for developers in particular: if you need to automate generation from your own code, confirm that the platform you pick offers an API or export route before you build a workflow around it. A tool with a good editor and no programmatic access suits a different job than the pipeline in this article.

A short manual pass catches what automation does not. Watch the whole video once at normal speed, looking only at continuity. Read the captions against the audio. Check that no real person's likeness, brand mark, or protected character slipped into the footage. Finally, check the disclosure rules of the platform you publish on, since many now ask creators to label realistic synthetic media, and the rules change.

The model is only one component of a long-form AI video. The reliability comes from the structure around it: a validated storyboard, prompts generated from it, clips forced into one format, and captions derived from measured data. Each of those is ordinary software engineering, and none of it depends on which generation model you choose, so the pipeline survives when the model changes.

Start with a two minute video and four or five scenes. Fix the seams there before scaling up, because every continuity problem you find at that size is one you will not have to hunt for in a ten minute project.

#10185. Your AI Pipeline Works. But Can You Trust It?

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

AI is becoming a part of almost everything we do in technology today. Whether it's detecting fraudulent transactions, assessing credit risk, forecasting demand, or personalizing customer experiences, organizations are increasingly relying on AI to make faster and smarter decisions.

And naturally, when we talk about building AI systems, most of the conversation revolves around model accuracy, performance, and deployment. We spend considerable time making sure our models are trained well, our pipelines run efficiently, and our systems can handle production workloads.

But here's something we don't always pay enough attention to: Can we actually trust the data feeding these models?

After all, even the most sophisticated AI model can produce misleading results if the underlying data is incomplete, inconsistent, or incorrectly interpreted. What's interesting is that these problems don't always show up as technical failures. Sometimes, everything appears to be working perfectly fine.

Imagine a financial institution that has recently deployed an AI-powered loan risk assessment system. The team has spent months preparing the data, training the model, testing its performance, and finally moving it into production.

The deployment goes smoothly. Data pipelines are running on schedule, predictions are being generated, and all the monitoring dashboards are green.

A few weeks later, however, the business team notices something unusual. The model has started classifying more customers as high risk than expected.

The data science team checks the model configuration. Nothing has changed. The engineering team reviews the pipelines. All jobs have completed successfully. Even the infrastructure looks healthy.

It turns out that an upstream source system changed the definition of a customer income field from annual gross income to annual net income. The field name remained the same, the data type was still numeric, and none of the existing technical validations detected the change.

The pipeline continued running as expected, but the model was now receiving information with a different business meaning.

This brings us to an important point: Just because an AI pipeline runs successfully doesn't necessarily mean we can trust its output.

Building reliable AI systems requires us to look beyond the model itself. We need to understand where the data originates, how it changes as it moves through different systems, whether it meets quality expectations, and who is responsible when something goes wrong.

In this article, we'll explore three important elements of trustworthy AI pipelines (data quality, data lineage, and accountability) and look at how engineering teams can incorporate them into their existing workflows without making the process unnecessarily complicated.

Most modern data pipelines come with some form of operational monitoring. Engineering teams track job completion, execution time, system availability, and processing failures.

These metrics are important because they help us understand whether our systems are functioning as expected.

Operational monitoring primarily tells us whether a pipeline executed successfully. It doesn't necessarily tell us whether the data being processed is accurate, consistent, or suitable for the model consuming it.

Suppose we're building a loan risk model that uses a customer's debt-to-income ratio as one of its input features.

Debt-to-income ratio = Monthly debt payments / Monthly income

Now imagine that an upstream system starts supplying annual income instead of monthly income.

The calculation still executes successfully. The values are numeric, the transformation doesn't throw an error, and the model continues generating predictions.

However, the resulting debt-to-income ratio is incorrect because the numerator and denominator represent different time periods.

This is where traditional monitoring falls short.

To build trustworthy AI pipelines, we need to look at different dimensions of system health.

A pipeline can be operationally healthy while processing unreliable data. Similarly, a model can continue producing predictions even when the meaning of an important input field has changed.

For engineering teams, the objective should be to identify these problems as early as possible, ideally before they reach downstream models.

We've all heard the phrase "garbage in, garbage out."

And despite all the progress we've made in artificial intelligence, this principle hasn't changed.

AI models may be capable of processing enormous volumes of data and identifying complex patterns, but they cannot automatically guarantee that the information they're receiving is correct.

This is where data quality becomes important.

Data quality refers to how well information meets the requirements of its intended use. In AI pipelines, it helps us determine whether incoming data is sufficiently reliable for model training, evaluation, or inference.

Let's look at four dimensions that are particularly relevant.

Imagine we're developing a loan risk model that relies on customer income, outstanding debt, repayment history, and credit utilization.

What happens if 20% of the incoming records are missing income information?

Should we reject those records? Should we replace missing values using an approved statistical method? Or should we flag them for additional review?

There isn't one answer that works for every system.

The right approach depends on the model, the business requirements, and the potential consequences of the decision.

What matters is that missing information is handled deliberately.

For example, replacing missing income with zero may allow the pipeline to continue running, but it could significantly distort the model's predictions.

Validity checks whether values meet expected formats, ranges, and business rules.

Although credit utilization can exceed 100% in certain circumstances, a value of 450% may warrant investigation.

This highlights an important distinction: Not every unusual value is incorrect, and not every technically valid value is meaningful.

A database may accept any numeric value, but the business may need additional rules to determine whether that value is appropriate for the model.

Engineering teams should distinguish between invalid records, unexpected observations, and legitimate exceptions.

This is one of the most common challenges in enterprise data environments.

Imagine two systems providing customer income information.

Both datasets contain a column called income.

If we combine these datasets without understanding their definitions, we risk introducing inconsistencies into downstream calculations.

The data may look perfectly fine from a technical perspective. The problem is that the same field represents different business concepts.

This is why data definitions, units, and transformation rules are just as important as schema validation.

Suppose a model predicts a customer's current financial risk using income information that was last updated 18 months ago.

The data might be accurate, complete, and consistent.

But is it current enough to support the decision?

Different AI applications have different freshness requirements.

A real-time fraud detection model may require near-real-time transaction information, while a long-term forecasting model may work with less frequently updated datasets.

The important thing is to define these expectations based on how the data will be used.

However, technical validation alone isn't enough.

Remember our original example where the income field changed from gross to net income?

Both values could easily pass a rule requiring income to be greater than zero.

Detecting that kind of issue requires clear business definitions, communication with upstream systems, and appropriate change management.

In other words, data quality isn't just about checking values. It's also about understanding what those values represent.

Now let's assume our loan risk model generates an unexpectedly high risk score for a customer.

The model output is available, but we want to understand what contributed to the prediction.

The income information came from a customer application system. Outstanding debt came from another database. Both datasets went through several transformations before reaching the feature engineering pipeline.

The final features were then passed to the model.

At this point, a seemingly simple question becomes surprisingly difficult:

Where exactly did the information used for this prediction come from?

This is where data lineage becomes valuable.

Data lineage describes how data originates, moves, and changes across different systems.

For AI pipelines, it helps us understand the journey from raw source data to the features used by a model.

Source Systems → Data Transformation → Feature Engineering → Model Inference → Predictions

Each stage introduces dependencies that can affect downstream results.

Data lineage can be captured at different levels of detail.

Dataset-level lineage shows relationships between datasets.

Loan Applications → Cleaned Applications → Model Features

This helps us understand how datasets move through the overall architecture.

However, suppose the model's debt-to-income ratio suddenly changes.

Dataset-level lineage might tell us which datasets were involved, but it may not provide enough detail to identify the affected field.

That's where column-level lineage becomes useful.

debt_to_income_ratio = monthly_debt_payment / monthly_income

Column-level lineage can show that the resulting feature depends on two upstream attributes.

If one of those attributes changes, engineers can trace the dependency and investigate the impact.

This is especially valuable in environments where multiple reports, applications, and AI models depend on shared data sources.

Large organizations may use dedicated metadata management and lineage platforms.

However, smaller teams can start by capturing essential metadata during pipeline execution.

OpenLineage provides an open standard for collecting and exchanging lineage metadata across supported systems.

It's important to understand that lineage doesn't automatically guarantee data accuracy.

A pipeline can be perfectly documented and still contain incorrect transformation logic.

But when something goes wrong, lineage gives engineering teams a clearer starting point for investigation.

Instead of searching across multiple systems and trying to reconstruct dependencies manually, they can identify where data originated and how it changed along the way.

An AI model starts producing unexpected results.

The data science team checks the model and finds no issues.

The data engineering team confirms that the pipelines are running successfully.

The source-system team verifies that data was delivered according to its interface.

Everyone has completed their individual checks, but nobody has identified who should coordinate the investigation.

This is where accountability becomes important.

AI pipelines often involve multiple teams responsible for different stages of the data lifecycle. Without clearly defined ownership, even relatively simple issues can take longer to resolve.

A practical accountability framework should distinguish between three responsibilities.

Data owners are responsible for defining the business meaning and expected quality of critical information.

For example, someone needs to determine whether an income field represents gross income, net income, or another financial measure.

These definitions should be documented and communicated to downstream consumers.

Data engineering and platform teams typically manage data ingestion, transformations, validation mechanisms, and pipeline operations.

Their responsibilities may include implementing quality checks, monitoring failures, and assessing the technical impact of changes.

However, technical teams shouldn't be expected to make business-definition decisions without appropriate input.

Data science and ML engineering teams are responsible for evaluating model behavior, managing model versions, and assessing how changes in input data may affect predictions.

If a critical feature changes, the model team may need to determine whether additional testing, recalibration, or retraining is required.

Defining ownership is useful, but connecting it to an operational process is even more important.

Consider a critical data quality failure during feature generation.

The exact workflow will vary depending on the organization and the risk associated with the application.

The objective is to ensure that technical alerts lead to clear actions.

After all, an alert that nobody owns is just another notification waiting to be ignored.

So far, we've discussed why data quality, lineage, and accountability matter.

But how can we bring these concepts into everyday engineering practices?

Here are five controls that teams can introduce without redesigning their entire architecture.

Data contracts define expectations between data producers and consumers.

They can document schemas, field definitions, units, freshness requirements, and compatibility expectations.

For example, rather than specifying only that annual_income is numeric, a contract should clarify whether it represents gross or net income, the currency, and the applicable time period.

This reduces the risk of downstream systems misinterpreting technically valid information.

Quality gates validate critical data before it progresses through the pipeline.

These checks can be implemented during ingestion, transformation, feature generation, or inference.

For example, a feature engineering process calculating debt-to-income ratios should verify that debt and income values use compatible time periods.

Failure handling should reflect the severity of the issue. Some records may be quarantined, while more serious failures may require stopping the pipeline.

Pipeline execution should produce sufficient metadata to identify source datasets, transformations, and downstream outputs.

Where appropriate, teams should also capture model and feature versions associated with predictions.

This information supports impact analysis, debugging, and investigation when unexpected results occur.

Datasets change, transformation logic is updated, features are modified, and models are retrained.

Versioning helps engineering teams understand which components were active at a particular point in time.

Relevant evidence may include pipeline configurations, dataset snapshots, model versions, validation outcomes, and deployment records.

Every critical data element or pipeline component should have an identified owner or responsible team.

More importantly, ownership should connect directly to incident management and change approval processes.

If a source definition changes, teams should know who evaluates the impact.

If a quality gate fails, the responsible team should receive the alert.

#10166. How Developers Can Detect Fake Payment Gateways and Build Secure Checkout Integrations

Online payments are an essential part of modern web applications. E-commerce platforms, subscription services, and online marketplaces all depend on payment gateways to process transactions and manage customer orders.

However, integrating a payment gateway involves more than adding a payment button or displaying a checkout form. Developers must ensure that payment requests are sent to legitimate providers, transaction information is validated correctly, and payment confirmations cannot be manipulated.

Fake payment gateways introduce another challenge. They can imitate legitimate payment interfaces, use copied checkout templates, and redirect customers to fraudulent websites. Even when a checkout page looks professional, its underlying implementation may not be trustworthy.

For developers, understanding these risks is important when designing payment workflows. This article explores common payment integration vulnerabilities and practical techniques for building secure checkout systems using JavaScript and Node.js.

A payment gateway connects an application with a payment processing service. It handles transaction requests and communicates payment results to the merchant's application.

A typical checkout process involves several components:

A frontend checkout interface where customers review their orders.

A backend application that creates payment requests.

A payment provider that processes transactions.

A webhook endpoint that receives payment notifications.

A database that stores order and transaction information.

Attackers can target different parts of this process.

For example, a fraudulent website may copy the appearance of a legitimate checkout page while sending submitted information to an unauthorized server. Another approach involves manipulating payment requests or creating fake confirmation pages that make an unsuccessful transaction appear complete.

These techniques are also relevant to marketplace fraud. An eBay scam can involve fraudulent payment requests and misleading transaction links, illustrating why developers need to consider payment security beyond the appearance of a checkout page.

The important distinction is that a legitimate-looking interface does not establish that the payment request is reaching an authorized provider.

#10128. What Is BIO Tagging and How to Use It for Document AI Training Data

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

If you've worked with named entity recognition (NER) in text, you've probably seen BIO tagging before. It's the standard way to mark where entities start, continue, and end inside a sequence of tokens. What you might not know is that the same tagging scheme has become the backbone of document AI -- the field where machine learning models extract structured data from invoices, receipts, contracts, and other business documents.

This guide explains how BIO tagging works, why it matters for document understanding, and how to build BIO-tagged training data that actually produces accurate models. We'll walk through the tagging logic, show real examples on financial documents, cover common mistakes that silently hurt model performance, and look at tools that speed up the annotation process.

BIO stands for Beginning, Inside, Outside. It's a labeling scheme that tells a model which tokens belong to an entity, which tokens continue that entity, and which tokens are just background noise.

● B (Beginning) -- the first token of a named entity

● I (Inside) -- any token that continues the same entity

● O (Outside) -- any token that doesn't belong to any entity

Take a simple line from an invoice: "Invoice #INV-2024-0891". If you're labeling invoice numbers, the BIO tags look like this:

Token          Label ─────          ───── Invoice        O #              O INV-2024-0891  B-invoice_number

Now consider a multi-word entity like a vendor name: "Acme Corporation Ltd". All three words are part of the same entity, so the tagging becomes:

Token          Label ─────          ───── Acme           B-vendor_name Corporation    I-vendor_name Ltd            I-vendor_name

The B prefix marks where the entity starts. The I prefix marks every following token that belongs to the same entity. Everything else gets O. This three-label structure is simple, but it gives models enough information to learn entity boundaries precisely -- even when two entities of the same type appear next to each other.

Traditional NER works on plain text -- a flat sequence of words. Document AI is harder because pages have two dimensions. A word's meaning depends not just on what comes before and after it in reading order, but also on where it sits on the page. The number "$4,250.00" could be an invoice total, a line item price, or a tax amount depending on its position relative to headers and columns.

Models like LayoutLMv3, LiLT, and BROS solve this by combining text tokens with their bounding box coordinates. Each token gets three inputs: the word itself, its position on the page (x, y, width, height), and its BIO label during training. The model learns to associate spatial patterns with entity types.

BIO tagging fits this architecture perfectly because it operates at the token level. Every word on the page gets exactly one label, and the model learns from thousands of these labeled tokens which spatial and textual patterns correspond to which fields.

If you're coming from a background in natural language processing in Python, the concept is identical -- BIO tagging works the same way whether your tokens come from a sentence or a document page. The difference is that document AI adds positional data to each token.

BIO isn't the only tagging scheme. You'll encounter several variants:

● BIO (IOB2) -- B marks the first token, I marks continuation tokens, O marks non-entities. The most widely used scheme.

● BIOES -- adds E (End) for the last token of a multi-word entity and S (Single) for single-token entities. More explicit boundary marking.

● BILOU -- adds L (Last) and U (Unit). Functionally identical to BIOES with different letters.

● IOB1 -- the original scheme where B is only used when two entities of the same type are adjacent. Rarely used today.

For document AI, standard BIO (IOB2) is the safest choice. Here's why:

● LayoutLMv3 and most pre-trained document models expect BIO format out of the box

● The FUNSD and CORD benchmark datasets use BIO, so your data format matches the academic baselines

● BIOES adds more label types, which means you need more training data to learn each one reliably

● Converting from BIO to BIOES is trivial if you later need it, but the reverse can lose information

Unless your model architecture specifically requires BIOES or BILOU, stick with BIO.

Before you tag a single token, you need a clear label taxonomy -- the list of entity types your model will learn to recognize. A poorly designed taxonomy creates confusion during annotation and cascades into model errors.

Ask yourself: what fields does the downstream system need? For invoice processing, a typical taxonomy looks like this:

vendor_name vendor_address invoice_number invoice_date due_date line_item_description line_item_quantity line_item_price subtotal tax total payment_method currency

**Rule 1 -- One label per semantic meaning. **Don't reuse amount for both line item prices and the invoice total. A model that learns "amount" means two different things depending on context will confuse them on novel layouts.

**Rule 2 -- Use hierarchical names. **Once your taxonomy exceeds 10 labels, flat names start colliding. line_item_price is better than price because it's self-documenting and prevents collisions with total_price.

**Rule 3 -- Include a catch-all label. **Call it other or O. Text that doesn't belong to any entity still needs a label for token classification. Without negative examples, the model can't learn what isn't a field.

**Rule 4 -- Match your model architecture. **Token classification models like LayoutLMv3 work best with fine-grained, word-level labels. Generative models like Donut output structured text, so they work with coarser labels. Choose your model first, then design labels to match.

Here's the practical workflow for turning raw PDF documents into BIO-tagged training data ready for model training.

Your model needs both the text and its coordinates on the page. For digitally generated PDFs, extract the text layer directly:

import pdfplumber def extract_words(pdf_path):     words = []     with pdfplumber.open(pdf_path) as pdf:         for page in pdf.pages:             for word in page.extract_words():                 words.append({                     'text': word['text'],                     'bbox': [word['x0'], word['top'],                              word['x1'], word['bottom']],                     'page': page.page_number                 })     return words

For scanned documents, you'll need OCR. Tesseract is the standard open-source option:

import pytesseract from pdf2image import convert_from_path def ocr_pdf(pdf_path, dpi=300):     images = convert_from_path(pdf_path, dpi=dpi)     all_words = []     for i, img in enumerate(images):         data = pytesseract.image_to_data(             img, output_type=pytesseract.Output.DICT         )         for j in range(len(data['text'])):             if data['text'][j].strip():                 all_words.append({                     'text': data['text'][j],                     'bbox': [data['left'][j], data['top'][j],                              data['left'][j] + data['width'][j],                              data['top'][j] + data['height'][j]],                     'page': i + 1                 })     return all_words

Models expect coordinates normalized to a 0--1000 range. This makes the model resolution-independent -- a word at the top-left corner is at (0, 0) whether the page is 72 DPI or 300 DPI.

def normalize_bbox(bbox, page_width, page_height):     return [         int(1000 bbox[0] / page_width), *        int(1000 *bbox[1] / page_height),         int(1000 bbox[2] / page_width), *        int(1000 *bbox[3] / page_height)     ]

Manually labeling every word on every page is slow. A single invoice might have 200-400 tokens, and you need hundreds of labeled documents for a usable model. Here are three approaches to speed things up.

Many financial fields follow predictable patterns. Dates, currency amounts, and invoice numbers can be pre-labeled with regex before a human annotator reviews them:

import re PATTERNS = {     'invoice_date': [         r'\b\d{1,2}[/-]\d{1,2}[/-]\d{2,4}\b',         r'\b(?:Jan|Feb|Mar|Apr|May|Jun|Jul|Aug|Sep|Oct|Nov|Dec)\w*\s+\d{1,2},?\s+\d{4}\b'     ],     'total': [         r'$[\d,]+.\d{2}\b'     ],     'invoice_number': [         r'\b(?:INV|inv)[- ]?\d{4,}\b'     ] } def pre_label(words):     for word in words:         word['label'] = 'O'  # default         for label, patterns in PATTERNS.items():             for pattern in patterns:                 if re.match(pattern, word['text']):                     word['label'] = f'B-{label}'                     break     return words

This won't catch everything -- vendor names, addresses, and line item descriptions need human judgment. But it can pre-fill 30-40% of the labels on a typical invoice, turning the annotator's job from labeling to reviewing.

Train a preliminary model on your first 50 labeled documents, then use it to predict labels on new documents. The annotator reviews and corrects the predictions instead of labeling from scratch. As the model improves, corrections become rarer and annotation speed increases.

The key is selecting documents where the model is least confident for the next round of human review. This focuses annotator time where it has the most impact on model accuracy.

These are the errors that show up most often when teams start building document AI training data. Each one silently degrades model performance.

If "Acme Corporation" is labeled as one entity on page 1 but "Acme" and "Corporation" are labeled as separate entities on page 5, the model receives contradictory training signals. Pick a convention and enforce it across your entire dataset. For vendor names, include the full legal name ("Acme Corporation Ltd") every time.

A common beginner mistake is labeling every token of a multi-word entity with B instead of using B for the first token and I for the rest. "March 15, 2024" should be B-date, I-date, I-date -- not B-date, B-date, B-date. The B-B-B pattern tells the model these are three separate entities, which is wrong.

The word "Total:" is a header, not a value. Only the actual value ($4,250.00) should be tagged as B-total. Labeling "Total:" as part of the entity teaches the model to include headers in its extractions, which corrupts downstream processing.

Some OCR engines split punctuation into separate tokens. "$4,250.00" might become ["$", "4,250", ".", "00"]. If you label only "4,250" and skip the dollar sign and cents, the model learns incomplete extraction patterns. Make sure every token in the entity is covered by B or I tags.

If 95% of your tokens are labeled O and only 0.5% are B-invoice_number, the model will learn that predicting O is almost always correct. This is the class imbalance problem. It's normal for O to dominate, but watch for entity types that appear in fewer than 1% of tokens -- those need more training examples or the model won't learn them reliably.

Before you start training, run these checks on your labeled data. Catching errors here is cheaper than debugging a confused model later.

An I-vendor_name tag should never appear without a preceding B-vendor_name. If it does, either the B tag was missed or the entity boundaries are wrong.

def validate_bio_sequences(labels):     errors = []     for i, label in enumerate(labels):         if label.startswith('I-'):             entity_type = label[2:]             if i == 0:                 errors.append(f'I-{entity_type} at position 0 with no preceding B tag')             else:                 prev = labels[i - 1]                 if prev != f'B-{entity_type}' and prev != f'I-{entity_type}':                     errors.append(f'I-{entity_type} at position {i} follows {prev}')     return errors

● BIO tagging assigns B (beginning), I (inside), or O (outside) to every token. It's the standard labeling scheme for token classification in document AI.

● Pair BIO labels with normalized bounding box coordinates so models learn both textual and spatial patterns.

● Design your label taxonomy before you start annotating. One label per semantic meaning, hierarchical names, and a catch-all O label.

● Validate early. Check BIO sequence integrity, label distribution, and bounding box coordinates before training.

● Start with 50-100 labeled documents, train a model, check errors, and scale up. Quality matters more than quantity.

● Use pre-labeling (regex or model-based) to speed up annotation. Manual labeling from scratch doesn't scale.

The annotation step is the foundation of every document AI pipeline. The model you train is only as good as the BIO labels you feed it. Take the time to build a clean taxonomy, label consistently, and validate before training -- it saves debugging time later and produces models that actually work in production.
