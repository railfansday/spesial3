#10422. Attacker Relentlessly Scans for Credentials by Impersonating AI Bots

The threat actor lurked behind bots posing as agents from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and others to find exposed data.

A threat actor lurked behind more than 30 different AI bots to find and steal exposed credentials, continuously scanning for sensitive data without success. The findings highlight a growing problem for organizations: distinguishing between which AI agents belong in their networks and which may be malicious.

A scanner impersonating 32 bots — including crawlers and assistants from OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Meta, and others — hunted for exposed credentials to various organizations, according to a report published today by CQ Prime Research, part of Cequence Security. The scanner requested files from affected organizations — which spanned retail, financial services, telecommunications, energy, and software — that hold passwords, API keys, and cloud credentials.

The scanner has been running since late September and was still active as of late October, according to the researchers. They identified the activity on Oct. 21, discovering that on a single day this month, 48% of Cequence-protected organizations received the malicious AI bot traffic.

Related:AI Gave Your Incident Archive a Second Job

The findings highlight a growing challenge for security teams at a pivotal time, as businesses must figure out which AI agents to trust and permit access to their services.

"Attackers can test every one of those allow and deny decisions, and Cequence is seeing them probe which agents a business trusts, then impersonate the agents they expect to get through," Will Glazier, director of CQ Prime threat research and data science, wrote in the report.

The scanner operates by firing off a burst of 50 to 190 requests in about 8 seconds and then moving on, working through targets one at a time, around the clock. Fortunately for the organizations affected, nearly all of the requests were blocked or failed, according to the report.

"It sends random nonsense paths first to find websites that answer every request, a common scanning technique," Glazier wrote.

CQ Prime Research identified the traffic as malicious through some telling behaviors that undermined the scanner's attempt to blend in with other traffic. The scanner rotates through about 32 bot identities evenly, which real bots do not do, according to the report; one machine claimed to be an agent from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Perplexity, all within seconds.

The attacker also used the contact address of Anthropic's crawler for all of its fake Claude identities, even when posing as different Claude agents, which shows the text was copied and not unique to each agent, according to CQ Prime Research.

Related:What We Missed: Knicks Phishing Test Goes Out of Bounds

The scanner also makes requests for configuration files, cloud credential files, and private SSH keys, from a list of about 90 paths, while never identifying itself or providing a contact address. Legitimate Internet-scanning services would never need these files and always identify themselves.

"The requests gave it away," observes Collin Hogue-Spears, senior director of solutions management at security firm Black Duck, which means "what Cequence documented is the test, not a breach."

However, what happens when AI agents scanning a network get through? This is what organizations are facing, particularly banks and financial institutions, with growing concern among experts that rogue agents are already logging into customer accounts at these organizations without them knowing. The result of such activity could cause global economic fallout.

For now, CQ Prime Research found that at financial services firms, AI agents acting for customers represented fewer than 1 in 4,000 requests. "The agents banks worry about most log in with a customer's own credentials through an ordinary browser and don't announce themselves," Glazier wrote in the report.

At this point, financial institutions should be starting to take action to identify which AI agents are legit and which aren't. But acting now already could mean they're playing catch-up to the activity of threat actors, observes Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

Related:9 Cyber Awareness Tips That Actually Match Today's Maze of Threats

"Banks are currently scrambling to figure out which AI bots belong on their networks, but cybercriminals have already moved on to the next phase," he says. "They are no longer trying to break into these systems. Instead, they are simply logging in disguised as trusted AI agents."

To mitigate the growing threat from AI bots and agents, security leaders must treat AI agents, API keys, and service accounts with the same operational rigor as human administrators, Barney suggests. "That requires eliminating implicit trust across every access path," he says.

Indeed, organizations should not trust a bot's claimed identity on its own, according to CQ Prime Research, and they should verify agents through signed requests, published address ranges, or direct integrations.

They also should monitor for behavior that does not match the claimed identity, "such as an AI assistant requesting configuration or credential files," Glazier wrote.

Organizations also should stop leaving static credentials in vulnerable files where scraping bots can easily find them to eliminate obvious security gaps. Instead, Barney says, "Security teams must move away from long-lived local credentials in favor of centralized, zero-knowledge secrets vaults."

Transactional emails are easy to overlook during application development. A registration form works, the database stores the user's information, and the backend successfully triggers an email. Everything appears to be functioning as expected.

Then a user reports that their password-reset message never arrived.

The problem might not be in the email template or the sending function. It could involve DNS configuration, authentication failures, message formatting, queue processing, recipient-server policies, or an incorrectly configured production environment.

For developers building SaaS applications, ecommerce platforms, and web services, reliable email delivery is an important part of the user experience. An account-verification message that never arrives can prevent someone from accessing an application, while a delayed security notification can create unnecessary confusion.

Understanding how to investigate these problems requires looking beyond the email-sending function. This guide explores a practical debugging workflow, the technical layers involved, and the tools developers can use to identify common email delivery failures.

One of the first mistakes developers make when troubleshooting email is assuming that a successful API response means the message has reached the recipient.

When an application submits an email through an SMTP server or an email service provider's API, the provider may accept the request and return a successful response. However, the receiving mail server still needs to process the message.

Depending on its configuration and policies, the receiving server might:

Accept the message and place it in the inbox.

Accept the message but classify it as spam.

Reject the message because of authentication or policy issues.

Accept the message but place it in another folder.

A successful request therefore confirms only that a particular stage of the process completed.

Developers should distinguish between three important states:

Submission: The application successfully handed the message to its sending provider.

Acceptance: The receiving mail server accepted responsibility for the message.

Inbox placement: The message became visible in the recipient's expected inbox.

These distinctions make troubleshooting considerably easier. Instead of immediately rewriting an email template, developers can identify which stage is actually failing.

Before investigating external email infrastructure, examine the application's own implementation.

A common example is a password-reset workflow. The application receives a request, generates a temporary token, creates a reset URL, and sends an email containing that URL.

Several problems can occur before the message even reaches an email provider.

The application generates an expired token.

The reset URL points to a staging environment.

An asynchronous task fails before processing the message.

The email service credentials are incorrect.

An API request times out without being retried.

The application records success before confirming the provider's response.

Avoid logging passwords, authentication tokens, reset URLs, or other sensitive information.

The objective is to establish a traceable record of what happened without introducing additional security risks.

Email authentication is another important area to investigate when messages are rejected or consistently classified as spam.

Three commonly used mechanisms help receiving servers evaluate whether a message is authorized to represent a particular domain.

Sender Policy Framework (SPF) allows a domain owner to specify which servers are authorized to send messages using that domain.

An email template that looks perfect in a browser can behave differently when rendered by Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail, or another email client.

This happens because email clients do not implement HTML and CSS in exactly the same way.

A layout may break because of unsupported CSS properties, image restrictions, unexpected font rendering, or differences in how tables are handled.

For this reason, email testing should be part of the development process rather than something performed only after users report a problem.

HTML validation: Check the markup for malformed elements, missing attributes, and unnecessary code.

Rendering tests: Preview the email across desktop and mobile email clients.

Link verification: Confirm that buttons, links, and personalized URLs point to the correct destinations.

Image testing: Check whether the message remains understandable when images are blocked.

Content inspection: Review subject lines, sender information, and the plain-text alternative.

Deliverability checks: Examine authentication, content-related warnings, and potential inbox placement problems.

Developers can also incorporate pre-send diagnostics into their release workflow to identify potential deliverability problems before a message is sent to a larger audience.

The important point is to treat these checks as one part of quality assurance, not as a guarantee that every message will reach the primary inbox.

For more information about email rendering differences, SitePoint's article on essential HTML email coding tips provides useful examples of client-specific behavior.

Testing transactional emails with real customer addresses during development can create unnecessary problems.

A better approach is to use a dedicated testing environment that captures outgoing messages without delivering them to actual recipients.

For example, Mailpit provides a local SMTP server and web interface for inspecting messages during development.

The final output generated by the application.

Another option is Mailtrap, which provides email testing capabilities for development workflows.

Consider a registration feature that sends a welcome message whenever a new user creates an account.

Instead of sending hundreds of test messages to real addresses, developers can configure the development environment to capture them in a testing inbox.

This makes it possible to repeatedly test different templates without affecting actual customers or generating unnecessary email traffic.

A separate testing environment also reduces the risk of accidentally sending development notifications to production users.

Applications that send large numbers of emails often use background workers instead of processing every message during the original HTTP request.

This approach helps keep the application responsive, but it introduces another potential source of failures.

Imagine an ecommerce application that sends order confirmations after checkout.

The application creates an order successfully and places an email task in a queue. However, the background worker becomes unavailable before processing the task.

The customer receives an order confirmation much later than expected, or perhaps not at all.

To investigate these problems, developers should monitor:

Retries are particularly important, but they need to be implemented carefully.

A temporary network failure may justify another attempt. A permanent rejection caused by an invalid recipient address usually does not.

Exponential backoff can help prevent repeated requests from overwhelming an unavailable service.

In a production system, add random jitter to avoid having multiple workers retry simultaneously.

Also consider idempotency. If a worker processes the same task twice, the application should avoid sending duplicate transactional messages whenever possible.

A reliable queue architecture should distinguish between temporary failures and permanent failures rather than retrying every error indefinitely.

Testing before deployment is important, but it cannot reveal every issue that may occur in production.

Real email traffic introduces changing conditions, including recipient-server behavior, sender reputation, invalid addresses, and differences between email providers.

Developers should therefore establish ongoing monitoring.

For Gmail-related monitoring, Google Postmaster Tools provides information about domain reputation, spam rates, and authentication.

It is particularly useful for teams sending enough email to generate meaningful reporting data.

Another useful resource is Can I Email, which documents HTML and CSS feature support across email clients.

These tools address different problems. Monitoring sender reputation will not fix a broken password-reset URL, just as validating HTML will not resolve a DNS authentication failure.

The best approach is to monitor each layer independently.

A repeatable checklist helps developers investigate problems without overlooking important details.

Before releasing a new email feature, review the following:

Confirm that the application successfully submits the message.

Verify that provider credentials and environment variables are correct.

Inspect SPF, DKIM, and DMARC configuration.

Validate the HTML and plain-text versions.

Preview the message on desktop and mobile email clients.

Verify that development and production configurations are separated.

Check delivery events and provider error reports.

Monitor bounce and complaint rates after deployment.

Not every application needs an expensive testing platform or a complicated monitoring stack.

A small project might only require a local email testing environment, basic DNS checks, and structured application logs.

A larger SaaS platform may benefit from automated integration tests, provider webhooks, queue monitoring, authentication reports, and dedicated deliverability testing.

The right setup depends on the application's sending volume, complexity, and the importance of its email workflows.

Reliable email delivery requires more than calling an SMTP function or integrating an email API.

Developers need to understand the complete process, from application logic and message construction to authentication, queue processing, recipient-server acceptance, and inbox placement.

The most effective troubleshooting strategy is to isolate these layers instead of treating every missing message as a template problem.

Start with application logs, inspect provider responses, validate authentication records, test email rendering, and monitor production behavior.

By incorporating these checks into development and deployment workflows, teams can reduce avoidable failures and build applications that communicate more reliably with their users.
