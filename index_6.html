The best AI humanizer for web content is the one that changes how a draft reads without changing what it says. If you need a specific tone, Humanize Max gives you four styles and three lengths in one run. If you already work in QuillBot or Grammarly, their humanizers fit the account you have. GPTinf, Humanize AI and Undetectable AI put an AI check next to the rewrite, and Ahrefs gives you quick variants of a short passage.

To compare them on equal terms, we ran the same 103-word AI-written release note through each tool in November 2026. It contains a product name, two image formats, a settings path and the word "must", so it shows whether a tool keeps the details a reader acts on. We also checked every feature, limit and price on the vendor's own pages on the same day.

A humanizer earns its place only if its output needs fewer fixes than the draft you started with, and these six checks show whether a tool will do that for your content.

Humanize Max is a single-purpose humanizer: you paste an AI draft, upload a file or load the built-in sample, choose a style and a length, and get a rewritten version in a few seconds. The four styles are Academic, Casual, Creative and Formal, and the three lengths are Shorten, Standard and Expand, so you can turn a stiff feature description into casual blog copy and trim it in the same run.

The result highlights every phrase it changed, which makes it quick to check whether a product name or a settings path came through. If the rewrite doesn't land, Run it again produces a different version of the whole text. At the time of writing, it takes up to 2,000 words per run and works without an account.

Pricing: Plans and limits are still being finalized, so check the site for current terms.

Best for: Rewriting AI drafts of blog posts, product pages and emails into a specific tone.

QuillBot's AI Humanizer sits next to its paraphraser, grammar checker and AI detector, which makes it a natural pick if you already use QuillBot for rewording. Basic mode is free for up to 125 words per run, while Advanced mode, which rewrites more deeply, comes with Premium (the pricing page also lists three Advanced uses on the free plan). In our test, it asked us to create a free account before it showed the first result.

Premium removes the word limit and adds Freeze words, which keeps the terms you choose unchanged, along with tone insights, a history of past runs and a Chrome extension that humanizes text inside ChatGPT and Gemini.

Pricing: Basic mode is free. Premium is listed at $8.33 a month, billed annually.

Best for: Quick fixes to a paragraph or two for writers who already use QuillBot.

GPTinf is a writing toolkit built around its humanizer, with an AI detector, plagiarism checker, grammar checker, paraphraser and readability checker on the same site. The humanizer has two modes, Standard and Academic, and takes pasted text or a PDF, Word or plain-text file. File-to-File mode returns the humanized document in its original format, so a long brief keeps its layout.

Custom Style lets you paste 150 to 1,200 words of your own writing, and later runs are shaped to match it, with no account needed. After each run, an AI-likelihood estimate appears under the output, so you can see whether a draft needs another pass. Treat that number as a probability, not proof of who wrote the text.

Pricing: A free plan is available. Paid plans start at $9.99 a month for Lite (5,000 words), with Pro at $24.99 and Unlimited at $59.99.

Best for: Freelancers and content teams that want to rewrite and check drafts without switching tools.

Grammarly's AI Humanizer comes in two layers. The tool on its web page is free, works without an account and offers basic humanization of pasted text or an uploaded document. A free Grammarly account adds the Humanizer agent, with four preset styles, a custom voice built from a writing sample, and support for English, Spanish, French, German, Portuguese and Italian.

Grammarly says the humanizer focuses on clarity and readability rather than on AI detectors, which matches how most web teams use these tools. If you need to show how a piece was written, Grammarly Authorship shows which parts came from AI, which came from online sources and which you wrote yourself. The basic tool's word counter allows up to 1,500 words per run.

Pricing: The basic humanizer is free. Grammarly Pro is listed at $12 on the plans page, which doesn't state the billing period.

Best for: Teams that already write in Grammarly and want one voice across documents.

Humanize AI is built for people who want to tune the output. Without an account you get the Free tone, a Rewrite level slider with three stops (Quality, Balanced and Max) and Custom style, which learns your voice from 150 to 1,200 words of samples. A free account opens eight more modes, including Formal, Informal, Academic, Expand and Shorten, and on desktop a Writing level menu sets how plain or dense the result reads.

View changes highlights every edit, and File-to-File accepts PDF, DOCX, TXT and MD documents and returns them in the same format. Paid plans also include a public API that supports the same styles and rewrite levels as the web app, so a developer can add a humanizing step to a CMS or publishing pipeline.

Pricing: Paid plans start at $9.99 a month for Lite (10,000 words), with Standard at $19.99 and Unlimited at $59.99. Yearly billing lowers the monthly price.

Best for: Teams that need precise tone control or want to humanize text through an API.

Undetectable AI keeps its humanizer simple: one text box, one button and no style or length settings. Its free allowance works differently from the others, with 1,000 words in total before you need an account and another 1,000 once you create a free one.

Under the output, a Check for AI button runs a detection check on the result, and the same site has a separate AI detector and plagiarism checker. Its FAQ says pasted text isn't published or shared but doesn't say whether it's stored, so keep confidential details out of the drafts you run through it.

Pricing: Paid plans start at $9.99 a month for Lite (5,000 words), with Pro at $25 (25,000 words) and Unlimited at $59 (1,000,000 words). Yearly billing lowers the monthly price.

Best for: Trying a humanizer on a full-length draft before you commit to an account.

Ahrefs, best known for its SEO platform, offers a free AI Text Humanizer among its writing tools. You paste up to 2,048 characters, choose one, three or five variants, and pick the version that reads best. It ran without an account in our test.

Variants are the useful part. For a hero headline, a meta description or a product blurb, comparing three rewrites side by side is faster than prompting a chatbot three times, and you can export all results as a text file. The character limit, which fits a few short paragraphs, rules it out for full articles.

Best for: Short marketing copy where you want several options to choose from.

The best AI humanizer tool for a documentation team isn't the one a growth marketer needs, so match the tool to the content in front of you.

There isn't one winner, because these tools solve different problems. The quickest way to decide is to run a paragraph from your own work, ideally one with a product name, a number and an instruction, through two or three of the tools above, and keep the one whose output you'd publish with the fewest fixes.

Google's guidance on using generative AI content is about value rather than the tool: mass-producing pages that add nothing for readers can count as scaled content abuse. A humanizer changes phrasing, not usefulness, so the same rules apply. Google also recommends fact-checking AI-generated titles, meta descriptions and image alt text before you publish.

The best humanizer for your team is the one whose controls match your content: tone presets for blog posts, variants for short copy, change highlighting for docs and a custom voice for marketing. Run one of your own paragraphs through two or three tools from this list, then read every highlighted change, especially names, numbers and words like "must", before anything goes live.

Efficiency is excellent, regulation and ripple are tight, and the fanless window covers half of its rated output at room temperature. Taiwanese capacitors, a noisy hot run and a 200 mm long chassis are the compromises, while the ten-year warranty and iCUE LINK integration add genuine value.

Corsair needs very little introduction to anyone who has built a PC over the past two decades. Founded in California in 1994 as a manufacturer of memory modules, the company has since grown into one of the largest brands in the PC component and peripheral market, with a catalog that covers cases, cooling, keyboards, mice, streaming gear, and of course, power supplies. Corsair entered the PSU market in the second half of the 2000s and built its reputation there by choosing its OEM partners carefully rather than manufacturing in-house. Channel Well Technology (CWT) has been one of the most important of these partners, producing a large portion of the RMx and HXi units over the years. The HXi series in particular has long been Corsair's high-end, digitally monitored family, positioned just below the flagship AX models.

We take a closer look at the HX1000i Shift Crystal to see if it deserves a spot in our best power supplies list. The HX1000i Shift Crystal is a bit of an odd bird, and Corsair is clearly fine with that. First shown at Computex 2026 and launching on November 12th, it combines the side-mounted connector layout of the SHIFT models with the digital HXi platform, and then wraps everything in a chassis with clear polycarbonate panels on three sides, turning the internals of the PSU into a part of the build's aesthetics. Beneath the show, the platform is built by CWT. It holds a Cybenetics Platinum certification at both 115 VAC and 230 VAC, and it is fully compliant with the ATX 3.1 and PCIe 5.1 design guides. It also integrates an iCUE LINK System Hub and Corsair's PinProtect+ per-pin current monitoring for the native 12V-2x6 connector. The 1000W model that we are reviewing today is rated to deliver its full power output continuously at ambient temperatures up to 50°C and comes with a ten-year warranty.

The HX1000i Shift Crystal ships in a very large and sturdy black cardboard box, with a large render of the unit on the front that does a good job of showing off the transparent panels and the lit fan. The model name is printed in big yellow and white letters across the bottom half, while the badges in the top right corner - ATX 3.1 with PCIe 5.1 and 12V-2x6 support, the Cybenetics certifications, and the iCUE LINK logo- tell you most of what you need to know before even opening the package.

The bundle is rather spartan for a product of this class. Inside the box, we found the AC power cord, four mounting screws, a handful of black cable ties, and a leaflet with safety and compliance information. There are no Velcro straps, no cable combs beyond those already on the cables, and no other accessories of any kind. It is not a deal breaker by any means, but a premium unit like this that is focused on aesthetics could have been a little more generous in this department.

All of the cables are fully modular and black, with individually sleeved wires and low-profile cable combs. There is a 24-pin ATX cable, two 4+4-pin EPS cables, three PCIe cables carrying four 6+2-pin connectors in total, and a native 12V-2x6 cable rated for 600W, alongside two SATA and two Molex cables with four connectors each. Eight Molex connectors on a modern 1000W unit is extremely generous, frankly more than any reasonable build will ever need. Corsair also supplies two iCUE LINK cables of different lengths and a USB cable that connects the PSU's monitoring interface to the motherboard.

With dimensions of 150 x 86 x 200 mm, the HX1000i Shift Crystal is a long ATX unit, which is a non-issue if you are getting the matching Corsair 4500X case anyway. In any other case, you will need to check your case's specifications before buying one. The side-mounted SHIFT connectors also require some free space next to the PSU, as the cables plug into the side of the unit instead of its face, which is great for cases with a hidden PSU compartment but not suitable for every chassis. The design is unlike anything else on the market. The steel frame is black, with Y-shaped cutouts forming the fan grille and a debossed pattern of the same shape on the opposite panel, while three of the sides are clear polycarbonate panels that reveal the white fan frame, the bulk capacitors, the coils, and the white PCBs. With the RGB fan lit, the unit very much looks like a piece of display hardware rather than a power supply.

The rear of the unit is a little more conventional, with the AC receptacle, a rocker on/off switch marked with the "HX1000i SHIFT" logo, and a large perforated area with the same Y-shaped cutouts for the exhaust. The modular connector bay is on the side, behind the clear panel. The face opposite the AC receptacle hosts the white control PCB, also visible through its clear panel, with two iCUE LINK ports, a USB Type-C port, and a small port marked "TACH". The hexagonal specifications sticker is on the solid steel top side of the unit and is an envelope-type that can be closed to hide the text.

The CRYSTAL is fully compatible with Corsair's iCUE software, which can be used to monitor the PSU, adjust its fan speed, and switch the 12V overcurrent protection between single- and multi-rail modes. Corsair also integrated an iCUE LINK System Hub that, according to the company, supports up to 24 iCUE LINK devices without the need for a separate hub. The most interesting addition, however, is PinProtect+, Corsair's per-pin current monitoring for the 12V-2x6 connector. If the current on a pin exceeds a user-customizable threshold, the PSU cuts power to the connector, and the RGB fan lighting turns red to signal the fault. Given the history of melting connectors, this is a genuinely useful feature rather than marketing fluff. We can confirm that it is working as intended, as we tried pulling 20A from a single wire of the 12V-2x6 connector and the PSU shut down instantly.

Cooling is handled by a 140 mm Corsair NR140HL-RGB fan. Corsair calls it a magnetic dome bearing design, and in practice it is a rifle-bearing fan with a half-domed shaft magnet, rated at 0.55A on 12V. The fan is white with addressable lighting, which also doubles as the visual alarm for PinProtect+. It is a decent fan, and the magnetic shaft should help with longevity, but it is worth mentioning that the regular HX1000i SHIFT uses a fluid dynamic bearing fan, which we would normally consider the better choice for a premium unit.

The OEM behind the HX1000i Shift Crystal is Channel Well Technology (CWT), Corsair's long-time partner for its HXi units. Almost every part inside the unit has been relabeled as "Corsair", including the capacitors and the magnetic components, so the OEM is not obvious at a glance, but the Cybenetics report confirms it. The layout is extremely clean and obviously optimized for aesthetics, which also explains the excessive length of the chassis. The bridge rectifiers have their own heatsink, the APFC components sit on another large heatsink, and the primary switchers have their own heatsink as well, while the secondary side components are on a vertical PCB right after the main transformer. All heatsinks are of good size and quality.

The transient filtering stage has four Y capacitors, three X capacitors, and two filtering inductors. Two bridge rectifiers sharing a large heatsink feed the APFC stage, which relies on two Infineon 60R099P6 MOSFETs and two fully encased filtering coils. The APFC stage also includes two bulk capacitors, one Corsair 560 μF / 420 V capacitor and one Corsair 470 μF / 420 V capacitor, for a combined capacity of 1030 μF. According to the Cybenetics report, these capacitors are supplied by Elite, a relatively reputable Taiwanese manufacturer, and they are rated for 105°C. They are not capacitors that we frequently encounter in anything above mid-tier, though, and we feel they are not a great match for a premium unit.

The primary inversion stage uses two more Infineon 60R099P6 MOSFETs in a half-bridge LLC resonant topology. On the secondary side, ten Infineon 014N04LS MOSFETs handle the synchronous rectification of the 12V rail, and the minor rails are derived from DC-to-DC converters. All of the capacitors on the secondary side are rebranded as Corsair, with black and white sleeves that match the color scheme of the unit, and are also made by Elite. These are mostly good parts, but it is a bit ironic that Corsair advertises 105°C Japanese electrolytic capacitors for the regular HX1000i SHIFT, while the version built to show its capacitors off uses Taiwanese ones.

For the testing of PSUs, we are using high-precision electronic loads with a maximum power draw of 2700 Watts, a Rigol DS5042M 40 MHz oscilloscope, an Extech 380803 power analyzer, two high-precision UNI-T UT-325 digital thermometers, an Extech HD600 SPL meter, a self-designed hotbox, and various other bits and parts.

At 25°C ambient, the HX1000i Shift Crystal is a very efficient power supply. It averaged 92.1% at 230 VAC and 90.5% at 115 VAC across the nominal load range, with the efficiency peaking at 93.6% (230 VAC) and 92.2% (115 VAC) at around 40% load. The curve is relatively flat after that, with the unit still achieving 91% at 230 VAC and 88.5% at 115 VAC while delivering its full 1000W. Friendly fraud prevention comes down to three jobs you can do in code: log the right checkout data, block risky payments before they settle, and automate your response when a dispute still lands. Get the first one wrong and you can't win the dispute later, no matter how good your case is.

In this tutorial, we'll build each piece in Node.js in a way that works with any payment service provider (PSP). Your code owns the data and the logic, and a thin adapter talks to Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com or whoever processes your payments.

Friendly fraud happens when a customer makes a real purchase, then disputes it with their card issuer. They might claim they didn't authorize it, that it never arrived, or that they canceled. Sometimes it's honest confusion. A spouse used the saved card, or the charge showed up under a name they didn't recognize. Sometimes it's a free refund with extra steps.

Either way, the money leaves your account. A dispute (also called a chargeback) reverses the payment, and your processor charges a dispute fee on top. Lose too many, and the card networks put your account into a monitoring program with higher fees.

It's also common. Visa puts friendly fraud at around 20% of fraudulent disputes globally, and up to 30% for high-volume online merchants. Mastercard goes further: it says first-party fraud makes up 75% of the fraud online businesses experience.

So why is this your problem and not finance's? Because the evidence that wins these disputes is created at checkout, by your code. If your app never stored the customer's IP address, device ID or login history, nobody can produce it three months later when the dispute arrives.

Log four things on every order: the customer's IP address, a device ID, their account ID and the shipping address. Those are the data elements Visa's Compelling Evidence 3.0 (CE3.0) rules check when you fight an "unauthorized" fraud dispute.

Here's how CE3.0 works. You win the dispute if you can show two earlier, undisputed purchases on the same card, made 120 to 365 days before the disputed one. All three purchases must share at least two data elements, and one of them must be the IP address or device ID. In plain terms: same card, same person, same device, and they never complained before.

Most PSPs support CE3.0, including Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com and Airwallex. But none of them can submit data you never captured. And because the window reaches back a full year, you need to keep these logs for at least 13 months (Mastercard's rule, below).

Mastercard has its own version, called First-Party Trust, for disputes filed under reason code 4837 (no cardholder authorization). It asks for one data point from each of three categories: a device factor (IP address, device ID or fingerprint), a delivery factor (shipping address, email or phone) and a different identity factor (such as account login ID or billing address). On the dispute route, Mastercard calls a lookup service that you host, with named fields such as deviceIpAddress, orderDeviceId, orderEmail and userIdName. That service has to answer in about 2.5 seconds on average and search 13 months of sales data.

First-Party Trust is live in the US. Per Mastercard's October 2026 announcement, it reaches Eastern Europe, the Middle East and Africa from October 2026 and the rest of Europe from July 2027. If you sell into Europe, the logging work is due now.

#10373. Fast, reliable A2UI, with swappable inference formats

We've heard your concerns about the speed of A2UI and generative UI experiences: people wait while the model generates each screen. This release gives you levers to make that faster, so you can choose the trade-off that fits each screen.

With this release, we’re happy to introduce swappable inference formats and share examples on leveraging decision models with A2UI, which help building faster and more reliable generative UI experiences. Using our new A2UI Express as an inference format you can reduce latency by up to 3x, and up-to 80x using decision models like Jev to make layout decisions. Express requires up to 3x fewer tokens to generate the same kinds of UI compared to Direct JSON. We'll dive into the details in this blog post.

A2UI is an agent-optimized protocol for generative UI, where a client advertises a catalog of trusted components as a contract, an agent sends A2UI messages describing a bespoke user interface composed from that catalog, and the client's renderer draws the UI (see Introducing A2UI).

Initially agents generated A2UI JSON transport messages directly, every curly brace and nested element. It turns out that generating JSON trees constrained by a complex JSON schema is slow and error-prone for LLMs. This coupled A2UI to LLMs, which output text, and made it difficult to integrate faster System One decision models.

Now agents can generate A2UI Express, an intermediate format optimized for inference speed and accuracy, and rely on deterministic code in A2UI's Agent SDKs to translate it into the JSON-based transport protocol. Agent builders are also free to swap in any other DSL and its associated compiler for any special use-cases, and our A2UI Agent SDK(s) make it compatible with transports and renders.

The format the model generates is what we call inference_format. You set it with one argument when you create the generator. The SDK generates the prompt instructions for that format and compiles the model's reply into standard A2UI messages. This post covers how the swap works, why we developed A2UI Express as the new default inference format, and how a decision model can build screens from layouts you define in advance.

An inference format has two parts, and the SDK generates both from your catalog's JSON schema. A prompt section teaches the model the format's syntax, and a compiler compiles the model's reply into validated A2UI messages. Because both come from the schema, a format can't describe a component or property that your catalog lacks.

A2UI Express is available in the Python agent SDK (a2ui-agent-sdk 0.9.0 on PyPI), in the TypeScript agent SDK (@a2ui/agent on npm), and in the Dart SDK (a2ui_agent 0.0.1-wip004 on pub.dev). In TypeScript, you pass new ExpressFormatFactory() as the generator's third argument. The Python and TypeScript SDKs can both stream Direct JSON chunk by chunk, and they compile Express from the complete reply.

Express writes one statement per line, in a style close to Python. Each line assigns a component to a variable, and the variable name becomes the component ID. Arguments are positional: the compiler maps them to property names in the order your catalog schema declares them, and _ skips one. $/title binds a value to the data model, and Event("accept") declares a server action. I would’ve loved nothing more than to recommend this year’s Legion Tab to small tablet fans and gamers everywhere, but when this thing is $200 more than Apple’s latest iPad Mini — which in and of itself is absolutely overpriced — and $100 more than the base OLED Steam Deck post-price hike, I just have a hard time telling anyone to buy it. Really, its Steam performance was one of the few areas that made me feel truly excited by this thing, but it’s simply too unpredictable an experience at this point (and price) for me to recommend.

Even setting aside the price, there’s simply too much here that feels unfinished or rough around the edges for my liking. We have seen a post-iPad Mini discount bringing this slate to an Apple-matching $700 ahead of the holidays, but even that price is $150 more than its predecessor was at launch without a whole lot to show for it. If you’re looking for a gamer-friendly tiny tablet, I do think Legion is a brand worth keeping your eye on, especially as DroidDeck and other Steam emulation apps continue to evolve. But at this point — and for this price — your best bet is waiting for whatever model follows this one. It might still be stupid expensive, but at least you’ll be getting OLED.

A decade ago, Nvidia’s Shield Tablet felt like a no-brainer addition to my everyday carry. But in this economy, gamers and tablet fans alike are simply better off dropping $900 elsewhere.

A language model writes by predicting likely text, one token at a time. It has no separate store of facts it checks against, and nothing in that process says "stop, you don't know this". #10341. How Text-to-Image AI Generators Work, and 5 Tools That Deliver Good Results

Type “a lighthouse on a cliff at dusk, oil painting, warm light” into a text box, wait ten seconds, and a picture that never existed appears. It feels like magic, but it is a well-understood pipeline of language models, neural networks and a great deal of math. Knowing how that pipeline works makes you a better prompter, helps you diagnose bad results, and makes it easier to choose the right tool for a job.

This guide explains how text-to-image generators turn words into pixels, why they fail in predictable ways, and how to write prompts that work with the model instead of against it. It then compares five tools that consistently produce good results, starting with Pollo AI, and finishes with a practical workflow, a troubleshooting table and an FAQ. Plans, credits and model versions change often, so confirm current details on each vendor’s site before you commit to a subscription.

Early image generators were mostly GANs, or generative adversarial networks. A GAN pits two networks against each other: a generator that produces images and a discriminator that tries to spot fakes. GANs could produce sharp faces and textures, but they were hard to train, often collapsed into repeating the same few outputs, and were poor at following free-form text.

A second line of research used autoregressive models, which generate an image piece by piece the way a language model generates words. These handled text prompts better but were slow at high resolution.

The shift came with diffusion models. Researchers showed that a network trained to reverse a gradual noising process could generate images with excellent quality and diversity, and that it was far more stable to train than a GAN. Combine that with text conditioning and a compressed image representation, and you get the systems behind most of today’s tools. Newer models increasingly swap the traditional convolutional network for transformers, but the core idea of iterative denoising is still the foundation.

Under the hood, mainstream generators combine three ideas: a way to understand text, a way to represent images compactly, and a way to generate them gradually. The sections below follow a prompt from keystroke to finished picture.

Neural networks cannot read words, so your prompt is first split into tokens, small chunks of text such as whole words or word fragments. A text encoder then converts those tokens into an embedding, a grid of numbers that captures meaning and context. Many systems use encoders trained jointly on images and their captions, so that the text “a red bicycle” and pictures of red bicycles end up close together in the same mathematical space. Some newer systems add a large language model to understand longer, more detailed instructions.

This is why wording matters. The encoder only sees what you wrote, and it has a limited capacity. A short, specific prompt produces a clear signal. A long, rambling one dilutes each instruction.

Instead of drawing on a blank canvas, the model begins with random static. The pattern of that static is controlled by a seed number. The same prompt with a different seed gives a different image, because it starts from different noise. If a tool lets you fix the seed, you can make results repeatable and change just one variable at a time.

During training, engineers take millions of real images and add noise to them in small steps until only static remains. The network is then trained to predict the noise that was added at each step, which teaches it to reverse the process. At generation time, the model applies that skill to pure noise, removing a little at each step. Because it has learned what real images look like, the “noise” it removes gradually reveals something that looks plausible.

Your text embedding is fed into the network through a mechanism called cross-attention. At each step, parts of the evolving image can “look at” relevant parts of the prompt and adjust accordingly. A region that will become a lighthouse attends to the word “lighthouse”. A region that will become the sky attends to “dusk”.

A setting called classifier-free guidance, often shown as guidance scale or prompt strength, controls how strongly each step is pulled toward the prompt. The model computes one prediction with the prompt and one without it, then exaggerates the difference. A low value gives the model freedom and often more natural, varied results. A high value follows the words more literally but can produce oversaturated, over-sharpened or distorted images.

Running diffusion directly on full-resolution pixels would be very expensive. The idea behind Stable Diffusion, known as latent diffusion, is to compress images into a much smaller latent representation using an autoencoder. Denoising happens in that compact space, and a decoder turns the final latent back into a full image. This is a major reason high-quality generation now runs in seconds on a cloud GPU instead of minutes.

The number of denoising steps and the scheduler, which decides how much noise to remove at each step, shape the speed and look of the result. More steps usually add detail up to a point, then give diminishing returns. Many tools hide these settings, but the trade-off is always the same: fewer steps are faster and cheaper, more steps can look more refined.

Most pipelines finish with an upscaler or refinement pass that sharpens detail and fixes small artifacts. Many tools also offer inpainting, which regenerates only a selected region, outpainting, which extends the canvas, and variations, which restart from a related image.

A generator can only produce what it has learned to represent. Models are trained on very large collections of images paired with captions, and the quality, diversity and licensing of that data affect both the output and the legal picture. Models trained on more photographs tend to produce convincing photorealism. Models tuned on illustration and concept art lean toward painterly looks.

This also explains a few behaviors. Common subjects look great because the model has seen thousands of examples. Rare or very specific subjects, such as an unusual machine part or a niche cultural item, often look generic or wrong. Biases in the data can show up as stereotypes, for example in how “a doctor” or “a CEO” is depicted, so it helps to describe the people and setting you actually want.

A password reset email is part of your login flow. An invoice notification is part of your billing experience. Yet development teams often treat these messages as background tasks: render a template, call a sending API, and move on. That approach leaves an important part of the product experience untested until a user cannot complete the action they started.

Testing the application interface alone leaves a gap. A successful request to an email provider does not establish that the recipient can find the message, understand it, or use its links. Product emails need explicit success criteria, useful diagnostic records, and recovery options when something fails.

A reliable workflow combines application tests, controlled mailbox checks, and pre-send diagnostics for authentication and inbox placement. A comparison of available tools can help you identify which checks your existing setup covers and where additional testing would be useful. The goal is to investigate specific gaps rather than add another service without a clear purpose.

Start with the action the recipient needs to complete. A weekly activity summary can tolerate a delay that would make a sign-in code useless. An invoice needs accurate amounts and a clear payment destination. A security alert needs enough context for someone to recognize the event without exposing sensitive information.

For each message, document its trigger, expected timing, required data, and expiration behavior. Decide what the application should display if submission fails or the user requests another copy. These decisions give developers and testers concrete acceptance criteria.

For a password reset, test the complete journey: requesting the message, receiving it in a controlled mailbox, following the link, and setting a new password. Also check what happens when the link expires or has already been used.

Template tests should cover the data your application actually encounters. Try long names, missing optional fields, non-Latin characters, and large order totals. Escape user-provided values appropriately, and make sure conditional sections disappear cleanly when their data is absent.

Inspect both HTML and plain-text versions. Instructions should remain understandable when images are blocked, while descriptive links and readable text should make the next action clear. Check that every URL points to the correct environment, especially when staging and production share template code.

A development sandbox lets you inspect generated messages without contacting customers. Use it to check headers, attachments, and content. Keep a separate rendering check for the email clients your audience uses, because a sandbox preview cannot reproduce every client’s behavior.

A background queue can prevent a slow sending API from delaying a web request. It also creates another failure boundary: a worker may time out after the provider has accepted the email but before your application records the result.

If the job retries blindly, the recipient may receive two messages. Assign a stable identifier to each logical notification and record the provider’s message identifier when available. Where supported, reuse an idempotency key for retries of the same send operation, following the provider’s documented retention window.

Distinguish retries from intentional resends. A user asking for a new reset link is a different event from a worker repeating an uncertain request. Define which link remains valid when another is issued, and test that behavior explicitly.

Retries also need limits. Temporary service failures may justify another attempt with backoff. A permanently invalid address needs a correction or suppression workflow, rather than repeated sending.

Provider events describe different stages of the sending process. An acceptance response means the provider accepted the request. A delivery event generally means the receiving mail server accepted the message; it does not prove inbox placement or that a person read it.

Capture the events your provider exposes, including bounces, delays, and complaints. Associate them with the original notification so support staff can investigate a particular report. Confirm each event’s documented meaning instead of treating similar labels across providers as interchangeable.

Webhook handlers should verify incoming requests using the provider’s documented mechanism and tolerate duplicate notifications. Avoid assuming events always arrive in chronological order. Record enough history to explain what happened without allowing a late event to overwrite a more informative state.

Before releasing a significant template or infrastructure change, send controlled messages through the production sending configuration to test accounts at commonly used mailbox providers. Use synthetic data, and check placement, rendering, link behavior, and how long the message takes to arrive.

Inspect authentication results in the received headers. Published DNS records are only part of the configuration; the actual message must use the expected signing domain and sending infrastructure. If you change providers or domains, repeat these checks rather than assuming the previous results still apply.

Treat test mailbox results as samples. They can reveal problems, but they cannot guarantee identical placement for every recipient. Mailbox settings, filtering decisions, and recipient history can produce different outcomes.

Provide a resend option with sensible rate limits and explain when users should expect the message. Where appropriate, let signed-in users verify or correct their address. For public account-recovery forms, avoid responses that reveal whether a particular address has an account.

Give support staff a way to distinguish a notification that was never generated from a failed submission or a message accepted by the receiving server. Protect that diagnostic access, limit retention, and avoid logging reset tokens, private message bodies, or unnecessary personal information.

Reliable product email depends on the whole journey, from the triggering event to the recipient’s next action. Testing each stage and making failures observable helps teams resolve problems before users repeatedly click resend or abandon the task.

Transactional emails are easy to overlook during application development. A registration form works, the database stores the user's information, and the backend successfully triggers an email. Everything appears to be functioning as expected.

Then a user reports that their password-reset message never arrived.

The problem might not be in the email template or the sending function. It could involve DNS configuration, authentication failures, message formatting, queue processing, recipient-server policies, or an incorrectly configured production environment.

For developers building SaaS applications, ecommerce platforms, and web services, reliable email delivery is an important part of the user experience. An account-verification message that never arrives can prevent someone from accessing an application, while a delayed security notification can create unnecessary confusion.

Understanding how to investigate these problems requires looking beyond the email-sending function. This guide explores a practical debugging workflow, the technical layers involved, and the tools developers can use to identify common email delivery failures.

One of the first mistakes developers make when troubleshooting email is assuming that a successful API response means the message has reached the recipient.

When an application submits an email through an SMTP server or an email service provider's API, the provider may accept the request and return a successful response. However, the receiving mail server still needs to process the message.

Depending on its configuration and policies, the receiving server might:

Accept the message and place it in the inbox.

Accept the message but classify it as spam.

Reject the message because of authentication or policy issues.

Accept the message but place it in another folder.

A successful request therefore confirms only that a particular stage of the process completed.

Developers should distinguish between three important states:

Submission: The application successfully handed the message to its sending provider.

Acceptance: The receiving mail server accepted responsibility for the message.

Inbox placement: The message became visible in the recipient's expected inbox.

These distinctions make troubleshooting considerably easier. Instead of immediately rewriting an email template, developers can identify which stage is actually failing.

Before investigating external email infrastructure, examine the application's own implementation.

A common example is a password-reset workflow. The application receives a request, generates a temporary token, creates a reset URL, and sends an email containing that URL.

Several problems can occur before the message even reaches an email provider.

The application generates an expired token.

The reset URL points to a staging environment.

An asynchronous task fails before processing the message.

The email service credentials are incorrect.

An API request times out without being retried.

The application records success before confirming the provider's response.

A few patterns and anti-patterns from running this loop ourselves and watching teams do the same.

Lead with a small, representative dataset, not a comprehensive one. The temptation when starting an eval is to try to cover every edge case the agent will ever see. The result is a dataset that takes an hour to run and that nobody runs on a change. Four cases that run in under a minute, as above, exercise the main behaviors and get run on every change. A regression dataset of three hundred cases that runs for an hour becomes a release gate at best and shelfware at worst.

Snapshot result files, not scores. The score is a summary; the result file is the evidence. When a regression surfaces three weeks after a prompt change, having the full result JSON from before and after the change (not just the mean score) is what lets eval compare show the specific case that moved. The per-case breakdown in Step 4 above would not have been possible from scores alone.

Separate the dataset from its expectations. The dataset is the inputs. The eval config is the expectations. They evolve on different cadences: the dataset grows as new real-world cases surface in production, and the config evolves as the team's definition of "good" matures. Keeping them in separate files, both committed to version control, makes it clear which half changed when a score moves.

Treat summary metrics with suspicion. The model-swap experiment in Step 5 is the clearest example. The mean dropped by half a point, but the per-case breakdown showed the drop was concentrated in one case while the others held or improved. A team that only looked at the mean would have made the wrong call.

More evals do not automatically lead to more fixes. A recent controlled study we ran found that coding agents using agents-cli run four times as many evals as coding agents without it, but change the agent after an eval about as often as the baseline. The behavior gain comes from what the skills put into the build at the start, not from the inner iteration. Evals are necessary for quality, but the quality comes from acting on what the evals show.

Most Kafka deployments in the industry plan to survive the loss of a single Kafka broker or an availability zone. But that's not enough for critical Tier-0 infrastructure where downtime or data loss of even a few seconds could have a massive business impact.

In such scenarios, the failure model should consider not just the loss of a broker or a zone, but the loss of an entire region. The business is expected to operate without interruption even when a whole region goes down, and it should be possible to also roll the cluster safely while operating in that state, to apply any break-glass changes.

But what actually happens inside Kafka when an entire region goes down? What happens to the replicas, producer writes, and KRaft quorum? And what happens when that region comes back?

Let's walk through these scenarios using a Tier-0 multi-region Kafka architecture.

For a Tier-0 multi-region Kafka cluster, we need three different regions with isolated failure domains and a minimum of two brokers in each region.

Brokers in each region should be configured with rack awareness to indicate their failure domains as follows:

For Tier-0 workloads, each topic's partitions should be configured to have a replication factor (RF) of 6. Kafka's rack-aware replica assignment will spread the replicas across the three regions. If the automatic replica assignment doesn't allocate two replicas per region, we have to explicitly reassign replicas immediately after topic creation to meet our availability guarantees.

This way, if one region goes down entirely, we still have four replicas surviving and the cluster would still continue to operate.

But just running brokers in multiple regions is not the solution here. In fact, that's the easy part. The hard question is, what guarantee remains when one of those regions disappears, and does it solve the business needs?

The two brokers in that region become unavailable, leaving four surviving replicas across Regions B and C.

For any partitions whose leaders were in Region A, Kafka needs to elect new leaders from the surviving in-sync replicas.

But having four surviving replicas alone does not determine whether Kafka will continue accepting writes.

The next step is to define what is considered a successful write in this Kafka setup. For this, we need two configs:

min.insync.replicas determines the minimum number of replicas that should be present in the in-sync replica set (ISR) of a partition for Kafka to accept writes when producers use acks=all.

acks=all is a producer-side config that ensures that the producer will get an acknowledgment only when the write has been successfully replicated to the required in-sync replicas.

These two settings work together to satisfy the durability guarantees we want.

When all three regions are healthy, ISR would include all six replicas. Let's say one region goes down, then the ISR would drop down to four.

Since minISR is three, writes will still continue to work.

There may be temporary interruptions while Kafka detects the failure, elects new leaders, and clients reconnect to them. But once the surviving leaders are available, the cluster can continue accepting writes as long as the ISR requirements are met.

The goal isn't availability at any cost, but rather the system should be available as long as the durability guarantees that we designed for can be maintained.

Now let's make the scenario more interesting.

Region A is still down. We have four replicas surviving across Regions B and C.

What if we need to roll the cluster one broker at a time, to apply any break-glass changes?

During that time, the ISR can go down from four to three. But since minISR is three, writes will still continue to work.

Let's look at how the cluster behaves under different failure scenarios.

Kafka will stop accepting writes only when the surviving ISR drops below three.

This is an important distinction for Tier-0 workloads.

It's not sufficient to design a cluster that survives a region failure. We also need to consider whether the cluster can continue operating safely while already in that degraded state.

So far, we focused on the data plane. Now switching gears, the KRaft controllers have a completely different availability model.

They form the metadata quorum, and a majority is needed to continue operation.

For our setup, five controllers could be distributed as 2 + 2 + 1 across the three regions, requiring a majority of three to maintain quorum.

If either of the regions containing two controllers goes down, three controllers remain. If the region containing one controller goes down, four remain.

So, the controller quorum survives the loss of any one region.

But, unlike brokers, we cannot necessarily roll the controllers at that point because taking one controller down could cause the quorum to drop below three.

With a static quorum, one way to solve this is to use more controllers, and a 3 + 3 + 3 distribution would provide this. Losing one region would leave six controllers with room to still roll the controllers, as we only need five to maintain quorum.

On the flip side, an increase in quorum size also increases the coordination overhead. The official Kafka documentation typically recommends three or five controllers, which tolerate one or two concurrent failures respectively.

Alternatively, with dynamic KRaft quorum (KIP-853), introduced in Kafka 3.9, we can add or remove controllers from the voting quorum without restarting the cluster.

For example, with our 2 + 2 + 1 setup, if a region containing two controllers goes down, the remaining three controllers can still maintain quorum. We can then remove the failed controllers from the voting membership, one at a time, reducing the quorum size to three. This allows us to roll one of the surviving controllers while still maintaining the new majority of two.

However, dynamic quorum reconfiguration is not automatic. The surviving controllers must be able to maintain quorum and successfully commit the membership changes before we can proceed with the controller restart.

You need to consider whether you want the same availability guarantee for the controllers as that of the data plane, and whether dynamic quorum reconfiguration during a regional outage is acceptable for your Tier-0 workloads.

So far, we assumed that a region either works or goes down completely.

But the WAN itself becomes a failure mode.

In this setup, a partly working WAN between two regions can be more dangerous than a link that's completely down.

Increased latency, packet loss, or intermittent connectivity issues between regions can cause replication to lag, ultimately resulting in replicas falling out of ISR.

The three regions might be independently healthy, but if the connectivity between them is not, then the availability of the cluster might be impacted.

Going back to the CAP theorem, to tolerate a network partition, we must choose between consistency and availability.

So, if a partition's ISR falls below the configured min.insync.replicas of three due to a network partition, Kafka stops accepting writes with acks=all rather than weakening those guarantees.

Similarly, if the KRaft controller quorum is lost, then no metadata changes, like leader elections, can happen in the cluster, even though existing data-plane traffic may continue for some time.

The availability guarantees we discussed also come with their own costs.

The first cost is client latency. With cross-region replication and acks=all, the network latency between these regions directly affects producer latency. Consumers can also pay the WAN latency cost when consuming from partition leaders located in a different region.

The second cost is replication overhead. With RF=6, there is additional storage, network traffic, and replication-related coordination cost in the brokers compared to a smaller number of replicas.

So, in an architecture like this, monitoring the health of the network paths between these regions is as critical as monitoring the Kafka brokers and controllers.

Metrics like round-trip latency (RTT) between regions, packet loss, TCP retransmissions, connection errors/timeouts, and network throughput/bandwidth utilization are a good start.

When one region goes down, all the partition leaders in that region would move to the two other surviving regions.

This implies that those two regions would have to handle more producer and consumer traffic, network throughput, and disk I/O.

Even if the cluster is designed to satisfy its ISR and controller quorum requirements, if it's not sized to handle these failures when one region goes down, the surviving regions would be overloaded, resulting in higher latencies, timeouts, and operational instability.

So, for Tier-0 infrastructure, capacity planning should always be done for failure states and not stop at normal operations.

Now let's say Region A comes back online.

The brokers in the recovered region might have to catch up on a significant amount of data before they become fully in sync again.

This can result in a sudden burst of cross-region network traffic and disk I/O across all regions.

Region recovery needs to be controlled so that it doesn't overwhelm the healthy part of the cluster.

This can be achieved by throttling the replica catch-up, with the trade-off of longer recovery time to minimize the impact on live production traffic.

Replication throttling needs a deeper discussion of its own, so we won't go into details here.

The important point is that bringing a failed region back online is not simply a matter of restarting its brokers. We need to make sure that the recovery process itself doesn't impact the business.

There is no shortage of ways to deploy Kafka across multiple regions. The challenge here is to pick the right architecture that addresses all our durability guarantees.

For Tier-0 workloads, this means designing the architecture around failures first.

What happens when a region goes down? Can we still roll the cluster? What continues to work, and at what point do we deliberately stop accepting writes?

Lastly, can we safely bring back the region that was down without impacting the business?

That is ultimately the difference between simply running Kafka across multiple regions versus actually supporting a mission-critical Tier-0 workload.

Generative UI is the layer that lets agents build interfaces at runtime, shaped by what each request needs - giving users a rich, interactive experience. Implementing that takes two pieces: a way to describe the UI and an agentic stack that connects your agent to your application and renders that UI.

Generative UI covers a spectrum of approaches, from using predefined higher order components, to enabling your AI agent to have the flexibility to decide how to build the UI.

At the controlled end, you build each component ahead of time, and the agent picks which one to render and with what data. It's brand-safe but it does not allow for deep customization and personalization based on the user’s intent. Frontend developers can now ship the building blocks once, and the agent composes them for each request.

We sent 1,000 streaming calls at the same moment through three self-hosted LLM gateways: the Langfuse AI Gateway, the LiteLLM proxy and AcruxCore. Each gateway was limited to 2 CPUs. As shipped, Langfuse finished 261 of the 1,000 streams and LiteLLM finished 403. Neither failure came from the gateway's own code.

Langfuse's failures came from Linux's open-file limit, which Docker sets to 1,024 by default. LiteLLM had that problem too, plus a second one: an empty key cache sent hundreds of database lookups at once, and the ones that waited longer than 10 seconds were rejected. This article shows how each failure looked, how we traced it to its cause, and the settings that fixed it.

This benchmark measured the resource consumption of three LLM gateways under scaling concurrent streaming loads.

Full latency, memory, and feature results are available in the Langfuse vs LiteLLM vs AcruxCore benchmark.

First-token times are medians of the streams that succeeded. Up to 250 streams, every gateway succeeded. Then the errors started, and they did not look alike:

A 502 and a 503 point at different layers, so we expected two different causes. Two of the three turned out to share one cause.

Linux counts every network connection as an open file, and it caps how many open files one process may hold. The cap has two values. The soft limit is the cap that is in force now. The hard limit is the ceiling: a process may raise its own soft limit up to that value, and no further.

A streaming gateway holds two connections for each stream: one from the client, and one to the model provider. That makes about 1,000 open files at 500 streams, and about 2,000 at 1,000 streams. A gateway also needs a few more files for its database, its logs and its own libraries.

Docker on our machine gave every container a soft limit of 1,024 and a hard limit of 524,288. All three gateways received the same two numbers. What differed was how each program treats them when it starts:

Node.js raises its own soft limit to the hard limit at startup. The Rust and Python services keep 1,024, and neither project's Compose file sets a higher value. So the two gateways ran out of open files near 500 streams, which is the point where the errors started.

The out-of-files error appeared as a failed connection, not as a clear message. The Langfuse gateway's logs showed failed connections to OpenAI, and also failed connections to Langfuse's own web service, which the gateway asks to check each key. Both come from one cause: a process that cannot open another file cannot open another socket. Its client then receives a 502.

If the gateway runs under a supervisor or several workers, check each worker's process ID instead of process 1. To see how close a process is to its limit under load, count its open file descriptors:

The same ulimits block works on the LiteLLM service. On Kubernetes or another platform, check /proc//limits inside a running container rather than assuming a value.

With the open-file limit raised, the Langfuse gateway finished all 1,000 streams. LiteLLM still returned 503 errors. To separate the two causes, we ran LiteLLM with one worker and only the open-file limit raised. All 500 streams succeeded in that ladder, but 595 of 1,000 failed with 503.

The 503 message names the authentication database, so we read LiteLLM's logs and its source. The log gave the same reason for all 840 failed streams in these runs, 595 in that ladder and 245 in the cold-cache runs below:

When 500 calls arrive while the key is not in the cache, each call looks the key up in Postgres. All of those lookups queue for 10 connections. The worker was also using its whole CPU allowance at this point, which slows every query it sends and receives. We infer that this CPU load lengthens the queue, but we did not measure it separately. Sprint estimates usually fail because development teams heavily rely on memory and assumptions instead of evidence. They assume similar tasks will take the same amount of time. Based on these half-baked assumptions, most managers plan around ideal sprint working hours. The backlog then fills with commitments that look realistic on paper but often end up delayed.

Actual development rarely follows that plan. There are always unexpected bugs and task dependencies that consume more time than teams account for in their estimates. Even the buffer time set aside for unexpected work may not be enough. This causes tasks to take longer than expected and pushes sprint commitments behind schedule.

Developer time tracking helps teams make better estimates with historical data. It shows how long previous tasks actually took, which provides managers with an evidence-based reference when they are planning similar work.

Reviewing this data before committing to a sprint scope turns estimation from guesswork into an evidence-based process.

Historical time data offers deeper insights, depending on how you collect and analyze it. Most importantly, it explains why planned work took longer than expected.

Before sprint planning, the following records should be reviewed.

Completed task duration: Check the time spent on finished tasks. Managers should compare similar work to understand typical development effort.

Actual versus estimated hours: Management should observe and identify differences between planned and recorded hours. If there are repeated delays, to some extent, it indicates inaccurate estimates.

Carryover work: Review unfinished tasks from previous sprints. Determine whether poor estimates or external delays caused the spillover..

Interruptions: Measure time spent on urgent requests and production issues. These activities reduce the development capacity that you had planned earlier.

Meetings and ceremonies: Review the time being spent on planning sessions and sprint reviews.

Bug-fix time: Examine hours spent resolving defects. You can also compare it to the time for developing new features.

Blocked tasks: Managers should account for the tasks delayed by missing approvals or technical dependencies.

Capacity differences: Compare team availability across sprints. Include leave and staffing changes in that time as well.

Review the reasons behind differences in previous task durations. Check whether developers encountered unexpected bugs or unclear requirements.

Estimation variance (%) = [(Actual hours − Estimated hours) ÷ Estimated hours] × 100

You wrote a good email. You hit send. Then half your list never saw it, because it went straight to spam.

That is a deliverability problem, and it costs more than most teams think. Every email that misses the inbox is a lead you paid for and never reached.

The good part is that deliverability is not luck. Mailbox providers like Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook judge you on a short list of things: who you are, who you send to, and how people react. Fix those and your inbox placement goes up.

This guide covers 10 email deliverability best practices in the order you should fix them, starting with SPF and ending with sender reputation.

Email deliverability is the share of your emails that land in the inbox instead of the spam folder or nowhere at all.

People often mix it up with delivery rate. They are not the same thing.

You can have a 99% delivery rate and still have most of your emails sitting in spam. That is why your email tool can say "delivered" while your open rate keeps dropping.

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a TXT record in your DNS. It lists the servers allowed to send email from your domain.

When your email arrives, the receiving server checks that list. If the sending server is not on it, the email looks suspicious.

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) adds a digital signature to each email. The receiving server uses a public key in your DNS to check two things: the email came from you, and nobody changed it on the way.

Your email provider gives you the DKIM record. You paste it into your DNS and switch signing on.

DKIM also survives forwarding, which SPF does not. If you can only get one of the two perfect, get DKIM right.

DMARC ties SPF and DKIM together. It tells mailbox providers what to do when an email fails both checks, and it sends you reports on who is sending mail under your domain name.

p=none means "take no action, just send me reports." It is the minimum Gmail, Yahoo and Microsoft accept from bulk senders.
