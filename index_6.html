#10255. Unifying Edge AI: How Coral NPU’s open standard brings portable, energy-viable intelligence from model to silicon

Edge AI complements the cloud to bring the best AI experiences. These rely on a continuous flow of context and intelligence across both, working as a model cascade where information is captured and processed in the right place at the right time. Ultra-low-power models on the edge can continuously sense and respond in real time, while seamlessly handing off to larger on-device or cloud models when deeper reasoning is needed.

Still, building for the edge is much harder than it should be. While cloud infrastructure has converged around a few standard software stacks, the edge is split across hundreds of proprietary chip architectures and incompatible toolchains. For ML developers, this creates a heavy fragmentation tax, where you end up rewriting kernels, re-quantizing models, and debugging vendor-specific compilers for every chip you target.

Last year, Google Research introduced Coral NPU with a simple idea: an open hardware and software standard is the only way to unify the edge and unlock developer productivity and portability. Our standards based, ML-first RISC-V architecture paired with a reliable open-source toolchain gives developers confidence that their models will compile and run, letting teams optimize a model once and deploy it across different SoC vendors, devices, and power envelopes.

Today, we are reaching three major milestones in the Coral effort:

Fixing fragmentation at the edge takes more than publishing an instruction set. Historically, many edge accelerators were designed to hit high peak-compute numbers on spec sheets, leaving compiler teams to figure out software support later. In practice, unsupported neural network layers fall back to a general-purpose CPU, which can hurt latency and drain the battery.

We designed the Coral NPU architecture and its compiler together from the start. Instead of chasing peak metrics, our focus is on energy-viable compute: helping to deliver the right performance at the right power envelope for your application.

Over the past year, we released the first three milestones of the 32-bit Coral NPU design: a C-programmable RISC-V scalar frontend for control flow (Milestone 1), an RVV v1.0 vector co-processor for SIMD operations (Milestone 2), and a tightly coupled memory subsystem with floating point support (Milestone 3). With this month's release of the Matrix Execution Unit (Milestone 4), the 32-bit architecture is complete.

Scalar and vector units are great for general control logic and signal processing, but modern ML workloads—from vision encoders to transformer attention layers—spend most of their time on 2D matrix-matrix (GEMM) and matrix-vector (GEMV) multiplication. Running 2D matrix math on a 1D vector engine forces the chip to repeatedly load weights and activations from memory, which can mean spending more energy moving data than doing math.

Our Matrix Execution Unit leverages the RISC-V VME specification (formally part of the Zvt family) to address this need and uses a dedicated outer-product multiply-accumulate (MAC) engine. Because an outer-product engine computes a full 2D tile of operations in a single cycle (256 MACs per cycle), it reuses weights and activations directly inside local registers. This brings quadratic throughput scaling to the core, configurable for up to 1 TOPS.

Having a dedicated matrix engine in a sub-10 mW envelope changes what developers can build on a small battery. Rather than stopping at simple keyword spotting or basic motion detection, devices can run continuous, multimodal workloads, including compact transformer models like Gemma, real-time voice translation on wearables, spatial audio filtering on hearables, and low-latency visual perception on robots.

To help silicon teams bring this open design straight into commercial production, the complete 32-bit RTL is available today on GitHub.

To make the hardware easy to target without writing custom assembly, we are also releasing the v1.0 foundation of our open-source MLIR/IREE compiler toolchain.

With this stack, you can bring models from JAX, PyTorch, or TensorFlow through standard representations like StableHLO and TOSA. The compiler partitions workloads between the host CPU and the Coral NPU core, handles operator fusion, tiles matrices to fit local SRAM and matrix accumulators, and progressively lowers workloads directly to Coral NPU scalar, RVV v1.0 vector, and VME matrix instructions. Alongside our open-source MPACT simulator and reference FPGA design, hardware and software teams can benchmark models and tune silicon configurations before a chip ever goes to fabrication.

Turning an open standard into commercial silicon takes collaboration across the entire semiconductor value chain. To help silicon partners bring this open design straight into commercial production, the complete 32-bit RTL is available today on GitHub, and we are partnering with VeriSilicon on the design implementation and verification (DV) to ensure turnkey readiness for silicon tape-outs. We are also working with GlobalFoundries on their 12LP+ FinFET process - accelerating core timing by 70%, and reducing standard-cell area by up to 15% across RTL iterations, with ongoing evaluation of 12LP+ ultra low-power libraries for the Matrix Execution Unit.

On the commercial silicon side, our collaboration with Synaptics shows how quickly this open standard translates into real hardware across very different power budgets without splitting the software experience.

Developed in collaboration with hardware and System-on-Module partner Grinn, the Coralboard lineup also gives teams a direct path from devkit prototyping to commercial production hardware.

With our 32-bit architecture and v1.0 compiler stack in place, we are already working on the next phase of the roadmap, including a 64-bit Coral NPU architecture and CHERI support for security.

A one-hour YouTube talk contains roughly nine thousand words, and finding the one point you need can take longer than watching the whole thing. A transcript solves that. With text in hand you can search for a phrase, quote an expert accurately, turn a webinar into a blog post, create subtitles or feed a summary tool.

Plenty of tools now promise instant YouTube transcripts, but they work in different ways. Some read the captions YouTube already has. Others listen to the audio themselves and produce a fresh transcript. That difference affects accuracy, speed and cost more than any feature list does. This guide compares six options, explains how each works and points out where each one falls short. Plans and limits change often, so confirm current details on each vendor's site.

Caption extraction. The tool pulls the captions attached to a video, either written by the creator or generated automatically by YouTube. It is fast and usually free, but it is only as good as those captions. YouTube notes that its automatic captions come from machine learning, so quality may vary, and accents, background noise and overlapping speakers can cause errors. Captions may also be missing entirely for some videos.

Fresh speech recognition. The tool takes the audio and runs its own speech-to-text model. This can be more accurate on difficult recordings and works when no captions exist, but it takes longer and often costs money.

Lynote is built for exactly this job. Paste a YouTube link, search by keyword or add a playlist, and it returns a timestamped transcript with an AI summary. Batch mode accepts up to 10 videos at once. The tool lists transcription in more than 100 languages, translation into 25, auto-chapters, one-click copy and TXT download, with SRT export also mentioned. The page says transcripts are free, with no sign-up required.

Best for: Students, researchers and marketers who want a quick, readable transcript or summary from a public video without installing anything.

Limitations: It extracts YouTube captions, so videos without captions cannot be processed. If the original captions are poor, the transcript will be too. For critical accuracy, review the text or use a service with human verification.

Before you install anything, check what YouTube already gives you. Many videos show a transcript option in the description area, and creators can review and edit automatic captions in YouTube Studio under Subtitles. YouTube offers automatic captions in dozens of languages for long-form videos and Shorts.

Best for: Quick lookups on videos that already have decent captions.

Limitations: Captions may not generate for videos with poor sound, long silences, overlapping speakers, mixed languages or unsupported languages. Export options are minimal, and there is no summary or cleanup.

Notta transcribes uploaded audio and video files and also accepts YouTube links. It lists support for 58 languages, real-time and bilingual transcription, and AI Notes that produce summaries and action items. A free plan includes 120 transcription minutes per month at the time of writing.

Best for: Professionals who handle a mix of recordings, meetings and YouTube videos in one place.

Limitations: The free minutes disappear quickly if you process long videos, and the strongest features sit behind paid plans.

Descript is an audio and video editor built around text. It transcribes recordings automatically, and deleting a word in the transcript removes the matching footage. You can import video, audio or a Zoom recording, and there is a free tier that does not require a credit card.

Best for: Creators who want to clean up their own videos and podcasts, not just read a transcript.

Limitations: It is an editor first, so it is more than you need for a quick YouTube lookup. It works with files you own, so you need the source media rather than a public link.

Rev offers both AI and human transcription. It advertises AI transcripts at 96 percent or better accuracy in minutes, and human transcripts at 99 percent or better, guaranteed within 12 hours. AI transcription covers 37 or more languages, while human transcription is English only. Pay-as-you-go rates start at $0.25 per minute for AI, and human transcription is listed at $1.99 per minute.

Best for: Legal, research and media work where mistakes are costly and a human check is worth paying for.

Limitations: The cost climbs for long videos, and the human option is slower than instant AI tools. Those accuracy figures are the vendor's own claims, so test your own audio.

Whisper is OpenAI's open-source speech recognition model, released under the MIT license. It handles multilingual transcription, translation into English and language identification. You install it with pip and need ffmpeg, and it comes in several model sizes from tiny to large, so you can trade speed against accuracy and hardware demands.

Best for: Developers building their own pipeline and teams with sensitive audio they prefer to keep on their own machines.

Limitations: You supply the audio, which means downloading it yourself and respecting the video owner's rights and YouTube's terms. Larger models need a capable GPU, and there is no built-in interface for non-technical users.

Start with the source. If the video already has good captions, a link-based tool is the fastest route. If the audio is difficult or the stakes are high, pick a tool that runs its own recognition or offers human review. If you are processing hundreds of videos or handling private recordings, look at Whisper or a paid platform with bulk features.

Whatever you choose, treat the transcript as a first draft. Check names, numbers and technical terms against the video before you publish or quote anything, and credit the original creator.

There is no single best transcript generator. Caption-based tools are quick and free, recognition-based tools are better when captions fall short, and human-verified services make sense when accuracy matters most. Try two or three on a video you know well, compare the results and keep a human in the loop for anything you publish.

Can I get a transcript if a video has no captions? Caption-extraction tools cannot. You need a tool that runs its own speech recognition on the audio, such as the options above that transcribe files.

Are AI transcripts accurate enough to publish? Often they are close, but not perfect. Accents, noise and technical terms cause mistakes, so review before publishing.

Is it legal to transcribe someone else's YouTube video? Transcribing for personal research or accessibility is common, but republishing the text may infringe copyright. Check the video's license and YouTube's terms, and get permission when in doubt.

What is the difference between a transcript and subtitles? A transcript is the full text, while subtitles are timed text shown on screen. Many tools can export both, often as SRT files.

Google’s Find Hub had a tumultuous launch for its performance, but also for a lack of clear direction in hardware. Over two years later, that’s finally arriving in the Google Pixel Tag which, even just a few days in, is the obvious gold standard in the race to build an AirTag for Android devices.

There are plenty of really good Android Find Hub trackers on the market. Pebblebee and Chipolo have been long-standing examples of how to build good dual-network trackers that work on both Android and iOS, while Motorola’s Moto Tag series has delivered two Android-only trackers that deliver better performance and more focused features. But even with the Moto Tag, there’s a clear compromise. Features like the ability to ring your phone are tied to a separate app and, in turn, have some inherent clunkiness.

Pixel Tag is the device that finally brings it all together, and it’s brilliant so far.

I’ve only had the Pixel Tag for a week and, frankly, it’s impossible to properly review a tracker in that time. There are only so many ways to artificially test out how a tracker works, as most cases where you’ll find value in this sort of product can only occur in real life.

So, with that said, let’s focus on the fundamentals.

Google Pixel Tag pairs to your Android phone just like any other Find Hub tracker – though much faster compared to some others I’ve tested – but the differences are obvious towards the end of that process. The final screen shows options for setting ringer volume and an option to set up “Ring this phone.”

Those new features are what make the Pixel Tag so good, and so appealing compared to all of the competition. A quick double-tap on the Tag’s button rings whichever phone you connected it to, assuming it’s in range, and it works pretty reliably. You can also set up “left behind” alerts (now available on other Find Hub trackers too) for when the phone leaves the tracker behind – you can set up exclusion zones such as your home or work address.

One of my favorite examples of the added polish from Google is the ability to adjust the ringer volume on the page where you actually want to ring the tracker. In a crowded but quiet space where you know the tracker is close by, being able to quickly set the volume to low is a great addition. In my book, it’s the reason to buy Pixel Tag over any other Android tracker.

UWB, of course, only makes up-close finding all the better. I found this decently reliable on my Pixel 11 Pro XL, but it is a little bit frustrating that the directional cues vanish within about 10-12 feet. Fine for a backpack or something like that, potentially a problem if your Tag is attached to a set of keys.

On that note, my biggest hardware complaint is that Google took the Apple route of having no built-in mounting mechanism. The super thin and light hardware of Pixel Tag is wonderful, don’t get me wrong, but being forced to buy a separate accessory just to attach it to my bag is frustrating! One of Google’s partners, Bellroy, built one of these accessories and it’s simple enough – a silicone case with a rope. I wish I didn’t need it, but it works, and at least you get two of them in a pack for $25.

The hardware itself is pretty good across the board, really, with the battery door also being slightly easier to open compared to the Moto Tag. Inside is a standard CR2032 battery that Google says will last “over one year.” I’d personally still prefer a rechargeable battery with USB-C or wireless charging, but this is a good enough solution given the amount of time the battery lasts – hopefully Google’s claim lives up.

Rounding out some of the key aspects here, it’s worth saying that the built-in speaker is more than loud enough at its highest setting, but the value for me so far has really come in that the low setting is actually low enough to not be a nuisance. I do find it a little strange that there’s no visual indication of where the Tag’s button is, but the packaging and app both do a good enough job of letting you know.

I’m looking forward to seeing how Pixel Tag performs as the travel season spins back up, so stay tuned for a later long-term review of Google’s tracker in the coming months. AMD’s EPYC ‘Venice’ CPUs are the first to debut the new Zen 6 architecture, and we’ve been testing two chips from the range over the past several weeks to see how they compare to Nvidia’s Vera, and more broadly, how the Zen 6 architecture departs from Zen 5 ahead of the expected consumer-focused Olympic Ridge launch next year. Zen 5 was a ground-up redesign of AMD’s storied Zen core, and Zen 6 takes that foundation and expands it in just about every regard.

Zen 6 uses TSMC’s N2P node, but it also comes with significant architectural changes under the hood. Within the core, a redesigned vector engine pushes AMD’s SIMD performance even higher, and it now natively supports half-precision FP16 instructions alongside full, 32-bit precision. It also splits integer schedulers within the execution engine, allowing for more instructions in flight through the core.

Across the die, AMD made other significant changes. The single IOD of previous generations is now split across two IODs, giving the SP7 platform access to 16 memory channels (though at the cost of PCIe 6.0 lanes compared to the previous generation and SP8 platform).

The CCDs have been upgraded, too, for the first time since Zen 3. A standard Zen 6 CCD can now scale up to 12 cores, while a Zen 6c packs 32 cores. With Zen 5 and 5c, CCDs topped out at eight cores and 16 cores, respectively. For the Zen 6c CCD, AMD also doubled the amount of shared L3 per core, which effectively quadruples the L3 compared to Zen 5c, since the CCD packs twice the number of cores.

Interestingly, Zen 6 is neither a marginal optimization to Zen 5, nor a complete architecture redesign. Stepping back, it looks like AMD pushes as many dials as it has access to to their highest level, and that holds up looking at performance.

The 256-core EPYC 9996 delivers 1,024 threads in a 2P configuration, which came out 93% ahead of the 768-thread 2P EPYC 9965 server we tested in SPEC CPU 2026’s intrate throughput test. The frequency-optimized EPYC 9686F excels elsewhere, notably in scientific computing and HPC workloads, and it serves as AMD’s competitor to Vera, at least before the LPDDR-based Verano platform arrives from AMD next year.

AMD says it believes Venice is fundamentally a generation ahead of the competition, which would be a worthless line if it wasn’t backed up by data. Intel’s Granite Rapids-AP is rarely in the conversation, and although Vera maintains some competitive positions, Venice is often ahead — sometimes orders of magnitude ahead.

Intel’s Diamond Rapids is set to launch next year, which should help balance the scales. But AMD has set an incredibly high bar with Venice, one that it will likely extend even higher for some workloads with the arrival of Venice-X and Verano next year.

There’s a lot to get to here. We have a full deep dive into the Zen 6 microarchitecture, a broader look at the chip itself, the platform(s) AMD is pushing out this generation, and of course, more than 100 benchmarks covering everything from Python performance to HPC.

The headline for Zen 6 is a 27% average IPC uplift over Zen 5 in enterprise, cloud, and AI workloads. The debut of this new microarchitecture is focused on the data center, so you shouldn’t take that 27% IPC improvement as broadly applicable to all workloads. Additionally, AMD arrived at that number by comparing fixed-frequency Zen 5 to Zen 6c with 16 cores. As we’ll get into in the next section, a Zen 6c CCD can comfortably fit 16 Zen 6c cores with room to spare, while 16 Zen 5 cores are split across two CCDs.

The compute die is built on TSMC’s N2P node, which is an already optimized version of N2 that comes with a 5% performance improvement. Compared to N3E (Zen 5 used N4P), N2P offers an 18% speed improvement at iso-power, or a 36% reduction in power at the same speed.

AMD described Zen 5 as a ground-up redesign when the microarchitecture first broke cover in mid-2024, characterized by a wider execution engine capable of dispatching and returning eight instructions per cycle, a wider frontend to feed it, full AVX-512 support, and doubled bandwidth between the L2 and L1 cache. Zen 6 is a continuation of the redesign we saw on Zen 5. It carries many of the same characteristics, as well as the same general topology, just enhanced in a number of ways.

As we go through the breakdown, keep in mind that Zen 6 and Zen 6c use the same microarchitecture. Zen 6c is just a space-optimized design that consumes less power, clocks lower, and, obviously, takes up less die space than a full Zen 6 core. But, the company uses the same microarchitecture inside the core, unlike Intel’s E-cores, which use a different microarchitecture than the P-cores.

Starting with the frontend, it’s largely unchanged from Zen 5, short of a few notable improvements. You get the same dual 4-wide decode pipes and 8-dispatch, but AMD has improved the BPU and op cache. For the BPU, it has a 64-entry Return Address Stack, up from 52 on Zen 5. AMD also increased the op cache size back up to 6.75K entries from 6K on Zen 5 (Zen 4 also had a 6.75K-entry op cache). This larger cache can serve up to 18 micro-ops per cycle, up from 12 on Zen 5.

AMD split the integer schedulers for Zen 6 (Zen 5 had a unified scheduler), now offering higher capacity, with 96 shared ALU/AGU and 48 dedicated ALU entries, compared to an 88 / 56 split for ALU/AGU on Zen 5. AMD also expanded the general-purpose registers to a 352-entry physical register file, up from 240 on Zen 5.

The vector execution engine is also improved, following on the heels of a true 512-bit register in Zen 5. Zen 6 carries forward that 512-bit data path and adds some additional improvements. Notably, the engine now supports half-precision AVX-512 FP16 instructions, which needed to be converted in Zen 5’s vector execution engine. Also notable is the FP load/store bandwidth. Zen 5 supports two loads of any size, but Zen 6 supports either two 512-bit loads or four 256-bit loads. Store bandwidth is unchanged, at one 512-bit store or two 256-bit stores.

Zen 6 carries forward the 48 KB 12-way L1 data cache from Zen 5, along with 1 MB 16-way private L2. The shared L3 is treated as victim cache for the L2, as is the case in Zen 5. However, AMD increased the in-flight window compared to Zen 5, with an 80-entry load queue and 128-entry store queue.

Perhaps the biggest change going from Zen 5 to Zen 6 comes in the density of the CCD. Zen 6 offers up to 12 cores per CCD, up from 8 on Zen 5, while Zen 6c scales up to 32, double that of Zen 5c. Elsewhere, the updated CCDs either match or improve on Zen 5/5c CCDs.

For Zen 6, AMD retained the 4 MB of L3 cache per core, increasing the total cache to 48 MB to accommodate the new 12-core limit. The maximum frequency of a standard Zen 6 CCD has increased from 5 GHz on Zen 5 to 5.3 GHz on Zen 6. Keep in mind these clocks are in the context of Venice; we expect higher maximum boost clocks on consumer Olympic Ridge.

Zen 6c has more enhancements. AMD doubled the amount of L3 per core from 2 MB to 4 MB, matching the normal Zen 6 CCD. And, with double the L3 per core and double the number of cores, the Zen 6c CCD has a total of 128 MB of L3, quadruple that of a Zen 5c CCD. For Zen 6c, the maximum frequency is also up, going from 3.7 GHz on Zen 5c to 4.1 GHz on Zen 6c.

Across both, AMD claims a “large core IPC uplift,” as well as improved power and SMT efficiency. We haven’t seen an overall IPC claim yet, though the numbers referenced above put Venice at about a 27% advantage over Turin in enterprise, cloud, and AI workloads.

In the context of Venice, AMD can fit up to eight CCDs on a chip, coming out to 256 cores in total for Zen 6c, and 96 cores total for regular Zen 6 for SP7 platforms (the initial lineup for Venice). AMD is moving past the SP5 socket available to Genoa and Turin CPUs for SP7, which is a slightly larger socket at 76 x 80 mm (SP5 is ~72 x 75 mm).

The topology of the chip is largely intact from Turin, short of the dual IO dies that characterize Venice. The two IODs live in the middle of the chip, flanked by Zen 6 CCDs. The second IOD is what gives SP7 chips access to 16 channels of memory per socket (8 channels per IOD), supporting up to 12,800 MT/s speeds using MRDIMMs in a 1DPC configuration. With RDIMMs, AMD supports up to 8,000 MT/s. The platform supports up to 8 TB of memory per socket, and AMD claims bandwidth of up to 1.6 TB/s per socket (1 TB/s per socket with RDIMMs).

Also on the IOD, there’s the hardware root-of-trust, as well as AMD’s confidential computing extensions, including execution encryption, memory integrity, and trusted I/O. New to the platform is PCIe 6.0 at speeds up to 64 Gbps per lane. A 1P SP7 configuration has access to 96 PCIe 6.0 lanes, while a 2P platform has up to 128. Each configuration also comes with “bonus” PCIe 4.0 lanes, either 8 for 1P configs or 16 for 2P.

Two 64G SerDes interfaces bring the PCIe lanes into the IOD, one of which is used to link the chips in a 2P configuration. That second interface can still optionally handle an additional 12 PCIe 6.0 lanes, giving the “up to” 128 2P lanes number. The bonus lanes run through a smaller 16G SerDes interface.

Platforms with the SP8 socket trade memory channels for PCIe lanes. The SP8 socket is smaller at 62.5 x 80 mm, and the platform supports eight memory channels instead of 16, though with the same support for up to 12,800 MT/s MRDIMMs. Memory bandwidth drops to 819 GB/s for MRDIMMs and 512 GB/s for RDIMMs at the maximum supported transfer rate.

However, SP8 chips have access to 64 PCIe 6.0 lanes per IOD, for a total of 128 lanes in a socket (plus the additional eight PCIe 4.0 bonus lanes). Just like SP7, some of those lanes are shared in a 2P configuration, dropping the total to 192 PCIe 6.0 lanes in 2P SP8 servers, with 16 additional PCIe 4.0 lanes. Both platforms support CXL 3.1.

AMD also provided a platform breakdown for its upcoming Verano range, which uses the Zen 6 microarchitecture on an LPDDR5X-based platform, leveraging replaceable SOCAMM2 DIMMs.

AMD has added a few quality-of-life features to Venice CPUs that should make them easier to manage when actually deployed. First is UPP, or Unified Power Provisioning, which is a power management feature that takes into account total platform power, allocating it as needed to different components, depending on the workload. With it, AMD says it’s able to deliver a power consumption of 830W on 16 servers to 706W on 18 servers, or a decrease in provisioned power by 15%, while increasing the number of servers per rack.

Also dealing with power is UBPS, or Utilization Based Power Scaling. It’s a dynamic power scaling feature that aligns with whatever workload is running on the server, which AMD says “helps close the gap between provisioned and consumed power.” Like UPP, UBPS is concerned with platform power, not just the power consumption of the CPU.

The most interesting addition is FAST, or Frequency Adaptive Spectrum Technology. The feature takes in telemetry from the platform and policies set in firmware to adjust the frequency within set power and thermal limits. It sounds a little more involved than a simple Precision Boost Overdrive for the data center, however.

AMD described FAST to us as allowing for two performance tiers within one socket. As an example, AMD pointed to a 96-core Venice CPU, saying FAST allows 32 of those cores to run in a “premium” category, with a base clock of 3.65 GHz and a max clock of 5 GHz, while a “batch” category with 64 cores runs at 2 GHz with a 2.4 GHz max. AMD described it as multiple commercial SKUs within a single socket.

Venice marks the point when AMD will start offering MEXT on its EPYC platforms. AMD aquired MEXT earlier this year, and it has used the feature internally. With HPE, AMD is rolling out “MEXT ready” platforms, which will, eventually, have access to MEXT licenses. The technology is interesting, especially given how constrained memory is right now.

The general concept of MEXT is using flash as DRAM. Even the fastest flash is orders of magnitude slower than DRAM, but MEXT attempts to take advantage of DRAM access patterns to expose more memory to the system without it actually being there. Pages that haven’t been accessed in several minutes are offloaded to flash storage, and an AI model predicts pages that are needed. Then, those pages are loaded back into memory as you need them.

Conceptually, it sounds too good to be true. AMD pointed out that MEXT is using a transformer-based LLM, and effectively, LLMs are predicting what words go next when generating text. Here, AMD says the bar isn’t even that high. As long as the model can stream the correct pages in, and do so fast enough to cover the flash latency, the order of them doesn’t matter.

MEXT is delivered through a Linux kernel module, and AMD has bold claims about its capabilities. The company has deployed the software internally on 145 servers, which it expects to expand to over 2,000 servers next year. Currently, AMD is using it for critical EDA workflows, such as Synopsis PrimeTime. The company says that it’s able to maintain 95% of the application’s performance on Venice with just 50% of the system DRAM. Going down as low as 25%, the company claims the app maintains 80% of its performance.

We’ll have more on MEXT shortly. The feature is exclusive to EPYC CPUs, and AMD was clear that it only applies to certain workloads right now. There are likely applications where you won’t be able to cut the DRAM capacity of a server, or where you’ll need to use a more conservative cut of 25% or less.

AMD gave us access to two Zen 6 Venice systems for the purpose of testing, both of which were dual-socket servers. For the first system, we tested the dense EPYC 9996, which is AMD’s high-core-count design, packing 256 cores and 512 threads per socket, for a total of 512 cores and 1024 threads across a 2P system. It’s a peak throughput SKU, designed to handle heavily concurrent workloads.

On the other end of the spectrum, we have the EPYC 9686F, which is still a high core count CPU in its own right, with 96 cores and 192 threads, giving a 2P system 192 cores and 384 threads. The “F” suffix for AMD’s data center CPUs notes a high-frequency SKU, designed to push performance up in more demanding applications, as opposed to the dense, frontend-focused EPYC 9996.

Both chips use AMD’s SP7 socket, which is geared toward data centers, with higher core count SKUs. The upcoming SP8 socket is focused on enterprise customers, with smaller core counts and support for fewer memory channels.

Although the EPYC 9996 is a dense design with the Zen 6c core, it comes with a fairly high boost clock of 4.1 GHz, though a much lower 2.55 GHz base clock, which it will likely be running closer to in real deployments. It comes with a massive 1,024 MB pool of L3 for the chip, as well as a 600W rated TDP, though it can be manually adjusted between 400W and 600W.

The EPYC 9686F proves out AMD’s claims about Venice, serving as one of two chips in the SP7 lineup to boost up to 5 GHz. It has a lower 500W TDP, though it can again run between 400W and 600W. This is the SKU AMD has heavily referenced in its published Venice benchmarks as a “96-core HF” model.

It’s an interesting model, perhaps more so than the EPYC 9996. It’s in direct competition with Nvidia’s Vera CPU, not only based on core count, but also on performance. These two chips trade blows in several benchmarks, and it’s clear that AMD is pushing this SKU as its “agentic CPU” of the Venice lineup, similar to how Nvidia has positioned Vera.

Vera’s use of LPDDR5 SOCAMM2 memory allows for higher transfer rates than standard RDIMMs in a field-replaceable package, though through a relatively tame eight-channel setup. Vera can get away with 8-channel memory, however, with its topology allowing for massive data movement across the chip. AMD’s distributed design necessitates a bigger memory configuration, with double the channels and access to even higher transfer rates through the use of MRDIMMs.

Starting off our benchmarks is SPEC CPU 2026, the de facto industry benchmark for server CPU performance. As usual with SPEC runs, the numbers below are “estimates.” We gathered the numbers from real SPEC runs, but we haven’t reported them to the SPEC committee, so they remain “estimates.”

For the throughput benchmark suites, intrate and fprate, we ran a number of copies for each application equal to the thread count of the chip. All of our tests were run on Ubuntu Server 26.04 with Linux Kernel 7.0, and we used GCC 15.2 for all but the Venice chips. GCC 16 brings official support for Zen 6, which we used here. However, we did regression testing against GCC 15 and found only minor differences, and nothing that would change our standings.

Using GCC 16 was important to keep our optimization flags equal. We used common GCC flags, including fast link-time optimization and march/mcpu flags to expose the correct processor instruction support. We also used the open-source jemalloc memory allocator across all tested systems.

The overall intrate scores set the tone, particularly for the EPYC 9996. It has access to 1,024 threads in a 2P configuration, multiplying the clear speed improvements in Zen 6 broadly across more threads compared to last-gen’s top-end EPYC 9965. The 9996 scored a massive 93% higher in this test, though that number requires some context.

Both throughput benchmarks in SPEC reward not only how much work is done, but also how quickly it’s done. With access to a much larger thread pool and improvements in Zen 6 broadly, there’s that multiplicative effect when looking at pure throughput. A 2P EPYC 9996 system is 93% faster than a 2P EPYC 9965 system in this benchmark, but not broadly across the board.

The EPYC 9686F provides some context, which is still good news for AMD, just not on the level of doubling the performance of the previous generation. We unfortunately didn’t have access to a 9575F for a closer gen-on-gen comparison, but the EPYC 9755 is a decent proxy, with its 128 cores and relatively high 4.1 GHz boost clock. Compared with that CPU, the EPYC 9686F in a 2P configuration is 19% ahead — not too shabby considering the thread disadvantage.

Critically for AMD, the EPYC 9686F is also 17% ahead of Nvidia’s Vera CPU. Vera punches far above its weight class compared to the x86 competition, as evidenced by its matchup against the 9755 and Intel’s flagship Granite Rapids-AP 6980P.

Flipping over to float performance, AMD did a lot of work on the vector engine in Zen 6, and it shows up in fprate. The differences between the EPYC 9996 and 9686F are smaller, though not in a way where both chips are hamstrung. Rather, the EPYC 9696F is able to push up closer to the 9996, leaving both Zen 6 chips running circles around the field.

AMD more than doubled the score with its Zen 6 chips compared to our Zen 5 touchpoints, which is a massive achievement. The EPYC 9686F excels even more over Vera in fprate with a 40% lead, despite only a minor bump in core count (88 on Vera to 96 on the 9686F). Intel’s Granite Rapids-AP isn’t in the conversation here for raw performance; though, apparently the range is still in high-enough demand that Intel has increased production in the tight CPU environment we’re currently experiencing.

These throughput benchmarks are most representative of how these CPUs will actually be deployed, running multiple copies of applications under high concurrency. However, SPEC CPU also includes its speed suite, which looks at application performance when you can dedicate the whole chip to running just that one application, more along the lines of a client CPU benchmark.

The EPYC 9686F naturally looks better here with its blistering 5 GHz boost clock. In the integer suite, the frequency-optimized chip is 21% ahead of Vera, both in a 2P configuration. The 9996 still holds up, only falling short of the 9686F, despite much more inter-CCD communication.

SPEC shows the various improvements in Zen 6 showing up in a compounding fashion. There’s the new microarchitecture with an optimized vector path, but also more cores per CCD. And also support for higher memory speeds, as well as more memory channels. In isolation, any of these improvements are solid (if rote) for typical gen-on-gen improvements. Add them up in a workload that can take advantage of all of those improvements, however, and Zen 6 is blistering.

Two of the biggest performance vectors in the agentic era are memory performance and Python performance, at least at the moment. Agentic frameworks are largely defined by Python between LangGraph and CrewAI, though alternative frameworks written in TypeScript and Rust have started to appear as well. Regardless, Python remains important for agentic CPUs right now.

Similarly, memory bandwidth is important for certain types of agents, particularly those that need to query large databases, regardless of if they’re a traditional relational database written in SQL or a non-relational database.

Starting with the industry-standard STREAM benchmark, AMD is ahead of Nvidia’s Vera by between 11% and 19%, depending on the test. However, this was with the OpenMP “true” policy for process binding, which binds processes to the thread they’re placed on to avoid them jumping around and skewing the results. Vera performs well, even with this policy, but its mesh cache and monolithic design allow it to realize big performance gains with the “spread” policy.

This policy also binds processes, but it distributes them across the chip as far away from each other as possible. AMD chips don’t see an improvement with the spread policy, and Intel’s Granite Rapids-AP sees a slight regression. Vera, however, remains on top of the pile, with a clear victory margin with the policy applied.

In the tried-and-true MBW benchmark, the pair of 9996s claim a small lead over Vera, with a small array size of just 128 MB, but Vera jumps into first place when the array size increases. AMD has certainly tightened up the memory matchup against Vera with 16 channels and support for 12,800 MT/s MRDIMMs, but Vera’s topology and already competitive memory subsystem still wins the day.

The clear differences in memory performance flatten off as we flip to Python, which is a much more hotly-contested area of performance. Starting with a broad overview, we have PyBench. Across the suite, the EPYC 9686F was 32% faster than Vera, while the EPYC 9996 naturally claims a smaller 14% lead with its relatively limited boost clocks. For gen-on-gen comparisons, the EPYC 9996 is an impressive 24% ahead of the EPYC, while the 9686F claims a lead of 35% over the 9755 – though, no small part of that advantage is the 900 MHz higher boost clock that the 9686F has access to.

PyPerformance allows us to go more granular into specific Python applications and functions, but a similar pattern appears. The rankings of other CPUs change, but across the vast majority of tests, the EPYC 9686F leads by a clear margin. It makes sense, too, given that Python heavily leans on a single thread at stock.

There are some exceptions, notably the networkx benchmark, where the throughput-optimized 9996 tops the charts, matching Nvidia’s Vera. Despite how impressive Nvidia’s chip is, considering the company’s infancy in data center CPUs and the chip’s tame spec sheet, Venice shows that the CPU game is AMD’s to win (or lose).

Breaking out of the agentic bubble, AMD has continually proven itself as the leading force in general-purpose data center applications, and that trend continues with Venice. As with all of the workloads here, they can touch on agentic AI depending on what the agent is set up to do; database performance, for example, is important if an agent is often querying a database. However, we’re going to keep our analysis at a high level here, as EPYC CPUs will be deployed for a broad range of workloads, some portion of which won’t come remotely close to agentic AI.

Starting with compression performance, AMD generally sits at the top of the charts for compression and decompression speeds, which is the case here with zstd and 7-Zip. In zstd, Vera claims an early lead at the lowest compression level. However, its poor decompression performance shows up even at level 3, and AMD takes over with a clear victory at the compression level 19, with Vera falling even further behind in decompression performance.

The long mode results are especially interesting. Although the rankings don’t change, Vera’s performance in these sustained compression workloads gets worse, while AMD is able to hang onto much more of its performance. Vera and the EPCY 9686F are within noise of each other in the level 3 compression test, and Vera falls to the bottom of the rankings when looking at decompression.

7-Zip has been, for years now, AMD’s game. The charts really speak for themselves here; we’re dealing in multiplicative factors compared to Vera, not to mention the weak competition from Intel’s Granite Rapids-AP. AMD is always strong in 7-Zip, which is compounding here considering the already high base level of performance we see out of Zen 6 across a wide range of workloads.

Onto rendering, Blender shows big gen-on-gen gains for Venice, with the EPYC 9996 offering somewhere between a 30% and 40% improvement over the EPYC 9965 depending on the scene. This is true even in demanding scenes like Barbershop, where render time can be expressed as minutes instead of seconds for many chips. The EPYC 9996 completed the render 35% faster than the EPYC 9965. The EPYC 9686F was able to match the dense EPYC 9965, despite packing half of the cores. Vera isn’t included in our Blender results because a native Arm version isn’t available.

Vera retains a top position in C-Ray, both at 4K and 1080p, though a dual EPYC 9996 configuration is within spitting distance. The script flips in OSPRay, with AMD pulling off clear leads in real-time ambient occlusion, scientific visualization, and path tracing. There are some massive jumps, with the EPYC 9996 nearly tripling the performance of the Xeon 6980P and Vera in the ambient occlusion and scientific visualization tests.

The reworked vector engine in Venice shows up even more clearly in the dim_512 tests, which further stress both the vector engine and memory bandwidth of the platform.

We run three Java suites to measure the ever-important programming language that shows up everywhere from scientific computing to database workloads. Starting on the former end, Java SciMark shows the EPYC 9686F in a clear lead over the competition, with a massive 92% lead over Vera in the composite score. For an apples-to-apples gen-on-gen increase, the EPYC 9996 managed a respectable 24% jump over the EPYC 9965.

Java SciMark provides a nice overview of some critical functions, such as Monte Carlo risk analysis that’s applicable to financial institutions. DaCapo and Renaissance are much wider suites measuring a range of Java-based applications. Many of these tests are highly specific, so the rankings jump around, depending on what the workload stresses. We can see a bit of that with Java SciMark, as well, with Vera claiming the top slot in FFTs. Venice, however, remains ahead.

In large column-based database workloads like Clickhouse, Venice also looks impressive, though not from the jump. Vera maintains its performance from a cold cache, while none of the x86 competition reach steady performance until the cache is warm. The reason should be obvious given Vera’s topology and its distributed mesh, which allows it to jump straight to its peak performance, even when the cache is cold.

Once it’s warm, the 9686F jumps into the lead. There’s a regression in performance going from the 9965 to the 9996, which makes sense for Clickhouse, as larger core counts can create issues in cache coherency.

To look at NoSQL performance, we have simdjson, which leverages SIMD instructions for JSON parsing, as the name implies. Once again, the vector engine in Venice shines, regardless of the workload you look at.

Vera showed strong compilation performance against AMD’s Turin chips, but Team Red leapfrogs that performance with Venice. Starting with LLVM, the frequency-optimized 9686F earns a top slot with makefiles, while the EPYC 9996 tops with Ninja. The drop is around 19% gen-on-gen, aligning with the performance we’ve seen with other workloads.

In PHP, where Vera is more competitive, the EPYC 9686F squeezes out the most marginal of victories, but perhaps most telling is the 9996, which is in the upper tier of chips, despite how dense it is. Building applications, the Godot game engine and ImageMagick once again show both Venice chips on the top of the stack, outclassing the performance of Vera, which previously held a top slot in ImageMagick, and only fell slightly behind the 9755 in Godot.

The 9686F shows large performance jumps in HPC workloads, which AMD says is the target of these lower-core, frequency-optimized SKUs. In CloverLeaf, the EPYC 9686F is more than twice as fast as Vera, and it’s nearly twice as fast with FFTE. In Octave, Vera retains its top position, but both Venice chips are only slightly behind.

Although not traditional HPC, the heavily threaded number crunching behind the scenes in the Stockfish chess engine allows the 1,024 threads in a 2P EPYC 9996 server to outclass the 9965 by nearly 50%. Primesieve shows similarly large leaps, with the EPYC 9996 calculating some 37 billion prime numbers in less than half a second.

AMD already had a strong position in the newly reinvigorated CPU market with Turin. The unique topology and strong focus of Vera has allowed Nvidia to make inroads in certain workloads, but even looking at two SKUs from AMD’s broad Venice lineup, it’s clear that AMD is able to push performance much higher across the board. In some cases, that’s by orders of magnitude gen-on-gen.

There isn’t one area of Zen 6, or the SoCs available with Venice broadly, that totally accounts for the performance increase. It’s small advantages throughout the design, pushing an already strong vector engine further, vastly increasing CCD density, and giving memory bandwidth a boon between support for 12,800 MT/s MRDIMMs and 16-channel memory courtesy of the dual IODs.

But the story AMD has been trying to tell, and the one that’s difficult to ignore in the current market of a one-size-fits-all approach we’ve seen from Nvidia and Arm, is that Venice is available in a broad range of different configurations. We tested two chips on SP7, but the enterprise-focused SP8 socket is arriving within the next few months. And next year, AMD is pushing out Venice-X with 3D V-Cache for HPC workloads, as well as Verano, which looks like AMD’s take on Vera with its field-replaceable SOCAMM2 LPDDR5X memory.

Even without those chips, the two SP7 chips we tested already give AMD a leading position among current data center CPUs. Intel’s Diamond Rapids could change that story come next year, but the EPYC 9686F provides top-tier performance with HPC and AI host node performance, while the EPYC 9996 provides unmatched throughput.

Behind almost every Twitch tool you have ever seen, the stat trackers, the moderation bots, the stream overlays, the alert systems, sits one interface: the Twitch Helix API. Twitch is unusually generous with public data, and Helix is the door to it. If you have ever wondered how third-party sites know who is live, what a channel's viewership looks like, or details a Twitch profile page does not even display, this is the machinery.

This article is a practical overview of Helix for developers who have not used it yet: what it offers, how its authentication works, what people build with it, and where the sharp edges are.

Helix is Twitch's current REST API, served from api.twitch.tv/helix. It replaced the old "Kraken" API, which was fully retired in 2022, so any tutorial still showing Kraken endpoints is a sign to keep scrolling. Helix covers users, streams, channels, games and categories, clips, chat, moderation actions, subscriptions, and events, with JSON in and JSON out.

Two things distinguish it from many platform APIs. First, a large share of it works without any user's permission, because the data is public by Twitch's own definition. Second, it is paired with EventSub, a push system that notifies your application when things happen (a stream goes live, a user follows, a message arrives) instead of forcing you to poll. Most serious Twitch tools use both: Helix for lookups, EventSub for live events.

Authentication is where most newcomers stall, mainly because Twitch offers several OAuth flows and tutorials rarely say which one you actually need. The short version:

Every request needs a Client ID, which you get by registering an application in the Twitch developer console (two-factor authentication on your account is required first). Requests carry the Client ID and a bearer token together; forgetting the Client ID header is the classic mystery 401.

App access tokens come from the client credentials flow, a single server-to-server request with your ID and secret. They have no user attached and that is fine, because public reads, user profiles, live streams, top games, clips, need no user context. For most read-only tools, this is the only flow you ever touch.

Tokens expire, so real applications cache them with their reported lifetime and refresh on a 401 rather than assuming a token lives forever.

A tour of the endpoints that power most tools:

Users. Give the users endpoint a username and it returns the account's public record: display name, profile image, description, Affiliate or Partner status, and the account's exact creation timestamp. That last field is interesting because Twitch does not display it anywhere on a profile, yet it is one of the platform's most useful trust signals, moderators use account age to spot throwaway accounts, and streamers use it to filter giveaway entries. The creation date alone powers a small subcategory of tools: free account age checkers, such as Twitch account age checker, are essentially this one endpoint with a clean page around it, taking any public username and returning the creation date, age, avatar, and broadcaster status. It is a good illustration of how far a single well-chosen Helix field can go.

Streams. The stream's endpoint reports who is live right now, with titles, categories, thumbnail URLs, and viewer counts. This is the backbone of every "is my favorite streamer live" notifier and every directory-style site, and polling it over time is how stat trackers chart viewership history.

Games and categories. Top games, category search, and metadata. Combined with streams data, this is how analytics sites rank categories and how tools answer "how crowded is this category right now."

Clips and videos. Clip metadata, view counts, and creation tools. Highlight compilers and clip-sharing bots live here.

Helix enforces a points-based rate limit per Client ID, and every response tells you where you stand through Ratelimit-Limit, Ratelimit-Remaining, and Ratelimit-Reset headers. Well-behaved tools read those headers and back off before hitting zero; badly behaved ones retry 429s in a loop and wonder why things get worse. For lookup-style tools the practical fix is caching, data like account creation dates never changes, so there is no reason to ask twice.

A few more edges worth knowing before they bite:

Usernames change, IDs do not. Store user IDs, not logins, or your saved data will eventually point at the wrong person.

Missing users are not errors. Looking up a nonexistent username returns a successful response with an empty data array, so handle the empty case explicitly.

Batching exists. The users endpoint takes up to 100 logins in one call, which matters the moment your tool checks lists instead of individuals.

Secrets stay server-side. The client secret identifies your application; calling the token endpoint from browser code hands your app's identity to anyone who opens devtools. Public-facing tools put a small server between the browser and Twitch.

The reason Twitch's third-party ecosystem is so large is that the barrier is so low: one public API, generous free access, and a platform where small questions genuinely matter to people. That is why the space is full of narrow tools that each answer one thing well, when was this account created, is this channel live, how fast is this category growing, which clips popped this week. Most of them are a weekend of work on top of one or two endpoints, and some of them grow into real products.

If you want to start, the path is short: register an application at the developer console, request an app access token, and call the users or streams endpoint for something you actually care about. The official documentation at dev.twitch.tv is current and well maintained, which is not something every platform can claim. Pick one piece of data Twitch collects but does not display, put a clean interface on it, and you are following the same recipe as most of the Twitch tools you already use.

#10228. Integrating TorchTPU into TorchTitan and Optimizing Models for TPU

Over the past years, Meta’s TorchTitan has emerged as the PyTorch ecosystem’s premier clean-room reference platform for large-scale generative AI model training. Built entirely on PyTorch-native scaling primitives, TorchTitan provides an unencumbered, highly modular showcase for pre-training flagship dense and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures like Llama 3, Qwen 3, and FLUX.

Historically, developers looking to train PyTorch models on Google Cloud TPUs faced some challenges: either undertake a complete codebase rewrite into JAX, or grapple with legacy torch_xla. That paradigm has officially shifted. With the launch of TorchTPU, Cloud TPUs now feature a native backend (torch.device("tpu")) integrated directly into PyTorch's core interface. Enabling a training script to use TPUs now only requires minimal changes to your device initialization code. Providing device compatibility, however, is only half the battle, developers also need high performance production-grade, distributed training recipes. This is where TorchTitan on TPU comes in. TorchTitan TPU is a fork of Meta’s TorchTitan implementation that is adapted to enable model training on Google Cloud TPUs. The fork is available at https://github.com/google-pytorch/torchtitan/

Rather than requiring engineering teams to invent custom TPU sharding strategies or hand-craft low-level XLA communication passes from scratch, Google and Meta co-developed TPU-native execution passes directly inside TorchTitan. TorchTitan serves as the definitive vehicle for scaling PyTorch on Google Cloud TPUs.

TorchTitan provides official, battle-tested recipes for pre-training large dense models (such as Llama 3 70B/405B and Qwen 3 8B/32B), Mixture-of-Experts (such as Qwen 3 235B MoE), and diffusion transformers (such as FLUX.1 12B).

Beyond initial pre-training, TorchTitan serves as the scalable trainer inside post-training pipelines like VeRL for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) alignment. In these workflows, TorchTitan handles the parameter update step while synchronizing weights with high-throughput serving engines like vLLM-TorchTPU.

Parallelism is also kept separate from the model code: techniques such as fully sharded data parallelism and tensor parallelism are layered onto a parallelism-agnostic model implementation. The exception is expert parallelism: the MoE layer performs explicit communication through a pluggable token dispatcher that each backend can implement in its own way.

TorchTitan builds a model from a configuration tree, and that tree can be rewritten before the model is constructed. This offers a clean way to replace individual blocks of a model with versions that run better on TPU, without touching the model definition or the shared trainer loop.

We use this actively. The TPU recipes replace standard attention with Splash Attention, a Pallas kernel that handles causal, sliding-window and packed-document masks without materializing the attention matrix; replace the final projection and cross-entropy loss with a fused Pallas kernel that never writes the full logits tensor to HBM; and replace MoE expert matmuls with grouped Pallas kernels. Each kernel is a JAX function turned into a PyTorch custom op by TorchTPU's torch.tpu.pallas.jax_op decorator, which is all the glue a new kernel needs. Each replacement is a config flag, so a recipe can turn it on, and a test can compare it against the plain PyTorch path it replaces. Kernels can come from tokamax, the OpenXLA kernel library for TPU and GPU.

FSDP2 is built around the GPU execution model. Its all-gathers and reduce-scatters are issued from module hooks as explicit collective calls, and overlap with compute comes from running them on dedicated CUDA streams synchronized with events. None of that is visible to a compiler: the collectives sit outside the traced region, so on TPU a torch.compile region breaks at every FSDP hook and a whole-step graph cannot be captured at all. TorchTPU provides no-op stream and event objects only so that FSDP2 runs; XLA has no use for them, since it schedules work from data dependencies.

SimpleFSDP takes the compiler's view instead. Parameter sharding is a DTensor parametrization: reading a module's weight performs a redistribute from sharded to replicated, which is the all-gather, and its autograd backward is the reduce-scatter. There are no hooks and no explicit collective calls, so both collectives are ordinary nodes in the traced graph, whether that graph is a per-block torch.compile region or the Graph Trainer's single make_fx step. The XLA compiler schedules the all-gathers ahead of the matmuls that need them and overlaps the reduce-scatters with the backward compute, inside one program. Mixed precision is part of the same mechanism, a policy that sets the gather dtype (bf16 parameters) and the reduce dtype (fp32 or bf16 gradients) on that redistribute. Hybrid sharding for multislice is the same code on a 2-D mesh: shard within a slice over ICI, replicate across slices over DCN.

On FLUX this is what brought TorchTitan to 99% performance parity with JAX MaxDiffusion: the per-layer HLO for a SimpleFSDP block is a single module with the weight all-gathers, matmuls, attention and norms fused together, which is the shape of program XLA was built to optimize.

Many PyTorch trainers either run in eager mode with CUDA graphs to cut launch overhead, or apply torch.compile to isolated regions, typically one transformer block at a time. TorchTPU does not yet support the PyTorch torch.Stream abstraction, which limits the performance of mixing eager-based FSDP2 with layer-wise torch.compile. SimpleFSDP helps by expressing parameter sharding in a way that is compatible with compilation, whether using layer-wise torch.compile or full-graph capture.

TorchTitan's Graph Trainer provides full training-step capture (forward, loss and backward), producing one FX graph ahead of time with PyTorch's make_fx tracer, skipping AOTAutograd partitioning, so the whole computation is visible to XLA). GraphTrainer also runs optimization passes: to implement graph-based activation memory policy that decides what to save, recompute or offload. The train step then executes as a single XLA program.

This is the model XLA was built for, and it makes PyTorch training on TPU conceptually the same as JAX, where the whole train step is one jitted function. Owning the whole step as one graph is also what makes the large-model compilation techniques possible: scan over layers, which runs the decoder layers as a single loop body so that compile time and memory stop growing with depth, and the graph splitter, which cuts the step into separately compiled forward and backward programs with activations offloaded to host memory in between. On Qwen3 235B this path reaches 29%+ MFU.

The optimizer can join the graph as well: While the fused_adam optimizer is very performant, a functional AdamW update can be traced into the train-step (functional_adamw_step) and ensures an iteration has no host-side work between the first forward op and the weight write-back. On Qwen3-8B this path reaches 51-52% MFU.

Every lever below is described in the TorchTitan TPU optimization guide, and nearly all of them are already wired into the recipes. For most models, getting a good MFU on TPU is a sequence of configuration changes on an existing recipe rather than new code. The order matters, because each step changes what the next one measures. A lot of optimizations in TorchTitan are available as ready-to-use building blocks that can be enabled by a flag flip (see illustration below).

4. Tune Compiler, Communication and Profile

To check that TorchTitan runs efficiently across different classes of models, we tuned and scaled several of them on TPU v7x (Ironwood). At scale, Llama 3 405B (large dense model) holds about 57% MFU from 128 to 1,024 chips. Qwen3 235B (large MoE model) reaches 32.42% MFU on a single 1,024-chip slice and 30.51% on 8,192 chips across eight slices. Muse Glimmer 30B (modern dense model) reaches 43.4% MFU on 128 chips (4x4x8). FLUX.1 (diffusion model) reaches 19.4% MFU on 1,024 chips (8x8x16).

The following case studies show how we got there for three of these models, each representing a different class:

FLUX.1 is a 12-billion-parameter diffusion transformer, which makes it a useful check that the playbook above is not specific to LLMs. We trained it at 1024x1024 resolution on a TPU v6e-8 with FSDP in bf16 and a batch of three samples. Out of the box, in eager mode with FSDP2 and full activation checkpointing, a step took 11.49 seconds. Applying the playbook in order brought that down one stage at a time. Deferred-and-fused eager execution cut the number of HLO module invocations by 28% and the step to 9.95 s (15.5% improvement). Replacing FSDP2 with SimpleFSDP reduced the step to 6.52 s (52.6% improvement): the trace was dominated by FSDP2 copying parameter shards into a flat staging buffer before each all-gather and back out into per-parameter tensors afterwards; SimpleFSDP all-gathers each parameter directly, so the staging buffer and both copies are gone (see the SimpleFSDP section above). The splash attention kernel, with sequence packing and segment ids so that no MXU cycles go to padding, took the step to 5.55 s (17.5% improvement), and per-block torch.compile, which hands XLA large sub-graphs instead of fine-grained modules and moves control flow out of the Python interpreter, took it to 3.02 s (83.8% improvement).

The final step came from three small, local rewrites in the model code that the profile pointed at: splitting a fused projection into two separate linear layers so its output no longer had to be sliced apart, simplifying the rotary embedding to plain element-wise math, and unpacking Q, K and V with a view instead of a transpose. None of them changes what the model computes; each removes copies and lets XLA fuse more of the graph. Together they brought the step to 2.78 s (8.6% improvement), 4.1x faster than the starting point and about 99% of the throughput of the JAX-based MaxDiffusion on the same hardware.

We also scaled FLUX from 8 to 1,024 v7x chips, keeping three images per TPU. The best configuration went from 2.53 s per step (20.76% MFU) on 8 chips to 2.71 s (19.4% MFU) on 1,024, which is 93% weak-scaling efficiency. Past one 64-chip cube, pure FSDP slows down due to the model’s compact size. Hybrid sharding via HSDP fixes this: it shards within each 4x4x4 cube and replicates across cubes. We used the topology-aware device mesh, which maps the parallelism optimally to the physical 8x8x16 torus.

Muse Glimmer 30B is a modern dense decoder: 52 layers, grouped-query attention with 32 query and 2 key-value heads, a 202k-token vocabulary with a soft-capped output head, and a layer pattern that mixes sliding-window and global attention, with RoPE skipped on every fourth layer. It is the case that shows how much works out of the box. The model definition, the parallelization and the training loop ran on TPU unchanged; the port came down to two model-specific pieces. Upstream implements the sliding-window pattern with FlexAttention, which has no TPU backend, so the first step was a windowed SDPA inner attention that reproduces the window exactly and gives a correctness baseline, checked against upstream on CPU. The second piece was the fused cross-entropy kernel, which we extended to apply the soft-cap to each vocabulary tile as it computes the loss, so the 202k-wide logits never have to be materialized; without that the LM head alone dominated memory. Everything else, including the Graph Trainer wrapper, was the standard onboarding ladder, with each feature behind a flag and its own test. The resulting recipe trains the 30B on 64 v7x chips (a 4x4x4 slice) at sequence length 16,384 with one sequence per device, Graph Trainer plus SimpleFSDP and fp32 gradient reductions, and reaches 5,848 tokens per second per chip at ~44% MFU.

Qwen3-235B-A22B is the largest MOE model in this set: 94 layers, 128 experts with 8 active per token, 235 billion parameters of which 22 billion are active per token. It is the case that exercises the large-model half of the playbook. At this depth compiling the whole unrolled step as one program is not an option: XLA plans every buffer of a program at compile time, and a 94-layer graph laid out flat asks for more HBM than the chip has before a step can run, while its compile time grows with every layer. Getting it to work at all meant enabling one of the two Graph Trainer paths for large models. Scan over layers is the XLA-native answer, the same construct JAX and MaxText rely on: the 94 identical layers become one compiled loop body, so compile time and program size stop growing with depth. The graph splitter is the more flexible alternative: it keeps the layers unrolled but cuts the captured step into per-layer executables, which works no matter what kind of layers you have (and does not require layers to be the same). We run both for this model and they land within a few percent of each other. Either way the activations do not fit in HBM at sequence length 4096 with 16 sequences per device, so both paths offload layer activations to host memory and prefetch them back for the backward pass.

From there the playbook applied, one change at a time, each measured on the same 64-chip slice. We raised the scoped VMEM budget so the compiler could keep more kernel intermediates on chip, which also removed an exposed update at the tail of the optimizer step. We enabled a set of libtpu scheduling flags that let XLA's latency-hiding scheduler overlap the FSDP all-gathers and reduce-scatters with the surrounding compute and offload the reduce-scatters to the SparseCores, so most of the collective time is hidden behind compute, leaving only a small exposed reduce-scatter tail in the backward pass. We switched attention to the tokamax splash kernel, and replaced the expert matmuls with specialized paired grouped-matmul kernels backed by a K-tiled weight-gradient kernel, with hierarchical reduce-scatter and the SparseCore latency-hiding scheduler to hide the expert-gradient collectives. A fused route-score backward removed a large fp32 copy from the MoE backward pass. The last round of fixes came from profiling at 1,024 chips: keeping the blocked activation layout under scan offload, raising the backward layer-scheduler threshold so the expert reduce-scatters overlap with compute again, and pipelining small reduce-scatters across the scan loop's back-edge. The recipe runs at ~32% MFU on 256 v7x chips with fp32 gradient reductions kept throughout.

We also used this model to test how far TorchTPU scales, from 64 chips up to 8,192-chip v7x pod scale, in both single-slice and multi-slice configurations. A TPU slice is a set of chips connected directly over the inter-chip interconnect (ICI); multislice lets one training job span several slices connected over the data-center network (DCN), which is how a job grows past the largest single slice. Within a slice we run FSDP over ICI, sharding parameters, gradients and optimizer state across the slice's chips; across slices the model is replicated and the gradients are all-reduced over DCN once per step, so the only traffic on the slower network is a single gradient reduction, which overlaps with the backward pass. That is hybrid sharded data parallelism (HSDP), and it is what the 2,048- to 8,192-chip runs use, with 1,024-chip slices as the sharding group. The framework behaved as expected throughout: per-chip throughput dropped by 8% going from 64 to 8,192 chips (4,568 to 4,208 tokens per second per chip), near-linear scaling across 128x more chips. One caveat: for a model of this size, data parallelism beyond about 1K chips is a scaling experiment rather than a training recipe. This is a weak-scaling run: the batch per device stays fixed as chips are added, so the global batch grows with the chip count and at 8k chips is larger than one would use to train this model. What the run shows is that the framework, the compiler and the collectives hold up at that scale, not that this is the configuration to train with.

The three case studies above are deep dives: one model at a time, with a parity test and a profile behind every change. A team arriving on TPU usually has a different problem. They have a model they already train, often a Hugging Face one, and quite likely it won’t be the model that TorchTitan ships for TPU. They may well want every last percent out of it eventually, but first they need an easy way to get it running at all. That is the problem the Transformers modeling backend solves: it builds the model from the Transformers library by its Hub id and runs it through TorchTitan's trainer and parallelism instead of its own. On TPU the backend has a TorchTPU lane with a single configuration that applies the first rungs of the playbook optimizations to any supported checkpoint, with no change to the model code: SimpleFSDP in place of FSDP2, per-block torch.compile, the splash attention kernel registered as an attention implementation with the transformers attention interface so the model's own attention calls land on it, the fused Pallas cross entropy fed the final hidden states and the head weight instead of materialized logits, and, for mixture-of-experts checkpoints, the TPU grouped-matmul experts substituted for the portable implementation. Most of the dense architectures the backend supports train this way unmodified; mixture-of-experts checkpoints run through the same path but without expert parallelism yet. None of this reaches the hand-tuned numbers above, but it is a working, reasonably efficient first run for a model that has no TPU recipe yet, and a way to find out whether it deserves one.

Sooner or later a model deserves more than a generic path: it is the one you will train for months, it has an architecture the backend cannot express, or the out-of-the-box number is not the one you need. Porting a model to a new accelerator used to be a specialist project measured in weeks, with the hard-won lessons living in one engineer's head. On TorchTitan TPU it is a repeatable procedure. The repository ships a model-onboarding skill for coding agents that captures how the case studies above were done and walks an engineer, or the agent itself, through the same ladder, with the tests, the acceptance bars and the traps built in.

The roadmap runs along five lines: production readiness, precision, hardware, parallelism and models. On production readiness, the recipes gain the operational tooling that long runs on Google Cloud expect: integration with the ML Goodput Measurement library, so a run reports how much of its chip time turns into preserved training progress and where the rest goes, and with Multi-Tier Checkpointing on GKE, which keeps checkpoints in local memory and in-cluster replicas with Cloud Storage as the durable tier, so large jobs save often and resume quickly. On precision, the float8 recipe that today ships for Llama 3 70B, built on torchao's Float8Linear and v7x's native fp8 matmuls, extends to the rest of the model set. On hardware, Google has announced its eighth-generation TPUs, TPU 8t for pre-training and TPU 8i for inference and reinforcement learning, and TorchTitan will support them as they become available on Google Cloud, with the same recipes carried forward. On parallelism, expert parallelism for the MoE kernels is the open item named earlier, and long-context training is next after it, now that million-token context windows are the norm for frontier models. On models, the targets are the next size up from Qwen3-235B: Qwen3.8-Max, a 2.4-trillion-parameter mixture of experts, and Kimi K3, 2.8 trillion parameters with Kimi Delta Attention in place of standard attention on most layers, which will exercise the kernel plug-in path as much as the scaling one.

TorchTitan on TPU is no longer an experiment. The same codebase and plain-PyTorch model definitions train Llama 3 405B at about 57% MFU on up to 1,024 chips, and run Qwen3 235B, a mixture-of-experts model, on up to 1,024 chips in a single slice. In a weak-scaling test with a fixed per-chip batch, Qwen3 235B kept 92% of its per-chip throughput from 64 to 8,192 chips. That shows the framework, compiler and collectives hold up at pod scale, even though a model of this size would not be trained at that scale. The pieces that make this possible are not buried in one recipe. SimpleFSDP, the Graph Trainer with scan and the graph splitter, host offload, the Pallas kernel library and the optimization playbook are building blocks, each behind a flag and each documented with the number it is worth. For models that TorchTitan does not ship, the Transformers modeling backend runs 500+ Hugging Face models through the same trainer with the first optimizations already applied, and for teams that want more than that, the onboarding skill turns porting a model into a repeatable procedure that an engineer or an agent can follow.

That leaves two ways to use it. The first is to adopt TorchTitan as the training framework: pick a recipe, launch it on a slice, and build on a configuration whose performance is already known. The second is to keep the training code you have and take what you need: the kernels are standard PyTorch custom ops, SimpleFSDP and the Graph Trainer are modules you can import, the compiler flags and the playbook apply to any TorchTPU workload, and every recipe spells out what it enables and why. Either way, the fastest way to find out what this does for your model is to run it.

#10216. Powering the Agent Development Lifecycle with any framework, from ADK to LangChain and beyond

End to end walkthrough of the new Agents CLI Extension System, with a worked LangChain example and a custom deploy policy

When we launched Agents CLI at Google Cloud Next '26, we built it around one strong opinion: going from an idea to a production agent on Google Cloud should take a single unified command surface. For teams building with Agent Development Kit (ADK), that opinionated path has worked. You run agents-cli create, write the agent logic, and ship to Cloud Run with observability and evals wired up, in about thirty minutes.

The agent framework landscape has grown more diverse in the months since. In addition to ADK, developers today pick between LangChain and LangGraph, specialized newcomers like Deep Agents, or in-house frameworks tailored to a specific business domain. Different teams prefer different frameworks, and the right choice for a greenfield prototype is rarely the right choice for a production migration. Enterprise admins may also have governance policies they want to enforce across the ADLC for all of their development teams, such as: a compliance scan, an SSO gate, a Model Risk Management hop.

Can a team benefit from the deployment, evaluation, and observability work in Agents CLI without being forced to adopt ADK, or without being forced to accept the default behavior of every command? Starting with version 1.5.0, the answer is yes. Agents CLI now ships with an Extension System that lets developers override or add commands without forking the tool. Version 1.8.0 ships the first-party LangChain extension, which replaces the framework-coupled commands with their LangChain equivalents and leaves the rest of the lifecycle untouched.

We will walk through two journeys below. First, to show you the ADLC with LangChain agents, we'll show you how you can scaffold, run, deploy, and evaluate a LangChain agent through the Agents CLI lifecycle using the first-party extension. Then we build our own small extension that wraps the deploy command with a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) scan, demonstrating the pattern enterprise platform teams can use to enforce their own policies across every project. Both journeys use the same mechanism.

Most agent development toolchains available today share a limitation: they are opinionated about one specific framework, and the surrounding capabilities (deployment targets, CI/CD pipelines, evaluation harnesses, observability wiring) assume the developer has adopted that framework. When your framework matches the tool's assumption, the opinionated path works. When it does not, you're left with three bad options.

The first is to fork the CLI, maintaining a divergent copy and re-syncing it on every upstream release. You pay a permanent maintenance tax, and every upstream improvement has to be hand-merged into the fork. The second is to wrap the CLI behind custom scripts, building a parallel command surface that invokes the underlying tool. The problem is that developers (and their coding assistants) forget about the wrapper and call the underlying tool directly, bypassing the intended abstraction. The third is to walk away and build everything from scratch, which can consume several engineer-months of work that the team had not planned for, and which rarely produces tooling as polished as the upstream project.

The problem shows up in two scenarios. One is enterprise customers adopting Agents CLI: in regulated industries, a central platform team often requires that every deploy invocation be wrapped with a compliance check, that login route through corporate single sign-on, or that evaluations flow through a Model Risk Management pipeline. Each of these requirements, implemented today, means either forking the CLI or building a wrapper layer. The other is teams that use ADK most of the time but need to run a non-ADK framework on a specific project. Within the Agents CLI command surface, six commands assume the ADK runtime: playground, run, and four eval subcommands. The other eighteen or so (scaffold, infra, deploy, publish, login, and friends) are framework-agnostic. Without extensions, choosing a non-ADK framework for a single project meant losing access to all twenty-four.

The Extension System handles both with one mechanism.

An Agents CLI extension is a directory in a Git repository that contains a single file called agents-cli-extension.yaml. The file declares which built-in commands the extension overrides and which new commands it adds. From the official documentation:

Extensions take two shapes, described in the Using Extensions page. A command override replaces the behavior of a built-in like playground, deploy, or eval generate. The overridden command receives the user's arguments verbatim, so the user experience stays consistent with the native command. A new command exposes a workflow the CLI does not currently provide as agents-cli . Both shapes can coexist in the same extension, and most non-trivial extensions use a combination of the two.

Extensions are distributed through Git repositories rather than through a dedicated package registry. The design draws from the extension model used by the gemini-cli project. There is no central index to publish to, no package format to maintain, and no ownership roster to apply for. Any directory inside any Git repository can serve as an extension, and Git handles the versioning, pinning, and distribution. For enterprise platform teams, this means creating an internal platform-extensions repository and pointing their developers at it, with no external registry to coordinate with.

An extension can be referenced in several ways, depending on where it lives and how the developer wants to use it:

The # syntax lets a single Git repository hold multiple extensions, with the fragment identifier selecting one of them. Enterprise platform repositories often bundle together a family of related extensions (one for deploy, one for login, one for scaffold create) under a single repo with shared ownership and release cadence.

Extensions install at two scopes. The default is the project scope, where the extension and its lockfile entry are committed to the project's own repository. Teammates and CI pipelines pick it up on checkout. The alternative is the user scope, enabled via the --global flag, where the extension is stored in the user's home configuration directory and applies to every project on the machine. The user scope is the one you reach for when an extension needs to be available before any project exists, such as a framework bootstrap extension that configures agents-cli create itself.

When both scopes define the same command, the project scope wins, and agents-cli info surfaces the source of each active command. Scoping is deliberately explicit: the Extension System will not silently merge or override extensions, and any conflict surfaces to the developer at install time or in the extension list output.

The Extension System follows the discipline of package managers like uv or npm: it resolves a Git reference to an exact commit SHA at install time, records the resolved SHA in a lockfile, and uses the lockfile to re-materialize the exact reviewed code on every fresh checkout. The agents-cli install command restores the vendored working copy from the pinned SHA without advancing the pin, which guarantees that CI and local development run bit-for-bit identical code. To advance a pin to the latest commit on the tracked reference, the developer runs agents-cli extension update, which re-resolves the reference and re-prompts for third-party trust. The complete lifecycle is in the Using Extensions → Lifecycle section.

The Extension System is marked experimental in v1.8.0. The manifest schema and the command surface may still change in breaking ways before the system is finalized, so teams depending on it should pin their Agents CLI version.

Friendly fraud prevention comes down to three jobs you can do in code: log the right checkout data, block risky payments before they settle, and automate your response when a dispute still lands. Get the first one wrong and you can't win the dispute later, no matter how good your case is.

In this tutorial, we'll build each piece in Node.js in a way that works with any payment service provider (PSP). Your code owns the data and the logic, and a thin adapter talks to Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com or whoever processes your payments.

Friendly fraud happens when a customer makes a real purchase, then disputes it with their card issuer. They might claim they didn't authorize it, that it never arrived, or that they canceled. Sometimes it's honest confusion. A spouse used the saved card, or the charge showed up under a name they didn't recognize. Sometimes it's a free refund with extra steps.

Either way, the money leaves your account. A dispute (also called a chargeback) reverses the payment, and your processor charges a dispute fee on top. Lose too many, and the card networks put your account into a monitoring program with higher fees.

It's also common. Visa puts friendly fraud at around 20% of fraudulent disputes globally, and up to 30% for high-volume online merchants. Mastercard goes further: it says first-party fraud makes up 75% of the fraud online businesses experience.

So why is this your problem and not finance's? Because the evidence that wins these disputes is created at checkout, by your code. If your app never stored the customer's IP address, device ID or login history, nobody can produce it three months later when the dispute arrives.

Log four things on every order: the customer's IP address, a device ID, their account ID and the shipping address. Those are the data elements Visa's Compelling Evidence 3.0 (CE3.0) rules check when you fight an "unauthorized" fraud dispute.

Here's how CE3.0 works. You win the dispute if you can show two earlier, undisputed purchases on the same card, made 120 to 365 days before the disputed one. All three purchases must share at least two data elements, and one of them must be the IP address or device ID. In plain terms: same card, same person, same device, and they never complained before.

Most PSPs support CE3.0, including Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com and Airwallex. But none of them can submit data you never captured. And because the window reaches back a full year, you need to keep these logs for at least 13 months (Mastercard's rule, below).

Mastercard has its own version, called First-Party Trust, for disputes filed under reason code 4837 (no cardholder authorization). It asks for one data point from each of three categories: a device factor (IP address, device ID or fingerprint), a delivery factor (shipping address, email or phone) and a different identity factor (such as account login ID or billing address). On the dispute route, Mastercard calls a lookup service that you host, with named fields such as deviceIpAddress, orderDeviceId, orderEmail and userIdName. That service has to answer in about 2.5 seconds on average and search 13 months of sales data.

First-Party Trust is live in the US. Per Mastercard's October 2026 announcement, it reaches Eastern Europe, the Middle East and Africa from October 2026 and the rest of Europe from July 2027. If you sell into Europe, the logging work is due now.

#10125. Fast, reliable A2UI, with swappable inference formats

We've heard your concerns about the speed of A2UI and generative UI experiences: people wait while the model generates each screen. This release gives you levers to make that faster, so you can choose the trade-off that fits each screen.

With this release, we’re happy to introduce swappable inference formats and share examples on leveraging decision models with A2UI, which help building faster and more reliable generative UI experiences. Using our new A2UI Express as an inference format you can reduce latency by up to 3x, and up-to 80x using decision models like Jev to make layout decisions. Express requires up to 3x fewer tokens to generate the same kinds of UI compared to Direct JSON. We'll dive into the details in this blog post.

A2UI is an agent-optimized protocol for generative UI, where a client advertises a catalog of trusted components as a contract, an agent sends A2UI messages describing a bespoke user interface composed from that catalog, and the client's renderer draws the UI (see Introducing A2UI).

Initially agents generated A2UI JSON transport messages directly, every curly brace and nested element. It turns out that generating JSON trees constrained by a complex JSON schema is slow and error-prone for LLMs. This coupled A2UI to LLMs, which output text, and made it difficult to integrate faster System One decision models.

Now agents can generate A2UI Express, an intermediate format optimized for inference speed and accuracy, and rely on deterministic code in A2UI's Agent SDKs to translate it into the JSON-based transport protocol. Agent builders are also free to swap in any other DSL and its associated compiler for any special use-cases, and our A2UI Agent SDK(s) make it compatible with transports and renders.

The format the model generates is what we call inference_format. You set it with one argument when you create the generator. The SDK generates the prompt instructions for that format and compiles the model's reply into standard A2UI messages. This post covers how the swap works, why we developed A2UI Express as the new default inference format, and how a decision model can build screens from layouts you define in advance.

An inference format has two parts, and the SDK generates both from your catalog's JSON schema. A prompt section teaches the model the format's syntax, and a compiler compiles the model's reply into validated A2UI messages. Because both come from the schema, a format can't describe a component or property that your catalog lacks.

A2UI Express is available in the Python agent SDK (a2ui-agent-sdk 0.9.0 on PyPI), in the TypeScript agent SDK (@a2ui/agent on npm), and in the Dart SDK (a2ui_agent 0.0.1-wip004 on pub.dev). In TypeScript, you pass new ExpressFormatFactory() as the generator's third argument. The Python and TypeScript SDKs can both stream Direct JSON chunk by chunk, and they compile Express from the complete reply.

Express writes one statement per line, in a style close to Python. Each line assigns a component to a variable, and the variable name becomes the component ID. Arguments are positional: the compiler maps them to property names in the order your catalog schema declares them, and _ skips one. $/title binds a value to the data model, and Event("accept") declares a server action. Ask one of the newest language models whether a backpack comes in green, and it'll usually admit it doesn't know. Ask it how to return that backpack, and it may send the customer to a returns page, an account screen, and a return label that the store has never had. That's still a hallucination: an answer that sounds right, comes with total confidence, and isn't true.

Whatever you're building, the fix is the same: stop trusting the model with the parts it can get wrong, and check its work before anyone sees it.

In this tutorial we'll build a small support bot for a made-up outdoor gear store, three times over. The first version is the prompt plenty of bots start with. Then we'll apply five fixes, run six tricky customer emails through every version, and finish with a bot where the model can't slip a made-up price, date, or product past the code.

An LLM hallucination is output that's fluent and confident but not backed by the facts: a product that doesn't exist, a policy nobody wrote, a date that's a week out, or a citation to a paper that was never published. The word covers two different failures:

Each one needs a different fix, so it helps to keep them apart.

A language model writes by predicting likely text, one token at a time. It has no separate store of facts it checks against, and nothing in that process says "stop, you don't know this". Training adds to the problem. OpenAI's research on why language models hallucinate points out that when models are graded only on how many answers they get right, a guess scores better than "I don't know", so they learn to guess.

Newer models are much better at the first one. They'll often say they don't know a price rather than invent one. But they still fill gaps with what's typical, and what's typical isn't always what's true for your business. You can't train that out of a model you call through an API. What you can change is what you ask it to do, what you give it, and what you check.

#10117. How Text-to-Image AI Generators Work, and 5 Tools That Deliver Good Results

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Type “a lighthouse on a cliff at dusk, oil painting, warm light” into a text box, wait ten seconds, and a picture that never existed appears. It feels like magic, but it is a well-understood pipeline of language models, neural networks and a great deal of math. Knowing how that pipeline works makes you a better prompter, helps you diagnose bad results, and makes it easier to choose the right tool for a job.

This guide explains how text-to-image generators turn words into pixels, why they fail in predictable ways, and how to write prompts that work with the model instead of against it. It then compares five tools that consistently produce good results, starting with Pollo AI, and finishes with a practical workflow, a troubleshooting table and an FAQ. Plans, credits and model versions change often, so confirm current details on each vendor’s site before you commit to a subscription.

Early image generators were mostly GANs, or generative adversarial networks. A GAN pits two networks against each other: a generator that produces images and a discriminator that tries to spot fakes. GANs could produce sharp faces and textures, but they were hard to train, often collapsed into repeating the same few outputs, and were poor at following free-form text.

A second line of research used autoregressive models, which generate an image piece by piece the way a language model generates words. These handled text prompts better but were slow at high resolution.

The shift came with diffusion models. Researchers showed that a network trained to reverse a gradual noising process could generate images with excellent quality and diversity, and that it was far more stable to train than a GAN. Combine that with text conditioning and a compressed image representation, and you get the systems behind most of today’s tools. Newer models increasingly swap the traditional convolutional network for transformers, but the core idea of iterative denoising is still the foundation.

Under the hood, mainstream generators combine three ideas: a way to understand text, a way to represent images compactly, and a way to generate them gradually. The sections below follow a prompt from keystroke to finished picture.

Neural networks cannot read words, so your prompt is first split into tokens, small chunks of text such as whole words or word fragments. A text encoder then converts those tokens into an embedding, a grid of numbers that captures meaning and context. Many systems use encoders trained jointly on images and their captions, so that the text “a red bicycle” and pictures of red bicycles end up close together in the same mathematical space. Some newer systems add a large language model to understand longer, more detailed instructions.

This is why wording matters. The encoder only sees what you wrote, and it has a limited capacity. A short, specific prompt produces a clear signal. A long, rambling one dilutes each instruction.

Instead of drawing on a blank canvas, the model begins with random static. The pattern of that static is controlled by a seed number. The same prompt with a different seed gives a different image, because it starts from different noise. If a tool lets you fix the seed, you can make results repeatable and change just one variable at a time.

During training, engineers take millions of real images and add noise to them in small steps until only static remains. The network is then trained to predict the noise that was added at each step, which teaches it to reverse the process. At generation time, the model applies that skill to pure noise, removing a little at each step. Because it has learned what real images look like, the “noise” it removes gradually reveals something that looks plausible.

Your text embedding is fed into the network through a mechanism called cross-attention. At each step, parts of the evolving image can “look at” relevant parts of the prompt and adjust accordingly. A region that will become a lighthouse attends to the word “lighthouse”. A region that will become the sky attends to “dusk”.

A setting called classifier-free guidance, often shown as guidance scale or prompt strength, controls how strongly each step is pulled toward the prompt. The model computes one prediction with the prompt and one without it, then exaggerates the difference. A low value gives the model freedom and often more natural, varied results. A high value follows the words more literally but can produce oversaturated, over-sharpened or distorted images.

Running diffusion directly on full-resolution pixels would be very expensive. The idea behind Stable Diffusion, known as latent diffusion, is to compress images into a much smaller latent representation using an autoencoder. Denoising happens in that compact space, and a decoder turns the final latent back into a full image. This is a major reason high-quality generation now runs in seconds on a cloud GPU instead of minutes.

The number of denoising steps and the scheduler, which decides how much noise to remove at each step, shape the speed and look of the result. More steps usually add detail up to a point, then give diminishing returns. Many tools hide these settings, but the trade-off is always the same: fewer steps are faster and cheaper, more steps can look more refined.

Most pipelines finish with an upscaler or refinement pass that sharpens detail and fixes small artifacts. Many tools also offer inpainting, which regenerates only a selected region, outpainting, which extends the canvas, and variations, which restart from a related image.

A generator can only produce what it has learned to represent. Models are trained on very large collections of images paired with captions, and the quality, diversity and licensing of that data affect both the output and the legal picture. Models trained on more photographs tend to produce convincing photorealism. Models tuned on illustration and concept art lean toward painterly looks.

This also explains a few behaviors. Common subjects look great because the model has seen thousands of examples. Rare or very specific subjects, such as an unusual machine part or a niche cultural item, often look generic or wrong. Biases in the data can show up as stereotypes, for example in how “a doctor” or “a CEO” is depicted, so it helps to describe the people and setting you actually want.

A password reset email is part of your login flow. An invoice notification is part of your billing experience. Yet development teams often treat these messages as background tasks: render a template, call a sending API, and move on. That approach leaves an important part of the product experience untested until a user cannot complete the action they started.

Testing the application interface alone leaves a gap. A successful request to an email provider does not establish that the recipient can find the message, understand it, or use its links. Product emails need explicit success criteria, useful diagnostic records, and recovery options when something fails.

A reliable workflow combines application tests, controlled mailbox checks, and pre-send diagnostics for authentication and inbox placement. A comparison of available tools can help you identify which checks your existing setup covers and where additional testing would be useful. The goal is to investigate specific gaps rather than add another service without a clear purpose.

Start with the action the recipient needs to complete. A weekly activity summary can tolerate a delay that would make a sign-in code useless. An invoice needs accurate amounts and a clear payment destination. A security alert needs enough context for someone to recognize the event without exposing sensitive information.

For each message, document its trigger, expected timing, required data, and expiration behavior. Decide what the application should display if submission fails or the user requests another copy. These decisions give developers and testers concrete acceptance criteria.

For a password reset, test the complete journey: requesting the message, receiving it in a controlled mailbox, following the link, and setting a new password. Also check what happens when the link expires or has already been used.

Template tests should cover the data your application actually encounters. Try long names, missing optional fields, non-Latin characters, and large order totals. Escape user-provided values appropriately, and make sure conditional sections disappear cleanly when their data is absent.

Inspect both HTML and plain-text versions. Instructions should remain understandable when images are blocked, while descriptive links and readable text should make the next action clear. Check that every URL points to the correct environment, especially when staging and production share template code.

A development sandbox lets you inspect generated messages without contacting customers. Use it to check headers, attachments, and content. Keep a separate rendering check for the email clients your audience uses, because a sandbox preview cannot reproduce every client’s behavior.

A background queue can prevent a slow sending API from delaying a web request. It also creates another failure boundary: a worker may time out after the provider has accepted the email but before your application records the result.

If the job retries blindly, the recipient may receive two messages. Assign a stable identifier to each logical notification and record the provider’s message identifier when available. Where supported, reuse an idempotency key for retries of the same send operation, following the provider’s documented retention window.

Distinguish retries from intentional resends. A user asking for a new reset link is a different event from a worker repeating an uncertain request. Define which link remains valid when another is issued, and test that behavior explicitly.

Retries also need limits. Temporary service failures may justify another attempt with backoff. A permanently invalid address needs a correction or suppression workflow, rather than repeated sending.

Provider events describe different stages of the sending process. An acceptance response means the provider accepted the request. A delivery event generally means the receiving mail server accepted the message; it does not prove inbox placement or that a person read it.

Capture the events your provider exposes, including bounces, delays, and complaints. Associate them with the original notification so support staff can investigate a particular report. Confirm each event’s documented meaning instead of treating similar labels across providers as interchangeable.

Webhook handlers should verify incoming requests using the provider’s documented mechanism and tolerate duplicate notifications. Avoid assuming events always arrive in chronological order. Record enough history to explain what happened without allowing a late event to overwrite a more informative state.

Before releasing a significant template or infrastructure change, send controlled messages through the production sending configuration to test accounts at commonly used mailbox providers. Use synthetic data, and check placement, rendering, link behavior, and how long the message takes to arrive.

Inspect authentication results in the received headers. Published DNS records are only part of the configuration; the actual message must use the expected signing domain and sending infrastructure. If you change providers or domains, repeat these checks rather than assuming the previous results still apply.

Treat test mailbox results as samples. They can reveal problems, but they cannot guarantee identical placement for every recipient. Mailbox settings, filtering decisions, and recipient history can produce different outcomes.

Provide a resend option with sensible rate limits and explain when users should expect the message. Where appropriate, let signed-in users verify or correct their address. For public account-recovery forms, avoid responses that reveal whether a particular address has an account.

Give support staff a way to distinguish a notification that was never generated from a failed submission or a message accepted by the receiving server. Protect that diagnostic access, limit retention, and avoid logging reset tokens, private message bodies, or unnecessary personal information.

Reliable product email depends on the whole journey, from the triggering event to the recipient’s next action. Testing each stage and making failures observable helps teams resolve problems before users repeatedly click resend or abandon the task.
