#92. Monday briefing: The many stages of the UK-EU relationship – and what’s next

In today’s newsletter: From situationship to a possible makeup, a look at the evolving relationship, and the bloc’s big feelings about the future

Good morning. Ahead of the hotly anticipated UK-EU summit this Friday, Andy Burnham is expected to make the reset of our relationship with Europe the pivotal theme of his white-tie speech at the lord mayor’s banquet in London tonight.

The prospect of closer ties – and the possibility of going “all the way” to reversing Brexit – has been met with jubilation and relief in some quarters and vehement opposition in others, while around Westminster and across the media the psychodrama has been spiralling for weeks.

At this point, I’m reminded of that Robert Burns couplet: “O wad some Pow’r the giftie gie us / To see oursels as ithers see us!” What does the EU think about all this? Are they ready to reverse what leading figures have repeatedly described as a colossal act of self-harm? Or are they viewing this latest iteration with bombastic side-eye? I posed these questions to Jennifer Rankin, the Guardian’s Brussels correspondent. First the headlines.

UK news | A woman who alleges she was raped by Russell Brand when she was 16 has waived her right to anonymity to campaign for a staggered age of consent, which would make it illegal for adults over a certain age to have sex with 16- and 17-year-olds.

Youth unemployment | Andy Burnham has pledged to intervene as early as childhood to tackle youth unemployment before a watershed review, promising “a root-and-branch rethink of how we support childhood in the modern world”.

Environment | The strongest El Niño in centuries has added urgency to UN climate talks in Turkey as vulnerable countries face “compounding” crises. In other Cop31 news, queer and gay protesters defied a crackdown on LGBTQ+ rights in Turkey to lead a civil society climate march.

UK news | Two people aged 22 and 23 have died after their car was hit by a BMW being pursued by police. West Yorkshire police said four people had been arrested and appealed for witnesses to come forward.

Air transport | The Trump administration says Friday’s four-hour nationwide Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ground stop that delayed or cancelled thousands of flight stemmed from routine maintenance. The US transportation secretary, Sean Duffy, blamed prior presidents and Congress for the stoppage.

Ahead of the summit, the pro-Brexit polity has been in broadcast mode: headlines from the past few days include: “Burnham’s plot to rejoin the EU would leave the average UK household £6,500 poorer” (Express); “Taxpayers funding pro-EU university courses” (Sunday Telegraph); “Staggering cost of PM’s EU reset” (Daily Mail).

Stay Out, a new campaign against rejoining the European Union, launched last week. While the guy you really want to hear from right now – David Cameron, the prime minister who lost the 2016 Brexit referendum – has insisted the UK’s problems have nothing to do with its exit from the EU.

Even within the Labour fold, there are rumblings of unease from MPs and unions.

But the one-day summit in Brussels this Friday does not include any formal discussion of rejoining: instead, the EU and UK hope to sign deals on food and drink, emissions trading and the youth experience scheme (a work and study exchange programme).

Still, the mood music is upbeat. Back in September, when Burnham first opened the door to a Brexit reversal, French president Emmanual Macron responded: “welcome back.” while Spanish prime minister Pedro Sánchez said: “Spain would welcome Britain’s return with open arms.”

The enthusiasm is genuine, says Jennifer. Indeed, some European diplomats have told her the technicalities could be wrapped up within a year because the UK remains closely aligned with the bloc in so many areas. “It would be such a vote of confidence in the European project for a country to decide to return after voting to leave”.

Situationship: what’s at stake at this week’s summit?

Though Burnham has predicted “some long nights” of negotiation this week, Jennifer says there’s optimism on each side as the first two deals on food and drink and emissions trading were largely agreed.

There are two sticking points with the youth exchange deal. First, the Home Office wants to limit numbers to the UK to keep migration figures low. Second, there’s disagreement over whether those students would pay UK domestic rates of tuition fees or the international rate, which is four or five times higher for some courses.

The UK is offering concessions on tuition fees if it can join Made in Europe, the EU legislation designed to protect domestic manufacturing from cheap Chinese competition. But the EU says it can’t make a deal on Made in Europe this week, because the law is still at a draft stage.

And here’s where it gets complicated. The European Commission has proposed delaying an agreement on tuition fees for six months, which could allow time to get the UK into Made in Europe, a diplomatic win for Britain. But there’s pushback from member states who want to pin down the UK on tuition fees this week. “This is a real priority for the EU, in almost emotional terms,” Jennifer says. “Lots of EU officials and diplomats studied in the UK, or their children did, and they felt this was one of the big losses of Brexit, that these person-to-person links were being eroded.”

On-again, off-again: what else could get in the way of closer working?

In play at Friday’s summit will also be a future deal on Britain’s return to the EU’s internal electricity market and a joint migration pact, with the UK pushing for access to EU crime databases to tackle the small boats crisis.

Member states are already working hard to smooth things out on the electricity front, to stop it becoming a sticking point. A couple of weeks ago, Jennifer reported on leaders from Germany, Denmark and elsewhere calling for deeper cooperation on clean energy – which she reads as an attempt to head off a row about how much the UK should contribute to EU cohesion funds for the evident privilege of cutting red tape, easing trade and, ultimately, reducing costs.

A similar row over UK contributions scuppered negotiations last year on our entry into a €150bn EU defence fund.

It could also be a challenge for the UK to realign with the EU on farming, after changing to a payment model that puts more emphasis on delivering environmental benefits. And past UK governments have been lukewarm about EU AI regulation, devised post Brexit.

Conscious partnership: will the EU welcome future closeness this week?

I learned long ago there’s no such thing as a stupid question, so I ask mine now: will there be a moment on Friday when Ursula von der Leyen offers the equivalent of Macron’s “welcome back”? Will there be a “just give us six months to sort out the paperwork Andy” vibe?

The European Commission president is, by nature, a cautious politician, Jennifer tells me, so she doesn’t expect anything so specific. “I’m sure she – and António Costa, the European Council president – will get asked about it at the press conference, and they’ll want to send a signal about the UK being welcome to rejoin.”

But behind closed doors, in Brussels and other capitals, what Jennifer hears repeatedly is that it’s up to the UK to decide what it wants. And, while the prospect of Brexiteer Nigel Farage in Downing Street has faded in recent months, there remains a deal of caution about the vicissitudes of UK politics. “Now the Burnham government is exploring the options, but there’s also an awareness that you could have a very different government in a few years’ time,” she says.

It’s not you, it’s me: what else is Europe worrying about?

The EU and its member states are preoccupied with far weightier problems: the war in Ukraine (Volodymyr Zelenskyy attends every EU summit), the rise in support for the far right, street protests, hybrid threats from Russia, a trade deficit with China that has reached a staggering €1bn a day in 2026.

“There are so many other issues crowding on the agenda,” says Jennifer. So it’s not all about us? “That’s actually been the theme of my decade of reporting from Brussels,” she says with a laugh.

There’s profound concern about the prospect of Marine Le Pen winning the French presidential elections next year, which could tip the bloc into a crisis if she pursues her resolutely anti-EU agenda, which includes reducing France’s substantial contribution to the EU budget.

Beyond that, there’s a broader question about increasingly divided and unstable politics across member states. “Every single member state has to agree to the UK’s re-entry,” Jennifer reminds me. “Who knows what the EU will look like in 2030 when Britain could be having a rejoin referendum?”

#91. Suspected Chinese spy balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing

The Chinese spy balloon that transited the US earlier this year was able to capture imagery and collect some signals intelligence from US military sites, a source familiar with the matter tells CNN.

The balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing in real time, the source said, and the US government still does not know for sure whether the Chinese government could wipe the balloon’s data as it received it. That raises questions about whether there is intelligence the balloon was able to gather that the US still doesn’t know about.

Still, the intelligence community has not been overly concerned about the information the balloon was able to gather, the person said, because it is not much more sophisticated than what Chinese satellites are able to glean as they orbit over similar locations.

A US intelligence official told CNN on Monday that “although analysis of the wreckage of the High Altitude Balloon remains ongoing, thus far, its flight over the United States does not appear to have provided critical new insights to the People’s Republic of China.”

The US also knew what the balloon’s path would be and was able to protect sensitive sites and censor some signals before the balloon was able to pick them up, officials have said.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

The balloon first crossed into US airspace over Alaska in late January before passing through Canada and down into Montana, where it hovered for a few days, leading the US to believe it was trying to surveil sensitive military sites, like Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana. It was eventually shot down by the US off the East Coast on February 4, and the incident further escalated tensions between Washington and Beijing, including the postponement of a diplomatic visit by US Secretary of State Antony Blinken to China.

A senior State Department official said in February that, as the balloon floated across the US, it “was capable of conducting signals intelligence collection operations.”

Gen. Glen VanHerck, the commander of US Northern Command and NORAD, said at the time that the US “did not assess” that the balloon “presented a significant collection hazard beyond what already exists in actionable technical means from the Chinese.”

The surveillance program, which includes a number of similar balloons, is in part run out of the small Chinese province of Hainan, officials told CNN. The US does not know the precise size of the fleet of Chinese surveillance balloons, but sources tell CNN that the program has conducted at least two dozen missions over at least five continents in recent years.

Roughly half a dozen of those flights have been within US airspace, although not necessarily over US territory, according to one official familiar with the intelligence.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

#86. ‘It’s like the 16th-century version of doomscrolling’: the Book of Miracles may fetch £1m at auction

Exclusive: Public institutions get first chance to bid for rare ‘wonderbook’ of apocalyptic visions and extreme weather

In one illustration, five suns blaze down on Leipzig in 1551. In another, blood rain falls on Rome in 1456. In a third scene, dated 1552, monstrous hailstones batter the Dutch city of Dordrecht during a storm so fierce people feared Judgment Day had come.

Created during the mid-16th century, when earthquakes, storms, floods and other extreme weather events were often interpreted as ominous warnings from God, the Book of Miracles contains 167 paintings of apocalyptic visions, celestial wonders and meteorological phenomena.

It is the only known copy. To this day, no one is sure why it was created.

Now, public institutions will have their first chance to bid for the Book of Miracles since scholars learned it existed. It is expected to fetch between £700,000 and £1m when it is auctioned in London on 10 December, as the star lot of the Christie’s valuable books and manuscripts sale.

Members of the public will also have a rare opportunity to see it in person for six days before it goes under the hammer.

For nearly half a millennium, scholars were unaware of its existence. Found in the library of a small baroque castle in southern Germany nearly 20 years ago, the manuscript, which is from 1550s Augsburg, was put up for auction alongside the castle’s furniture, carpets and tankards in 2008 and sold for just over £100,000.

Shortly after the sale, the manuscript entered the private collection of Mickey Cartin, one of Connecticut’s biggest contemporary art collectors.

Eugenio Donadoni, Christie’s international specialist in medieval and Renaissance manuscripts, said the book was an “exceptionally rare” German wonderbook.

“German wonderbooks functioned as the sensationalist mass media of their day,” he said. “They were used by Lutheran theologians such as Martin Luther and Philip Melanchthon to further their Protestant eschatology, but crucially – with all the depictions of comets, cosmological events, natural disasters, all verified by witnesses, with exact dates and geographic coordinates – they laid early foundations for empirical observation and reporting. They mark a shift from medieval to Enlightenment.”

He expects the sale will attract collectors of both historic and modern art because some of the scenes, which are painted in watercolour and gouache, are “super surrealist … almost Dalí-esque”.

“If it weren’t for the little captions, which are obviously 16th-century German, some of them look so vividly modern … it’s really jaw-dropping,” Donadoni said.

The scenes depicted include a plague of locusts arriving in Poland on a “terrible wind” in 1527 and the North Sea flooding the coast of Frisia – in what is now the Netherlands and Germany – in 1230.

Other pictures are of fiery comets and eclipses, while scholars think an illustration of “wondrous dragons” flying across the sky over Bavaria in 1533 depicts the northern lights.

Underneath each scene, a German picture caption describes what is happening and where and when the event took place.

“All of these events are portentous,” said Donadoni. “The artists of the Book of Miracles, relying on contemporary broadsheets, chronicles and histories, have condensed into pictorial form the concerns of an age that was gripped by an anxiety – and learned curiosity – about the future.”

The book records how uneasy people felt about climatic anomalies at a time of doctrinal disunity, when there was growing interest in documenting and cataloguing visible, tangible scientific phenomena, he said.

“It’s really fascinating … it is a window into how Europeans were dealing with the impending end of the world, political uncertainty and extreme – and strange – weather events.”

The five suns over Leipzig, for example, is thought to depict the optical illusion of a parhelion, more commonly known as a sun dog. “You see in the sky various different rings of light and it looks like suns,” Donadoni said.

“Blood rain” occurs when high concentrations of red-coloured dust particles, usually from the Sahara, mix with water droplets in rain clouds. “Many of these things have actual rational scientific explanations to them,” Donadoni said.

He said he imagined that leafing through the pages of the book in the 1550s was similar to bingeing on bad news on social media today: “I think it could be a little like the 16th-century version of doomscrolling.”

In this way, the book straddles a momentous turning point in history. “We’re right at that period where the superstition of the middle ages turns into the age of scientific discovery,” Donadoni said.

Till-Holger Borchert, the director of the Suermondt Ludwig Museum in Aachen, has studied the manuscript and said the first time he saw it, his reaction was: “Wow”. “It’s one of a kind,” he said. “It grabs you by your heart or your soul or your belly.”

He said he thought £1m was a “very conservative estimate” of its value. “It [the final price] could possibly be much more,” he said.

A facsimile of the manuscript was published in 2013 that got people “really excited” about it, Borchert added, and since then the Book of Miracles has become “a kind of icon for people who are interested in apocalyptic things”. It would be wrong to value it merely as an historical document: “It’s more than that. It speaks to us, to our generation.”

Can the tourist destination become the first urban island to rid itself of the stoat — an introduced predator that has ravaged New Zealand’s endemic wildlife?

Frank Lepera, stoat operations manager at Te Korowai o Waiheke, installing a trap where a stoat was recently reported, on Waiheke Island, New Zealand.Credit...

Reporting from Waiheke Island, New Zealand. She did not see a live stoat on the island.

To catch a stoat, you need to think like one. For Tilde Sorensen, that means imagining having tiny legs and picturing which route the weasel-like creature might take as it haunts the wilderness of New Zealand, preying on unique birdlife.

“We’ve caught all of the ones that will happily go into a trap. So now we’ve got to figure out how to get the really shy ones,” Ms. Sorensen said of the stoat population on Waiheke Island, a 40-minute boat ride from New Zealand’s largest city, Auckland.

The campaign to eradicate stoats, and many other introduced species, has widespread support in New Zealand, where introduced predators, including rats and possums, kill about 25 million native birds each year.

Waiheke Island, which covers about 36 square miles, is home to 4,300 people, a number that swells to about 45,000 in the summer.

#70. Russia's mega-rich are already imagining a future after Putin

Lawyers for former President Donald Trump told a New York judge on Monday that they oppose the request by media outlets to broadcast Trump’s arraignment on Tuesday.

“We submit that the media request should be denied because it will create a circus-like atmosphere at the arraignment, raise unique security concerns, and is inconsistent with President Trump’s presumption of innocence,” the Trump team said in a letter to New York Supreme Court Judge Juan Merchant.

Trump’s attorneys said in their letter to Acting New York Supreme Court Judge Juan Merchan that he is required under court rules to take into account potential security concerns.

“As Your Honor is well-aware, this case presents extraordinary security concerns (including Secret Service-related concerns) and we submit that any video or photography of the proceedings will only heighten these serious concerns,” they wrote.

Among the Trump lawyers signing the new letter was Todd Blanche, who, CNN reported, recently joined the former president’s legal team.

Meanwhile, the Manhattan District Attorney’s office also responded Monday to the news outlets’ request, and said that it was deferring to the judge to decide how best to manage his court room.

However, the district attorney’s office wrote that it would “be a defensible exercise of the Court’s discretion to exclude or restrict videography, photography, and radio coverage of the arraignment in the interest of avoiding potential prejudice to the defendant, maintaining an orderly proceeding, assuring the safety of the participants in the proceeding, or for other reasons within the Court’s broad authority to manage and control these proceedings.”

But the prosecutors also noted that there “does not appear to be a categorical prohibition on cameras during an arraignment” under existing New York statutes and case law.

While the district attorney’s office stopped short of opposing the request, its lawyer pointed out to Merchan that a similar request for audio-video broadcasting was made for the 2021 arraignment in the tax fraud case against Trump Organization and its CFO Allen Weisselberg.

The judge responded to that 2021 request by “allowing a limited number of still photographs to be taken prior to the commencement of proceedings,” the prosecutors noted Monday.

CNN is one of the outlets requesting camera access for Tuesday’s arraignment.
