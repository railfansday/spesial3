#45. Weapons, war and wavering trust: Germans want to see more restraint in foreign policy

According to a study by the Körber Foundation, a growing number of Germans now oppose providing military aid to Ukraine. The majority also believes that Germany should take a neutral position between the US and China.

For decades, German foreign policy was never seen as particularly controversial at home. The country was firmly integrated into NATO and supported EU expansion and cooperation across Europe. It also advocated for multilateral approaches to resolving conflicts around the world. All of this enjoyed broad public support. But all of this has also changed since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine and the wars in the Middle East. Now, Germany's foreign policy has become increasingly contentious and is fueling growing public anxiety.

Long-held assumptions are beginning to crumble, including the belief that the US would ultimately remain firmly on Germany's side. This is one of the central findings of a new survey by the Hamburg-based Körber Foundation.

In the foundation's latest poll on public attitudes on German foreign policy, 65% of respondents said they fear further military conflicts around the world. Meanwhile, 48% believe that Germany should adopt a more restrained approach to international affairs — a five percentage point increase from last year.

Since 2017, the non-profit Körber Foundation, established in the northern German city of Hamburg in 1959 by industrialist Kurt Körber, has conducted the annual surveys titled "the Berlin Pulse." For this year's study, the polling institute Forsa surveyed around 1,500 eligible voters in September.

The findings make one thing clear: The current government's approach under Chancellor Friedrich Merz — seeking to have a say in virtually every international conflict and drawing on Germany's position as the largest EU economy — is increasingly met with skepticism. Only 40% of respondents now favor greater international engagement, down from 48% in 2025.

Florian Bigge, who helped compile the study for the Körber Foundation, commented on the findings on Monday: "This is a significant decline, especially since we have been asking this question for years, with changes in the range of just two to three percentage points."

Meanwhile, 77% of respondents want Germany to focus primarily on diplomacy rather than military action.

The most dramatic shift in public opinion concerns the war in Ukraine. Germany's military support for the Eastern European country, including the delivery of billions of euros' worth of weapons, no longer has the backing of a majority of the population. In September 2026, only 46% of respondents supported continued military assistance, compared to 59% in the previous year's survey. Currently, a narrow majority of 53% want to halt weapons deliveries altogether.

As Jonathan Lehrer of "the Berlin Pulse" explains, opposition is particularly strong among supporters of the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD).

"Eighty-eight precent of AfD supporters oppose military assistance to Ukraine," Lehrer said. "This is an interesting finding, especially considering political developments in Germany, including state elections in which the AfD gained support by campaigning against aid to Ukraine."

The AfD has recently overtaken the chancellor's conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) in nationwide polls.

Support for Ukraine was also the subject of a recent poll conducted by the opinion research institute Infratest Dimap. This study also found that public support for Ukraine is continuing to decline, with a majority now opposed to providing assistance (see graphic).

One finding from the Berlin Pulse study is particularly alarming for politicians and experts committed to transatlantic relations. As the US and China are increasingly seen as rivals on the global stage, 83% of respondents believe that Germany should remain neutral. Only 15% would side with the US.

This may stem from the policies of US President Donald Trump. Florian Bigge of the Körber Foundation said of the widespread preference for neutrality: "This sentiment is strong among Germans across all age groups and political parties. In eastern Germany, support for neutrality is at 92%, compared to 81% in the west. We interpret this as a desire for security but also reluctant commitment: Germans do not want to take sides in this conflict between the great powers."

The picture becomes clearer when respondents are asked about Russia. Under President Vladimir Putin, 82% of those surveyed see Russia as a military threat. However, public opinion is more divided when Germany responds firmly to such threats, including hybrid attacks. For instance, after the attempted drone attack on Leipzig Airport in early May, Germany responded by closing the Russian consulate in Bonn. However, only 51% support such measures, while 46% favor a more restrained approach.

"The gap between external expectations and domestic support is the central challenge facing German foreign policy," said Sarah Pagung of the Körber Foundation.

#28. What happens if a nuclear weapon explodes in space?

Earth's atmosphere offers protection from a nuclear blast in space, but satellites are more exposed. We explain how radiation could damage them, why the effects can linger and what protection is possible.

A nuclear explosion in space would be unlikely to cause widespread injuries on Earth. But it could disable satellites that provide services we depend on every day, including some that save lives.

In the United States, satellite failures could make it harder for emergency crews to communicate in remote areas. If GPS were also disrupted, drivers and pilots could lose navigation guidance, and financial networks could lose a source of precise time.

We have some idea of what would happen because it's already been done.

On the evening of July 8, 1962, people in Hawaii held "watch-the-bomb parties" and followed the countdown on shortwave radio. Then the night sky lit up. The United States had detonated a nuclear weapon about 400 kilometers (250 miles) above the Pacific. The test was called Starfish Prime.

An electromagnetic pulse knocked out some streetlights in Hawaii, and some Pacific radio links briefly went down. The glow faded within minutes, but some satellites failed months later.

The air was too thin for a conventional fireball to form or for a destructive shock wave to reach the ground. Charged particles made denser air below glow, creating an artificial aurora. The atmosphere also absorbed much of the initial radiation.

Satellites are especially vulnerable because they orbit above most of the atmosphere that shields people on the ground from the initial radiation.

In orbit, the initial burst of X-rays can damage spacecraft right away. But Earth's magnetic field can also trap electrons released after the blast, as it did after Starfish Prime. Satellites receive another dose each time they cross the resulting radiation belt. Over time, that exposure can damage electronics and solar panels.

That would depend on where the explosion happened and which satellites crossed the radiation belt. GPS satellites send signals that help phones, planes and ships work out where they are. They orbit about 20,200 kilometers above Earth and could keep working even if satellites closer to Earth failed.

But satellites do different jobs. A ship's emergency beacon can use GPS to work out its location, then send it to rescuers through a separate satellite network. If part of that network failed, the alert could take longer to get through.

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty bars its parties from placing nuclear weapons in orbit. The 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty prohibits its parties from carrying out nuclear explosions in space.

The Outer Space Treaty has no dedicated system for checking what spacecraft carry. A detonation is easier to detect because US satellite sensors are designed to pick up the signals it gives off.

Shielding could help protect satellite electronics, and backups could keep some services running. During a GPS outage, pilots over the contiguous US can use ground-based navigation aids. Operators could launch replacements, but those satellites could still face lingering radiation or debris.

Radiation could leave many satellites intact but unable to steer. If two objects collided, fragments from the crash could hit other satellites, creating more debris and triggering further collisions. If enough debris built up for the chain reaction to keep going on its own, it could continue even if no new satellites were launched. This is known as Kessler syndrome.

Whether an explosion would trigger such a cascade is uncertain. If it did, the debris could endanger replacement satellites for years.

Some radioactive particles could enter the atmosphere and eventually settle on the ground. But a blast in space is too far away to sweep up the soil and water that carry fallout from a surface explosion.

As the particles disperse, their radioactivity decreases over time. The concentrated local fallout that can expose people near a ground-level blast to high doses would be unlikely.

Asked about the reports, Rutte said: "We will always make sure we defend every inch of Nato territory, and that includes making sure we are safe when it comes to the space element."

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he hadn't seen the latest reports but dismissed reports citing "unknown sources" as "hardly worth taking seriously".

The Russian embassy in Berlin told German media it rejected what it called "unfounded allegations" and said the reports were part of a "propaganda campaign".

Rutte told the press conference in Berlin that Nato had a variety of response options.

"When it comes to the military domain, we will always make assessments of our deterrence and defence and make updates where necessary," he said.

Citing anonymous German military and intelligence sources, the reports by broadcasters NDR and WDR, and newspaper Sueddeutsche Zeitung, said the Russian satellite was dubbed "Sputnik-S" and was equipped with a nuclear warhead. Standing alongside Rutte, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz did not confirm or deny the reports of Russia's plans, but said they "reveal the level of escalation Russia is evidently prepared to pursue".

Russia is a signatory to the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which bans weapons of mass destruction from orbit. In 2024 the US claimed Russia was developing a "space-based" anti-satellite weapon armed with a nuclear warhead. Russia denied the accusation.

"Our position is clear and transparent. We have always been categorically against the deployment of nuclear weapons in space and we are still against it," President Vladimir Putin said at the time.

#13. What China's Xi could take away from Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia

A comeback for the ages: Trump's influence on Huang's return to Nvidia

Jensen Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia CEO has riveted the world - including China, where it has become the top trending post on Weibo, the country's biggest social media platform.

But to many, Huang's return, which is being dubbed one of the greatest U-turns of all time, seems like a vindication for China.

That's probably how Xi Jinping sees it too.

Beijing has dismissed claims that one of the women who had an affair with Jensen Huang had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, its version of the CIA, as a spy. Chinese state media has also framed the spy saga as a smear campaign orchestrated by China hawks in Washington. The Global Times, a Communist Party mouthpiece, described it as a "witch hunt".

Now the White House, and President Donald Trump himself, have said Huang poses no national security threat, although investigations into the spy allegations are still ongoing.

Trunmp and Xi ahead of their meeting in Shenzhen last week

Xi will have learned many lessons from this scandal, especially about Trump's pressure points.

For one, the fact that Huang's reappointment came so soon after Xi's meetings last week with Trump in Shenzhen could be read as a sign that direct engagement is an effective way to influence Washington.

Trump has said the two men discussed Huang, but did not elaborate further. Beijing has not commented.

The episode has also reinforced the view among Chinese officials that there are two camps in Washington: those in favour of restricting Chinese access to cutting edge tech to protect US national security, and those, like Huang, who believe that such restrictions will not limit Beijing's ambitions.

Huang has repeatedly argued that US export controls will force Chinese firms like Huawei to build independent alternatives - all the while shutting American companies out of the world's second-largest economy.

But him being back at the helm of Nvidia is no guarantee of relief for Beijing.

Rather, the sale of advanced chips will remain a key point of contention between the US and China, especially as the two sides try to reach a trade deal before another Trump-Xi meeting, which is expected in December.

Already new rules are being put in place that will worry Beijing.

The US Bureau of Industry and Security is tightening restrictions by targeting not only the direct export of advanced chips to China, but also Chinese firms' ability to use computing power in data centres overseas.

The measures are designed to close potential loopholes that allow Chinese companies to access advanced Nvidia and AMD chips through cloud providers operating in third countries, including Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand.

China has yet to react with measures of its own. But investors will be watching closely for any escalation which could weigh on US equity markets.

Jensen Huang at an Nvidia event in Berlin in October

China has already used its considerable chokehold over the world's rare earth metals to bring the US to the negotiating table over trade.

If Washington restricts chip exports and Beijing retaliates, that could be the start of a cycle that has a profound effect on the stock market because American companies, from car makers to defence firms to chip manufacturers, all rely on rare earths.

What China has realised is that more trouble lies ahead as it tries to build what it describes as a "constructive strategic relationship" with the US.

Trump's tariffs are no longer at the heart of that tussle. Now it's about control of critical technology and minerals which could, in turn, affect each side's military influence and economic power.

Among the Trump lawyers signing the new letter was Todd Blanche, who, CNN reported, recently joined the former president’s legal team.

Meanwhile, the Manhattan District Attorney’s office also responded Monday to the news outlets’ request, and said that it was deferring to the judge to decide how best to manage his court room.

However, the district attorney’s office wrote that it would “be a defensible exercise of the Court’s discretion to exclude or restrict videography, photography, and radio coverage of the arraignment in the interest of avoiding potential prejudice to the defendant, maintaining an orderly proceeding, assuring the safety of the participants in the proceeding, or for other reasons within the Court’s broad authority to manage and control these proceedings.”

But the prosecutors also noted that there “does not appear to be a categorical prohibition on cameras during an arraignment” under existing New York statutes and case law.

While the district attorney’s office stopped short of opposing the request, its lawyer pointed out to Merchan that a similar request for audio-video broadcasting was made for the 2021 arraignment in the tax fraud case against Trump Organization and its CFO Allen Weisselberg.

The judge responded to that 2021 request by “allowing a limited number of still photographs to be taken prior to the commencement of proceedings,” the prosecutors noted Monday.
