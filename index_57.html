#111. Cop31 climate talks break down as key deal rejected

The EU, the UK and allies in the developing world rejected what they called an unacceptable deal on the future of the climate, which failed even to mention fossil fuels, in a dramatic finish to the Cop31 UN talks in Turkey.

The proposed deal “failed to meet the moment”, said Ireland’s environment minister, Darragh O’Brien, speaking for the EU. “We will not endorse a decision that does not respond to the science and that actually risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required. [It] does not even mention fossil fuels.”

As the talks, scheduled to end on Friday, dragged on into Sunday afternoon in the longest climate summit ever, it was clear that the “Antalya pact”, drafted by the Turkish and Australian joint presidencies of the summit, would not achieve the consensus needed to pass.

The Antalya pact invites countries to electrify their economies, calls for rich countries to provide finance to the poor to help them adapt to the impacts of the climate crisis, and calls on countries to minimise the “overshoot” of the 1.5C global heating threshold, of the Paris agreement.

But it failed to endorse the “transition away from fossil fuels” that more than 130 countries demanded, and included controversial provisions on “carbon capture and storage” (CCS) technology that some have said perpetuates fossil fuels.

The presidencies avoided outright collapse of the “conference of the parties” by passing decisions on various related matters, including the “just transition” to help workers in high-carbon industries shift to a low-carbon economy.

But Murat Kurum, Turkey’s Cop president, acknowledged there was no agreement on the draft “cover decision” text and so it had not been adopted.

“We have done our utmost; ultimately, we have not imposed this text on anyone, nor have we issued an ultimatum ⁠such as a ‘take it or leave it’ demand,” he said. “It is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognise ⁠that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted.”

Many countries, including the EU, UK and developing countries including small island states and some of the poorest countries, said they could not support the Antalya pact deal, which was supposed to be main outcome of the Cop.

In a dramatic intervention, O’Brien said on behalf of the EU that it could not accept the final text in its current form. “It fails to meet the moment … We will not endorse a cover decision or an Antalya pact that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change.” He said the EU would not accept a decision “that greenwashes fossil fuels”.

His intervention was met by applause from the hall, and was later followed by similar objections from Panama, the Marshall Islands and Norway.

“This feels like a real line in the sand moment. Countries are standing up to fossil fuel interests and saying no,” said Bill Hare, the chief executive of the advisory group Climate Analytics.

But Mohamed Adow, the director of the thinktank Power Shift Africa, who called it “the worst Cop ever”, said: “The EU has rejected this text, but it cannot wash its hands of the wreckage it helped create ... For countries facing failed harvests, destroyed homes and mounting debt, this amounts to abandonment by the wealthy nations most responsible for the crisis.

“Europe’s hypocrisy is staggering. Its governments demand fossil fuel roadmaps from others while expanding fossil fuels themselves.”

Saudi Arabia defended the decision to include CCS in the text. Other countries, including the Africa group, China and Pakistan, also did not object to the deal, despite some having reservations. Pakistan told the conference that just because a few items in the pact were objectionable to some, the rest should not be sacrificed: “Climate action is not a menu from which we can select only what suits us.”

The Trump administration responded to requests for comment by denying it had force-fed Cora Pérez. But the detainee sent more details – and his is far from an isolated case.

Cora Pérez was one of two men on hunger striker at a Texas facility for whom ICE sought and received a federal judge’s permission to force-feed in recent weeks, a Guardian investigation found.

Cora Pérez and the other man were locked up inside the Port Isabel service processing center, an ICE detention facility in south Texas, just a few miles from the US-Mexico border, on the coast near where Elon Musk’s SpaceX launches rockets. Cora Pérez began a hunger strike in August to protest against his prolonged detention by federal immigration officials while his case plays out. At first he only drank water, then after three weeks, he says he began to drink one Ensure nutritional shake per day.

On 30 October, court records show ICE received permission to forcibly feed and hydrate him, and to restrain him while doing so.

The next day he says six guards at the center held him down, while medical staffers inserted a feeding tube through his nose and down his throat, and pumped nutritional liquid into his stomach – a process he called “traumatizing, both physically and psychologically”.

On 3 November, ICE then received permission from a federal judge to force-feed another hunger striker at Port Isabel, from Uzbekistan, federal court records show. The DHS says the Uzbek man has not been subjected to the practice, however.

On Wednesday night, after weeks of communications, Cora Pérez was suddenly transferred to another detention center in Texas. His relative says neither of them were alerted to any plans to transfer him, or a reason for it.

The DHS did not specify why Cora Pérez was transferred, adding that ICE makes custody determinations on a “case-by-case” basis based on “various factors”.

The revelations come as ICE’s detention practices continue to come under fire amid allegations of mistreatment, and as an increasing number of sporadic hunger strikes have been breaking out in ICE facilities across the country.

The two men’s cases in Texas, revealed here for the first time, are some of the most recent instances of government lawyers seeking a federal judge’s permission, as required by ICE policy, and receiving the go-ahead to force-feed detained hunger strikers. The Guardian’s reporting is based on court records, interviews, dozens of messages exchanged and an interview conducted with Cora Pérez via a facility phone. It further reveals how the Trump administration is increasingly obtaining such court orders to force-feed detained immigrants, a secretive practice widely condemned as torture by human rights organizations and medical ethics groups.

Detainees driven to hunger strike are typically using it as a last resort to protest against poor conditions and the Trump administration’s preference for arrest and prolonged detention while their civil immigration cases proceed through the system.

Cora Pérez said detention center staff inserted the feeding tube and left it in his body, a not uncommon practice, according to experts, and introduced nutrition – first a “clear liquid”, followed by a “milky liquid”, he recalled – through the tube twice more that day, before he cracked and said he broke his hunger strike, citing what he called “horrific torture”. He began voluntarily drinking nutritional shakes the following day, he said.

“ICE does not hold back on anything, and they do anything they want, like we have seen, even killing their own US citizens,” he wrote to the Guardian, referring to the fatal shootings of Renee Good by an ICE officer and Alex Pretti by a border patrol officer in January of this year during Trump’s pivotal immigration crackdown on Minneapolis.

Detained hunger strikers subjected to involuntary medical procedures, including force-feeding, have rarely spoken out about their cases or revealed their identities, much less while still detained. Cora Pérez’s messages offer a rare glimpse at conditions inside a detention center’s restricted medical unit where hunger strikers and suicidal detainees are held.

“They just took [the Uzbek man] in an ambulance after [him] fainting in front of the medical lobby where they take our vitals, our weight and our temperature,” Cora Pérez said in an alarmed message last Monday morning.

The Uzbek man was returned the following day to the facility, Cora Pérez said.

“I see him every day because I see him in the room in front of me, but they don’t let us talk to each other,” he added.

The Guardian independently confirmed the Uzbek’s man identity but is not publicly naming him, because efforts to contact him have been unsuccessful. The man arrived at the Mexico-US border in April 2022 and was released into the country while navigating his immigration case. He was arrested by border patrol officials in April of this year and placed in detention. The man does not appear to have any criminal record and the DHS did not point to any criminal history.

In a statement, DHS said that after they received the court’s permission to force-feed the Uzbek man, he “has voluntarily taken in fluids and there has been NO force-feeding or forced hydration”.

Security operations at the sprawling Port Isabel facility in Los Fresnos, Texas, are run by a private prison company, Akima Global Services, while healthcare is primarily handled by government workers.

“ICE respects detainees’ rights to exercise self-expression and medical autonomy by refusing nourishment and medical care,” the DHS said in a statement. “ICE Health Services Corps may provide involuntary medical treatment only to prevent imminent life-threatening harm or death. Medical staff may provide treatment only to medically stabilize the patient, bringing the detainee outside the imminent harm, incapacitation, or death.”

The US attorney’s office in the southern district of Texas, which represented ICE in its force-feeding request, did not respond to multiple requests for comment. Virginia-headquartered Akima Global Services, and its parent company Nana Regional Corporation, based in Alaska, did not respond to a request for comment.

Force-feeding cases are shrouded in secrecy. After a detainee launches a hunger strike, ICE often sequesters them in medical isolation cells for monitoring, according to ICE’s detention standards. The DHS said that ICE continues to offer hunger strikers three meals per day.

After a certain period of not eating, which varies per case, ICE will request a federal judge’s permission to force-feed the hunger striker. Many court records, and sometimes entire court dockets, are sealed and hidden from public view. In Cora Pérez and the Uzbek man’s cases, like in others, they did not have any legal representation to challenge the force-feeding requests.

The Guardian found at least five instances in which ICE requested permission to force-feed hunger strikers in Port Isabel since January 2025. The federal judge who ruled on Cora Perez and the Uzbek man, Rolando Olvera, has similarly ordered in at least three other cases, records show. Olvera’s judicial assistant said the court had no comment for this article.

ICE is known to have requested permission to force-feed at least 21 detainees across the US since January 2025, the Guardian found, but the number is probably higher since an unknown number of records and dockets remain sealed, unsearchable and not feasible to locate. Most of the cases coinciding with the second Trump administration and tracked by the Guardian are in Texas and Florida, with others in Georgia, Arizona and Washington state. Under the Biden administration, ICE force-feeding requests were also made in Louisiana and California.

Amy Zeidan, an emergency medicine physician based in Atlanta and an expert on healthcare in immigration detention, said force-feeding hunger strikers can amount to “torture”, and lead to significant psychological distress. She also said it was a violation of medical ethics.

“Essentially it’s like inserting a straw into your nose,” Zeidan said. “You have a gag reflex, so most times people will gag, and that’s really uncomfortable. And people will even vomit repeatedly if it continues to irritate their gag reflex.”

Zeidan added that the forceful insertion of a feeding tube can lead to the swelling of the nostrils and the throat, and even tear them, leading to bleeding.

“It is like torture – somebody is doing a painful procedure to you that you don’t consent to,” Zeidan added. “Autonomy is a fundamental principle of medical ethics. We can’t do something to patients if they don’t want to, even if we think it’s good for them.”

There are numerous cells and rooms inside the Port Isabel medical unit, including isolation cells and shared rooms for ill detainees, according to Cora Pérez. In recent weeks, there were five people in the Port Isabel medical isolation cells used for hunger striking or suicidal detainees, he added. He described the cells as “practically freezing”, constantly surveilled with a security camera, and with just a bed, toilet and sink. As well as himself, he says he observed the Uzbek man, a Middle Eastern man, a “tall” eastern European man and a Black man of unknown nationality in the isolation cells. Unit detainees are escorted by facility staff to showers and sometimes receive yard and television access, he said.

Cora Pérez worries for the other detainees. The Black man, whose identity or nationality he does not know, seems to have “serious mental health problems”, Cora Pérez observed.

“He harms himself and spends the night yelling and hitting the door, and does not let anyone sleep,” he said.

The Guardian has independently confirmed the eastern European and Middle Eastern men’s identities, but is not publicly naming them because efforts to contact them have been unsuccessful. The DHS did not comment on the other men inside the medical isolation unit, saying they could not comment without the Guardian providing their identities.

Cora Pérez had left his son and parents in Cuba and requested asylum at the Mexico-US border in 2023, according to a relative who spoke to the Guardian on condition of anonymity due to fear of retaliation by immigration authorities. Cora Pérez went to live with the relative in Florida, where he worked in maintenance at a hotel and as a food delivery worker, he said.

In March 2025, Cora Pérez was arrested by local police and later convicted of two misdemeanors, including “breach of peace” and “obstructing an officer without violence”, according to local records. He was sentenced to two months in jail, his relative said. But ICE placed a hold on him and as he was being released in June 2025, immigration officials took him into custody – a common system – and soon transferred him to the Adelanto detention center, a privately-run facility in California.

Days in Adelanto turned to weeks, then months, then over a year, according to a federal court order denying one of his petitions requesting his release. His multiple requests to immigration and ordinary federal courts for release from detention, while he fights his asylum case, have been unsuccessful.

“All his claims will be heard by an immigration judge, and he will receive due process. This administration will not ignore the rule of law,” DHS said. “Criminal illegal aliens are not welcome in the US.”

In August, he stopped eating to protest against his protracted detention.

“I have spent two birthdays behind ICE’s bars,” he wrote. “The reasons I did the hunger strike in Adelanto are for my political asylum, for the time I have spent detained, [and] because I have a 14-year-old son who is starving to death in Cuba because he does not have my help.

“Also due to the mistreatment by the officials,” he added.

Cora Pérez did not tell his family about the hunger strike, so as not to further worry them. It was not until he was transferred to Port Isabel in late September that he confessed to his relative he had been on a prolonged hunger strike.

“He doesn’t share details so that I won’t get upset or worry,” the relative said in a phone interview.

Cora Pérez’s mother, who is in Cuba, “does not know anything, because she is sick, and with his detention she has gotten worse”, the relative added, between tears. “I don’t want her nor his grandmother to know anything about it,” the relative added.

On 30 October, the federal prosecutors requested permission for ICE to forcibly feed and hydrate Cora Pérez. Hours later, a federal judge approved the procedures without hearing from Cora Pérez. Cora Pérez told the Guardian in various instances he felt it was unfair he was not given the opportunity to present his case to the judge before the judge approved his force-feeding.

“It’s very disturbing because the judge is basically making a decision on information that is only provided by one side,” said Linus Chan, a professor of clinical law and the director of the detainee rights clinic at the University of Minnesota law school. “That just seems ridiculous.”

Cora Pérez was force-fed the following day, he said in his messages and in a phone call, adding that he did not physically resist because he “had seen a lot of mistreatment of others detained”.

“[ICE does] what they want with us, whatever they want, because a federal judge gave them permission,” he added. “I have suffered on the island [of Cuba] and now at the hands of ICE.”

After asking Cora Pérez about the DHS’s denials that he was force-fed, he repeated that he was. “Brother, ICE says and does whatever they want. They deny, punish, retaliate and do everything at their own will. They have even sent Cubans to Africa,” he said.

The number of people detained by ICE has soared under the Trump administration. And due to policy and legal changes, tens of thousands of people are forced to stay in detention for long periods while they fight their immigration cases.

Chan, from the University of Minnesota, says prolonged detention and delays in their cases lead to logical conclusion. “That’s exactly when people protest,” he said.

“And the only form of protest, for many of these people, is a hunger strike,” he said.

As Trump’s hardline immigration crackdown continues and people are detained as a first resort while their cases play out, Chan said: “Hunger strikes will become more common.”

#30. Haitians excited but uneasy as El Salvador troops fight gangs

Foreign combatants in Port-au-Prince welcomed by many after years of gang control but some fear offensive could exacerbate volatile situation

A bullet-peppered mural of the Haitian striker Emmanuel Sanon covers the facade of Port-au-Prince’s main football ground. But more than two years have passed since any sports stars graced the pitch.

In 2024, as the surrounding city was almost entirely captured by heavily armed gangs, Sylvio Cator stadium – where football greats including Brazil’s Ronaldo and Roberto Carlos once played – also fell under criminal control, its dilapidated red and blue stands used as an outpost by fighters working for a gang boss called Izo. Haiti’s national team, the Grenadiers, found itself homeless and was forced to play its World Cup qualifying games abroad.

That changed last week, as Haitian police and troops from El Salvador reconquered the stadium. Nayib Bukele, El Salvador’s authoritarian president, called the development a step towards delivering Haiti from a criminal insurrection that has displaced more than 1.5 million people and killed thousands.

“We now have the upper hand on the ground,” claimed Bukele, who has sent more than 2,000 soldiers to Haiti since mid-October.

Outside the Sylvio Cator stadium, named after an acclaimed Haitian athlete who won a silver medal at the 1928 Olympics, there were joyous scenes as citizens celebrated a foreign politician they hope will prove an unlikely saviour.

“There’s a wind of hope blowing through our country and, thanks to God, we will get through this. Long live Bukele!” said a 55-year-old decorator who asked to be named as Bòs Baton.

Stood by Sanon’s battle-scarred fresco, Baton recalled being driven from an area called Poste-Marchand after criminals set his home ablaze during an armed uprising that started in February 2024. “Today, we are happy because there are places we couldn’t go to before that are accessible – and it’s thanks to Bukele’s intervention that we can be here today,” Baton said.

Identical sentiments have been voiced on the streets of El Salvador’s capital, San Salvador, in recent years as a result of Bukele’s controversial anti-gang crusade, which has freed millions from gang rule – albeit at a high price.

Since that crackdown began in early 2022, more than 90,000 Salvadorans have been jailed as part of a mass incarceration campaign that has slashed murder rates but also fuelled fears that El Salvador’s democracy has died.

Bukele’s domestic clampdown has made the millennial strongman a darling for rightwing politicians and voters across Latin America and the Caribbean, who hold his actions up as proof that, with an iron fist and a malleable approach to human rights, rampant criminality can be tamed.

Now, as Bukele seeks to extend his seven-year presidency in an election in February, he is hoping to burnish his gang-busting credentials overseas.

“It’s an operation that projects force, that projects Bukele’s image as the saviour … and positions El Salvador as a key reference in the region for [how] … to deal with gangs and security,” said Tiziano Breda, a Latin America regional expert from the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project.

The extent of Bukele’s ambitions in Haiti is unclear. The operation’s trigger was the 18 October abduction of four Salvadoran aviation technicians who worked for a UN-backed security force. On Monday, Bukele’s vice-president, Félix Ulloa, announced Salvadoran troops would withdraw as soon as the four men were rescued, “because this is not an occupying force – we are not there to stay”.

But Ulloa also said his government was responding altruistically “to a cry for help from the Haitian people and government”, suggesting its mission could be extended.

Bukele has also hinted his plans go beyond short-lived ground operations to recover his citizens, or their remains. “We will triumph. God will give us victory,” the politician vowed this week.

Many Haitians hope his combatants will stay and have been thrilled by cinematic social media clips posted by Bukele showing helicopter strikes on a gang compound and the mansion of a gangster nicknamed Taliban.

Banners and billboards around Port-au-Prince celebrate Bukele as a 21st-century liberator and feature slogans such as “The bandits won’t get a wink of sleep!” and “We’re tired! Give us Bukele!”

Standing outside the liberated football ground, bricklayer Venel Saindor recalled how criminals torched his home in Carrefour-Feuilles three years ago. “It’s impossible to enter my neighbourhood because the thieves are still there. They shoot at us,” the 56-year-old explained, lifting his shirt to reveal a bullet scar above his right nipple.

“We need Bukele to help us return to our homes,” added Saindor, as a Salvadoran soldier with a belt-fed heavy machine gun kept watch from the stadium’s terraces.

Monique Clesca, a writer and activist, echoed the bricklayer’s enthusiasm. “Haitians are elated … [and] I feel elated also … It is a moment of hope,” she said on Tuesday, as thousands of demonstrators poured on to the streets to champion Bukele, chanting: “Viv Bukele!” and “Bukele is not playing games!”

As a sea of protesters swept towards the prime minister’s offices, people from all walks of life spoke of how armed groups had destroyed their lives and their collective hope that Bukele may provide a solution.

“I used to drive an old bus. The bandits burned it. They burned my house down too,” said Wilné Persona, a 50-year-old driver, adding: “Bukele is the one we are waiting for … because we can no longer bear the situation.”

A food vendor called Claudy Mayas urged “Papa Bukele” to stand his ground. “Bukele, I bless you, your wife and your children,” said Mayas, 51. “The Haitian people are with you, just as you are with us.”

Clesca attributed the palpable excitement to the widespread perception that Bukele’s “decisive” action had exposed the incompetence, inefficiency and indifference of Haitian and foreign leaders. “Basically, what Bukele has done is show that leadership is about resolving a problem for your population,” she said, praising how the stadium and other areas were recaptured in less than a month.

Amid the excitement there is also unease. Some fear Bukele’s offensive could exacerbate an already volatile situation in and around Haiti’s capital, much of which resembles a war zone. This week, there was heavy fighting between Salvadoran troops, police and gangs in Village of God, the shantytown that is Izo’s main base. On Thursday, police said officers had killed at least 34 suspected criminals during an operation near the stadium.

Some suspect El Salvador’s president, a social media whiz with 22 million followers on X and TikTok, could be exaggerating his troops’ role in recent successes with help from a strike team of online propagandists and fans. “Nayib Bukele is gang kryptonite,” one US enthusiast gushed this week, calling his intervention “a miracle”.

Others fret over Bukele’s ability to distinguish between gang members and civilians, given the indiscriminate nature of his mass detention campaign in El Salvador, where thousands of innocent people have been jailed without due process.

Clesca downplayed concerns over the toll Bukele’s crackdown has taken on democracy and the rule of law in El Salvador, which now has the world’s highest per capita incarceration rate. Haitian politics was so contaminated with organised crime, she argued, “it’s almost like the mafia has taken over 10 Downing Street”.

“If you’re talking about [an] assault on democracy, Haiti is … almost a case study that is … even worse, more flagrant, than El Salvador,” Clesca said.

The security situation was so dire that urgent action was needed. “Let’s just be honest … in Haiti, we’re being brutalised,” Clesca said. “Girls are being taken as sexual slaves. They’re being gang raped. So somewhere along the line, we have to say: ‘Haiti helped [South American independence hero] Simón Bolívar [who received shelter and support from Haiti in the early 1800s] … and now, Bukele is helping us’.”

At Tuesday’s rally, protesters spoke of El Salvador’s president in divine terms. “Today, we see Bukele as a saviour, as someone sent by God,” said one man, before adding a word of caution that spoke to countless past interventions that have ended in frustration and failure.

“There is one thing I will say to President Bukele,” he said. “He must not start with us and leave us halfway.”

#21. Bank of England directors illegally profited from post-abolition enslavement in Cuba

New research finds that the British-led Cobre Mining Company helped industrialise Manchester via copper deposits and enslaved African people

Slave-trade wealth was embedded in Britain’s financial system, research finds

British businesses run by long-serving directors of the Bank of England profited from “brutal” enslavement in Cuba for decades after slavery’s abolition in the British empire, according to research.

Men and boys were enslaved in prison conditions at the El Cobre copper ore mine near Santiago de Cuba, which supplied British firms during the Industrial Revolution, the historian Chris Evans has found.

In the 1840s, Cuba was the most important foreign source of copper ore for the UK. Copper was critical to Manchester’s cotton industry, railways and telegraphy.

Previous research has revealed that dozens of financiers involved in the founding in 1694 of the Bank of England invested in enslavement.

Now, Evans’s research reveals that ties between key figures in the UK’s financial system and enslavement of African peoples continued even after the government outlawed it.

Britain abolished its trade in enslaved people in 1807 and passed legislation abolishing slavery in most of its empire in 1833.

However, as Britain’s Slavery Abolition Act was coming into force, “City of London figures” were developing mining of El Cobre’s copper ore deposits, an operation which depended on enslavement in Spanish-controlled Cuba.

Key players in the highly lucrative operation included the Bank of England directors Charles Pascoe Grenfell (1790-1867) and William Thompson (1792-1854), acting in a private capacity.

Enslaved copper miners at El Cobre were likely to have been trafficked to Cuba by illegal voyages from the Bight of Biafra, in modern-day Nigeria, as Evans details in his book, Slavery’s Long Goodbye – Capitalism, Nationalism and Christianity in the Age of British Emancipation.

The death rate at El Cobre means it is likely that thousands of Africans were exploited there. Their life stories remain largely undocumented. At the time, slavery was legal in Cuba but the importation of enslaved people was not.

As a partner in the Cobre mining and export company, Grenfell was a driving force in enslaved miners’ exploitation.

Cobre was “likely to be the largest slave enterprise in the western hemisphere” by the 1840s, Evans said. Investors who held Cobre shares from the outset received £102 in dividends for every £40 share between 1836 and 1865.

Grenfell was also a partner in a Swansea-based smelter Pascoe, Grenfell & Sons, which bought imports from the mine, in an era when the Welsh port was the centre of the global copper trade.

Meanwhile, Thompson, a former lord mayor of London, exploited enslaved miners through the ore export firm he directed, the Royal Santiago Company.

The UK is facing increased pressure to make a formal apology to Caribbean and African nations for Britain’s centuries-long perpetuation of transatlantic chattel slavery. Commonwealth leaders say “structured dialogue” with the UK prime minister, Andy Burnham, will begin in the next year.

While the Tory leader Kemi Badenoch has claimed Britain has nothing to apologise for, scholarship – dating back to the Trinidadian statesman Eric Williams’s 1944 book, Capitalism and Slavery – reveals the global impact of the colonialism and chattel enslavement that was integral to British capitalism’s success.

Evans, a former professor at the University of South Wales, said: “As Britain ostentatiously winds down its own slave system in the 1830s, British capital is boosting slavery elsewhere in the western hemisphere.

“The illegal trade, involving French and Spanish merchants, was flourishing and substantial. There were British (anti-slavery) patrols off the coast of Cuba, but they’re relatively few in number and intercept only a very small proportion of slave ships.”

Conditions at El Cobre were “extremely brutal”, with daily violence and torture. “Slave catchers” were employed to snatch miners who tried to escape to the mountains.

In 1817, under British pressure, Spanish authorities signed a treaty that banned trafficking enslaved Africans to Cuba. But as Cuba’s sugar and coffee plantations relied on enslaved African workers, local colonial authorities ignored it, with 25,000 captives arriving in 1859 alone.

In 1843, pressure from the British and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society prompted legislation intended to prohibit British subjects from owning enslaved people in other jurisdictions, after undercover investigations exposed British mining companies’ activities in Brazil and Cuba, including at El Cobre.

But after lobbying by mining interests, parliament diluted the legislation, allowing companies to retain enslaved workers as their “property” and hire others. The concessions prompted abolitionists to withdraw their support for the law, which failed to end the exploitation.

Neither Grenfell nor Thompson appears to have faced legal penalties for their involvement. Grenfell became the Liberal MP for Preston in the late 1840s, later boasting that his father had been an abolitionist. Grenfell remained a B of E director until 1864.

Almost half of women in west and central Africa cannot withhold consent, with girls worst affected, UNFPA finds

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In west and central Africa, almost half of women (45%) are not able to say no in their own marriage, while a third of women (33%) in east and southern Africa and in Arab states face the same predicament. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

Younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no, compared with older women aged 45 to 49, according to analysis in the report of eight countries with comparable data (Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia). The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

He said that as a first step rape laws across the continent needed to change. According to Murunga’s 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for what, outside marriage, would be classed as rape, and young wives have few options when it comes to protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

Underpinning these laws is the mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because, by marrying, wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. In many areas, religion plays a key role in affirming these beliefs.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organisation supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

She added: “In west Africa, sex is seen as an obligation in marriage. You must do whatever your husband demands.

“Once he has paid your bride price [a traditional marriage custom in which the bride’s family are paid money, livestock or goods by the groom], he owns you and you cannot reject his demands. Whatever he wants, he gets, including in terms of intimacy, finances and raising the children.”

#8. Longest-ever UN climate talks end with no deal as deep divisions exposed

Australia's Chris Bowen sits at the top table at the summit surrounded by other delegates

Countries have failed to reach a major new deal to tackle climate change after deep divisions over fossil fuels and money emerged at the UN COP31 talks in Turkey.

Negotiations were due to finish on Friday but ran late into Sunday in search of common ground, becoming the longest UN climate conference in history.

But the final deal proved unacceptable to the EU, Pacific island nations and others because it failed to directly mention fossil fuels, the main cause of global warming.

Meanwhile, many poorer countries objected to the absence of any clear financial commitments from richer nations to help them adapt to the impacts of rising temperatures.

The last time a COP's main political agreement collapsed like this was in 2009 in Copenhagen.

UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres, in his last COP before he steps down, said he was "deeply disappointed".

He took aim at what he called "powerful fossil fuel interests" who he said were taking "a page from Big Tobacco's playbook: distorting the science, delaying policy, blocking action, and piling up huge profits while people everywhere pay the price".

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, whose country co-hosted the talks, attributed the failure to "difficult geopolitical times".

"That is the reality," he said on Monday.

After two weeks of negotiations, exhausted delegates were presented with a new, draft deal in the early hours of Sunday to try to break the deadlock.

But the words "fossil fuels" did not appear anywhere in the text. The only indirect reference to coal, oil and gas was a nod to a deal agreed at COP28 in 2023, when countries agreed to "transition away" from fossil fuels in energy systems.

The EU's representative, Irish Minister Darragh O'Brien, made it clear that the EU "strongly" objected, saying that the "decisions go nowhere near the level of ambition needed to tackle climate change".

"It is unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no mention of fossil fuels," he said.

Other countries, such as Panama and the Marshall Islands, also criticised the deal for doing nowhere near enough to tackle the pace of climate change.

A first draft of the deal on Monday mentioned "fossil fuel" or "fossil fuels" six times, but that was unacceptable to oil-producing nations.

Saudi Arabia's delegate Mohammad Ayoub said that Arab countries "continued to engage in good faith" but argued that they only accounted for a small proportion of historical greenhouse gas emissions.

Russia warned not to "turn to slogans" over coal, oil and gas, claiming that if countries moved away from fossil fuels too quickly it would lead to "fuel poverty".

Climate advocates reacted with dismay at the failure of the talks.

"It's time that we empower those leaders with a reinvigorated multilateral process that reflects the latest science and represents the will of the majority of the world," he added.

Another major sticking point has been money for poorer countries to adapt to the impacts of climate change, such as more intense heatwaves and more severe droughts.

Last year at COP30 in Brazil, nations agreed to triple the finance for that purpose.

Chris Bowen, Australian government minister and COP31 president of negotiations, said there were "unbridgeable red lines" in the discussions.

He pointed to "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory". At the same time, "the impacts of climate change are getting worse", he said, raising the stakes.

The year 2026 could end as the hottest on record and has brought extreme weather and natural hazards to much of the world, from summer heat and drought in Europe to flash flooding in Nepal.

And COP31 was the first annual UN conference to formally discuss how one of the key international targets for limiting climate change would be missed.

In the Paris talks of 2015, nearly 200 countries agreed to try to limit global warming to 1.5C above pre-industrial levels of the late 1800s, aimed at avoiding some of the worst effects of climate change.

The UN says the Paris deal has driven "near-universal" climate action. In the early 2010s, global warming of 4C or more was thought to be a serious possibility by the end of the century.

Now, the world is likely to limit warming to about 2.8C by 2100 based on current government policies, the UN says.

But action has not been fast enough to meet that Paris goal, with the world likely to "overshoot" 1.5C of long-term warming in the next few years.

Many countries say they are committed to eventually bringing warming back down to 1.5C, but that will require an ambitious ramping up of technologies - which do not yet exist at scale - to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Although the main deal fell through, some agreements were reached, including progress on a "just transition mechanism" - a framework to support workers and communities as economies shift towards clean energy.

The COP31 president also said more than 80 countries backed Turkey's target to increase the share of global energy use met by electricity to 35% by 2035.

But following the weekend's events, some have questioned whether the UN climate process - which relies on consensus - can succeed in a geopolitically divided world.

The UN's climate chief, Simon Stiell, said he would seek advice on potential reform to the process, with the next conference scheduled for November 2027, COP32 in Ethiopia.

"[The consensus process] has worked to get us where we are," he told the BBC. "It got us through Paris... so it can work," but acknowledged it "fell short" on Sunday.

The UN climate process has "suffered setbacks before and come back stronger," Stiell told delegates. "It must do so again."

#7. Race to replant five South African baobabs after export blocked

The trees, intended for an unnamed buyer in Georgia, were reportedly dug up without the necessary permits

Environmentalists in South Africa are racing to replant five centuries-old baobabs after the 20-tonne trees were dug up by an exporter, who tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia.

The baobabs arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, in north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they will be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. The trees had been stranded at a warehouse in Durban since July, when they were intercepted by authorities who said they had been moved without the necessary permissions.

The department of forestry, fisheries and the environment confirmed the trees had been destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted to be exported to Shekvetili Dendrological Park, owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned their export as biopiracy, but later allowed to go ahead.

Otherwise, such exports are very rare, say experts. David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors the trade in wild animals and plants, said: “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish.”

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation, who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. The winter months of May to October, when the trees lose their leaves and are dormant, is a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” she said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges associated with moving such large trees, which are transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five baobabs weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, which means a permit is required to cut or disturb them.

“A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself,” said Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya. “These trees are biodiversity anchors.”

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies, to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in its cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on its fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, added Ippolito. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

Baobabs have shallow roots, which means they do not need to be planted very deeply, and these regrow fairly quickly, said Venter. Once replanted, the trees will be stabilised with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

The trees are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” said Venter.

A criminal case has been opened against the owner of a landscaping company allegedly responsible for the illegal harvesting and transportation of the trees.
