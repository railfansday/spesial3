#95. India has been named a ‘destination of the year’ for 2027 – here are insiders’ tips on travelling beyond the tropes

As tourism booms, three experienced female travellers give their take on how to make the most of travelling here

Don’t get This is India delivered to your inbox? Sign up here

The 13th-century Persian scholar Zakariyya al-Qazwini never travelled to India. But he catalogued it in his famous work The Wonders of Creation and Rarities of Existence, which drew on accounts of a distant land filled with “rarities par excellence”. For al-Qazwini, as Helen Pfeifer writes in the London Review of Books, wonder was not an emotion but an intellectual method of making sense of the world and acquiring knowledge.

India has long occupied such a space in the global imagination, as a land of extraordinary riches; spirituality, customs, chaos and contradictions. This has shaped how the country is perceived from afar, but it can often flatten its complexities.

So when, last week, Travel + Leisure named India its destination of the year for 2027, I found myself considering what it means that the country holds such multitudes. The magazine’s recognition will undoubtedly add to its growing appeal as a travel destination. But expectations built around India can differ from the experience of being there, particularly for women.

My own experiences of travelling in India largely surround my work as a journalist, often visiting places that would never make it into a travel guide. I remember the towering chinar tree outside the home of a woman in Kashmir whose son had been killed in an encounter with the security forces; the rolling hills of Odisha, where we travelled to meet a guerrilla fighter; the tiny hut in Chhattisgarh where, after a 13-hour journey, our team found a meal of rice and lentils. These scenes have stayed in my mind, for their landscapes as much as for the stories and circumstances.

Ultimately, such experiences were far more illuminating than those from a conventional travel itinerary, and taught me to navigate uncertainty and embrace discomfort. For this week’s newsletter, I spoke to three women about the journeys in India that stayed with them and what travelling here has meant to them.

One of the most memorable journeys for travel writer Shivya Nath was a trek through the Tirthan Valley in Himachal Pradesh, with a crew of local women.

Trekking has always been a male-dominated industry and Nath, author of the book Rootless and Restless, was struck by the personal journeys of the women leading the expedition. (She travelled with an organisation which trains women to become guides, overcoming social and family restrictions.) “For some of the women, spending even two nights away from home required negotiating with their families,” she told me.

The route wound through dense forests in the Great Himalayan national park, across ancient bridges, over gushing, undammed rivers, accompanied by the soundtrack of birdsong. “The only sounds you hear are of nature,” she recalled. Along the way, they stopped to forage lingri, a seasonal wild fern used in regional cooking, which they later prepared at a riverside camp they set up for the night.

The following morning, the group hiked to a waterfall, standing together on a large rock in the middle of the cascading water. Being with “just a group of women who love being in the outdoors, was a really powerful feeling for me”, she said.

For Afsha Sethi, a corporate leadership professional from Bengaluru who has travelled extensively in India, one of her most cherished travel experiences is an unlikely one. As part of an extracurricular project when she was in high school, students were paired with a local community through an NGO. She spent a week shadowing a farming family growing rice and areca nut in Karkolli, in northern Karnataka.

Walks through lush forests with waterfalls were a daily routine. “We would come back with leeches on our legs, and then put salt to get rid of them,” she recalled.

The trip also brought her into contact with something she had not encountered before. When her roommate told the host family about getting her period, their sleeping arrangements were shifted to the courtyard. “It turned out to be a great experience, because the weather was very nice and [we were] looking at the stars and just chatting late into the night,” she said. It reminded her that travelling through India can mean encountering different social conventions.

‘The moment somebody puts up something on Instagram, everyone throngs to it’

The travel boom in India is led by domestic travellers. The country recorded almost 2.95bn domestic tourist visits in 2024, a jump of 17.5% from the previous year, according to recent government data. Overall, India ranks 31st in the World Economic Forum’s Travel and Tourism Development Index 2026, up eight spots since 2024.

Women accounted for 43% of foreign arrivals, an interesting counterpoint to India’s reputation as a challenging destination for female travellers.

But the boom raises questions about what happens to the places being discovered. “Social media has definitely accelerated the over-tourism problem,” says Nath. “I think one of the most basic things travellers can do is to avoid geotagging locations.” She suggests choosing community-owned tourism initiatives that allow local people to benefit directly from visitors.

Nayanika Gaffino, a mother and recreational athlete who has also clocked up the miles around the country, has seen this happen first-hand in Goa, where she lives. “The moment somebody puts up something on Instagram, everyone throngs to it … even though it doesn’t have the capacity to handle so many people,” she says, adding there are beaches her family now avoids because of this.

Travelling with girls, Gaffino also has to consider practicalities such as hygiene and access to clean public toilets and safety. As such, she gravitates towards boutique properties that are accustomed to hosting families with children.

Nath acknowledges that solo travel can require extra vigilance. On solo trips, she prefers family-run homestays or guesthouses where someone is aware that she is on her own and can watch out for her. At the same time, she adds, India can be an intensely hospitable place where people generously invite you for tea or a meal.

Discovering a place, she says, should also include finding ways to preserve the very qualities that led you to it in the first place. The journeys the women described involved seeing a side of India that differed from their own expectations. This is what makes al-Qazwini’s understanding of wonder relevant for me. To approach something with wonder is not to be impressed by just beauty or uniqueness but to have a curiosity about what it might show us – its possibilities and its limitations.

#93. French minister under fire for Playboy magazine cover

French government minister Marlene Schiappa has come under fire from members of her own party after appearing on the front cover of Playboy magazine.

Schiappa, who has been a government minister since 2017, appeared on the cover of the magazine to accompany a 12-page interview she did on women’s and LGBT rights. Schiappa, who is the current Minister for the Social Economy and French Associations, was photographed for the cover wearing a white dress.

Schiappa has been a long-time advocate for women’s rights and was appointed as the country’s first ever Gender Equality Minister in 2017. In this role, she successfully spearheaded a new sexual harassment law which allows for on the spot fines to be issued to men who catcall, harass or follow women on the street.

Her appearance has drawn criticism from political colleagues including French Prime Minister Elisabeth Borne.

Borne pulled up Schiappa over the cover, telling her it “wasn’t appropriate, especially during this period,” CNN affiliate BFMTV reported Saturday, citing a source close to the prime minister.

France is currently in the throes of a political and social crisis triggered by French President Emmanuel Macron’s move to push ahead with controversial pension reforms despite widespread public opposition.

“We are in the middle of a social crisis, there is the issue of policing, there are people between life and death, and I have the impression of being behind a smoke screen,” Sandrine Rousseau, Green Party politician and fellow women’s rights activist, told BFMTV Friday.

France wracked by more pension protests amid rising violence on the streets

French politician, Jean Luc Mélenchon who came third in the 2022 presidential elections criticized both Schiappa’s appearance and the decision by French President Emmanuel Macron to give an interview to children’s magazine, Pif Gadget, this week.

“In a country where the President expresses himself in Pif and his minister in Playboy, the problem would be the opposition. France is going off the rails,” Mélenchon tweeted Saturday.

Schiappa responded to her critics in a tweet on Saturday, saying: “Defending the right of women to have control of their bodies, that’s everywhere and all the time. In France, women are free. With all due respect to the detractors and hypocrites.”

French Interior Minister, Gérald Darmanin came to Schiappa’s defense during an interview with French news channel CNews on Sunday calling her a “woman of character.”

“I wanted to say that Marlene Schiappa is a courageous female politician who has her character and who has her style which is not mine, but I respect,” he remarked.

#92. Iran launches drone attacks against US military base in Bahrain

Strikes cast further doubt on peace talks between Tehran and Washington with increasing risk of escalation

Iran has launched drone attacks against a US military base in Bahrain, casting further doubt on peace talks between Tehran and Washington and raising the spectre of escalation.

Blasts sounded over the Gulf nation on Sunday in the second Iranian drone attack in three days in retaliation for US strikes in Iran.

Meanwhile, in Yemen, Iran’s Houthi allies advanced against pro-government forces in the south, opening a potential route to Aden. Iran also claimed that a mine sank an oil supertanker in the strait of Hormuz.

The developments deepened gloom about the chances of any diplomatic breakthrough in the region.

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps said they launched six drones targeting a US air defence radar at the Al-Rifaa helicopter base in retaliation for “hostile US actions”. Authorities in Bahrain said air defences intercepted the drones before they reached their targets.

At a press conference in Tehran the foreign ministry spokesperson, Esmaeil Baghaei, said Iran would continue to respond to any military aggression by the US or other states. Asked if the latest strikes indicated a change in Iranian direction as negotiations with the US have stalled, he said Iran was “not waiting for anyone” and was focused on its security.

US forces have conducted a series of strikes against Iranian targets in recent weeks, including Qeshm Island in the strait of Hormuz.

Donald Trump last week called them minor strikes and said Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states had cautioned him against large-scale combat operations.

However, the president also said Washington was no longer seeking a deal with Iran and that he had instructed the vice-president, JD Vance, to switch his focus to alleged corruption and fraud in Democrat-run cities and states.

Vance – who blamed the Republicans’ loss of Congress in midterm elections on the Iran war – had requested a personal meeting with Iranian negotiators to discuss peace terms.

A conflict that began with joint US and Israeli attacks against Iran in February remains at an impasse, with the US maintaining a naval blockade and Iran its chokehold over the strait of Hormuz.

In a separate development, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards claimed that two ships hit mines in the strait of Hormuz and that one, an oil supertanker with 2m barrels of oil, sank. The ships suffered “massive explosions and fire”, said the statement.

There was no immediate independent verification. The US military recently claimed it had cleared all mines from main routes through the waterway.

The strikes against Bahrain suggested Iran has decided on a tit-for-tat response to US attacks.

Bahrain hosts significant US bases, including a forward base that was home to the US Fifth Fleet and US Naval Forces Central Command, operating as a central support hub for military operations in the Middle East.

Tehran repeatedly targeted the kingdom until September, when there was a lull in attacks. That ended on Friday when Iran launched drones against a US radar facility. US Central Command said it shot down two drones without any injury or damage.

The attacks on Sunday prompted sirens and alerts from Bahrain’s authorities urging residents to seek shelter.

“It’s exactly what I was hoping wouldn’t happen: Iranian attacks starting again, with all the worry and uncertainty that go with them,” resident Hayat Ibrahim, 65, told Agence France Presse.

Bahrain’s foreign ministry called the resumption of attacks “heinous aggression” and a flagrant violation of international law.

The kingdom rejected Tehran’s “flimsy pretexts and baseless claims” to target its territory, it said in a statement. “The kingdom also rejects the transformation of its territory into an arena for settling scores and holds Iran fully responsible for these attacks and their consequences.”

Jordan, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar condemned the attacks and expressed solidarity with Bahrain.

“The Southern Armed Forces announce the implementation of ‌a redeployment operation for its forces from Dhubab Airport and the Bab el-Mandeb area, west of Taiz Governorate,” the main military force fighting for Yemen’s government in the south said in a statement.

The admission signalled the apparent failure of a six-week government counteroffensive to drive the Houthis from the mouth of the Red Sea to secure a key oil route.

#90. Revealed: Israel uploaded millions of photos to Amazon’s facial recognition platform to track Palestinians

Upload came as the Israeli military was increasing its reliance on Amazon’s cloud platform to meet the technological demands of its bombardment of Gaza

The Israeli military uploaded 3.5m photos to Amazon’s cloud platform in order to use the US company’s facial recognition technology to analyse images of Palestinians, the Guardian can reveal.

Leaked documents show the expansive catalogue of images was moved into Amazon’s cloud in the early months of Israel’s devastating aerial bombardment and ground offensive in Gaza.

The images were uploaded by a department of the Israeli military that holds a vast collection of photos covering the Palestinian populations in Gaza and the West Bank.

The department informed Amazon of the decision to upload the images in early 2024 so it could use Rekognition, the company’s AI-powered image analysis platform for a facial recognition project.

The documents obtained by the Guardian form part of an investigation into the Israeli military’s reliance on the technology and infrastructure of Amazon’s cloud computing business to monitor Palestinians.

According to the files and interviews with military sources, Israel has in recent years migrated huge volumes of data – including sensitive intelligence material and information about Palestinians living under occupation – into Amazon’s datacentres.

The large set of images was moved into an Amazon datacentre near Tel Aviv by a department commonly known as Rolling Stone, which sits within a military software unit.

The department takes its name from a system widely used by the Israeli military that serves as a central repository of information about Palestinians living in Gaza and the West Bank.

At the time, the department was exploring using facial recognition technologies to analyse the extensive catalogues of images of Palestinians it holds, several sources with knowledge of its work said.

After Israel’s ground invasion of Gaza in October 2023, its military routinely collected images of Palestinians as they passed through checkpoints, often after they had been forcibly displaced by the bombing campaign.

It’s not clear whether the 3.5m images uploaded to Amazon’s Rekognition were captured at these checkpoints, which effectively served as pop-up surveillance posts.

However, a military source familiar with facial recognition efforts at the checkpoints said Amazon’s cloud infrastructure was used due to the “very large scale” of images collected at the sites.

Israel has long used facial recognition technology, particularly in its occupation of the West Bank. Face scanning is routine at checkpoints, while Israeli soldiers use custom-built smartphone apps to collect images of people living in the Palestinian territory.

In the early stages of its campaign in Gaza, Israel used Rekognition to identify hostages captured by militants in the Hamas-led attack on southern Israel on 7 October 2023. The military also deployed facial recognition technology to identify people it suspected were militants.

The New York Times has previously reported how the military experimented with commercially available technology in Gaza to detect faces in crowds and in drone footage, at times wrongly flagging civilians as militants.

The new documents reveal the Israeli military’s use of Amazon technology, both for experiments with facial recognition and for a more far-reaching project to move its data on to the servers of the computing arm of the conglomerate founded by billionaire Jeff Bezos.

They also shed light on what the Israeli military is moving into the cloud and how it uses Amazon Web Services (AWS) infrastructure to support the collection and processing of Palestinians’ personal data, typically gathered indiscriminately, through intrusive means and without their consent.

The files suggest the ties between AWS and the Israeli military deepened during a period in which Israel has been accused of committing grave crimes in Gaza, including genocide – a charge denied by Israel, but supported by a UN commission of inquiry, Israeli human rights groups and genocide experts.

In early 2024, a small group of Israeli soldiers worked from AWS’s offices in Tel Aviv, located on the 28th floor of one of Israel’s tallest skyscrapers.

The soldiers were exploring ways to use the company’s cloud infrastructure to more effectively operate a facial recognition programme underway in Gaza, several military sources said.

It’s not clear whether their work was made operational or if it related to the 3.5m images uploaded to Rekognition. The presence of the group at AWS’s offices, however, underscores the close relationship between the Israeli military and the cloud provider.

An AWS spokesperson said its customers “regularly visit our offices for briefings and meetings”. They added: “Any suggestion that these visits are anything other than routine customer engagement is wrong and unsubstantiated.”

The foundation of Israel’s relationship with Amazon is a sweeping 2021 contract known as Project Nimbus. Under the multibillion dollar deal, AWS and Google provide cloud services to a wide range of government and military clients.

The Guardian revealed last year that the Nimbus contract contains unusual terms that prohibit the US companies from restricting or suspending Israeli government and military use of their cloud services, even if Israel breaches their terms of service.

This would prevent the kind of unilateral action taken by Microsoft last year when it disabled the Israeli military’s access to technology used to operate a mass surveillance system uncovered by the Guardian and +972 Magazine.

As part of the Nimbus deal, AWS opened new datacentres in Israel in early 2023, allowing the Israeli government and military clients to migrate data from their own servers, according to documents. A large proportion of data was also moved from AWS datacentres in Ireland.

A central component of the Nimbus contract, files show, was an obligation on AWS to provide the Israeli military with access to a segregated area within its Tel Aviv datacentres, fortified with enhanced digital and physical security measures.

This isolated area within the AWS cloud – used to store sensitive material relating to military operations and intelligence collection – was described by the company and its client as a “walled garden”.

The Israeli military’s reliance on AWS reflects its embrace in recent years of leading US tech companies to meet the technological demands of war with rapidly scalable storage, computing power and AI-driven services.

The role of AI in Israel’s offensive in Gaza has come under fresh scrutiny particularly after the release of NAZA, a Guardian-produced documentary about the remote surveillance and killing of Palestinian civilians.

The Rolling Stone department is among AWS’s most significant Israeli military clients, documents suggest. Its use of Rekognition in early 2024 to analyse 3.5m images came after it had expanded its use of AWS products and services to store and analyse its data.

Files show the department migrated significant volumes of the data it holds into the so-called “walled garden” in AWS’s cloud throughout 2023. A military source involved in this effort said it had planned to move all of its databases from the military servers into AWS’s cloud.

The source, who spoke on the condition of anonymity, said: “The plan [at Rolling Stone] was to migrate pictures, videos, all the confidential intelligence information – everything.”

A spokesperson for the Israel Defence Forces (IDF) said it “does not intend to comment on engagements or services related to operational needs”.

The Rolling Stone department oversees several expansive databases that collate data on Palestinians, including the system from which the branch takes its name.

Used by the military for decades, the Rolling Stone system is constantly updated and includes data such as basic personal and family details, property holdings, and information on checkpoint crossings, work permits, and criminal activity.

A soldier who provided testimony to Breaking the Silence, an Israeli NGO, in 2016 described Rolling Stone as “a computerised system where all the information relevant to the control of the Palestinian civilian population is centralised”.

As a result of Israel’s control of the population registries covering Gaza and the West Bank, the Rolling Stone department has amassed millions of Palestinians’ photographs taken for the identity cards issued to every resident when they turn 16 years old.

Sources familiar with its work said the images were routinely used for facial recognition programmes. These include Blue Wolf, a smartphone app Israeli soldiers use in the West Bank to scan Palestinians’ faces and access information about them, including whether they’ve been flagged as suspects for arrest.

In Gaza, at temporary Israeli checkpoints established after the ground invasion, cameras trained on Palestinian people were used to capture their images as they approached the sites. Facial recognition was then used to match people to images held in Rolling Stone’s systems, sources said.

The checkpoints became notorious in Gaza as places where people were pulled aside and detained. Many were taken into Israeli custody and held incommunicado, amounting to enforced disappearances, according to several UN bodies and rights groups.

Documents suggest AWS’s cloud technology was used at times during 2024 to support the work of the checkpoints. Military insiders said the checkpoints were referred to by Israeli soldiers as Nakaz – a Hebrew word for drainage.

#89. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

Saudi Arabia has been accused by anti-Houthi forces over its military strategy after its Yemeni allies withdrew troops from the strategic Bab al-Mandab strait and pulled back forces from parts of Marib in the central part of the country.

The Saudi-backed government explained the repositioning by saying the strategic priority was to break the attritional siege on Taiz, Yemen’s third city, and to protect Aden, the southern port city in which the Saudi-backed and UN-recognised government is now based.

Many blame the Houthi advance on the absence of a consolidated military command and control among the disparate forces backing the Aden-based government. Saudi Arabia responded to the setback by sidelining the commander of its Yemen coalition, Lt Gen Fahd bin Abdulaziz al-Salman, and replacing him with the chief of staff, Gen Fayyad bin Hamed bin Ruqad al-Ruwaili.

The Houthis’ steady advance towards the areas surrounding Bab al-Mandab, the narrow strait linking the Red Sea with the Indian Ocean on Yemen’s west coast, culminated on Sunday with a formal withdrawal of Yemen government forces, including some in the Southern Giants Brigades that had always been reluctant to defend territory outside the old southern Yemen. In 1990 Yemen had been divided in two, and many in the south want to return to a separated state.

The Saudi-backed government launched a fresh military campaign 42 days ago to drive the Houthis from Yemen but so far it has backfired.

The forces redeployed on Sunday have moved along the coast to al-Suqyah, 95 miles west of Aden, and inside the old Southern Yemen.

The retreat seems to be acknowledgment of Houthi resilience and is all the more remarkable given the Saudi air superiority. More than 300,000 Yemeni civilians have been displaced.

Amr al-Bidh, special representative of the exiled Southern Transitional Council (STC) leader, Aidarous al-Zubaidi, a separatist anti-Houthi force that is opposed by Saudi Arabia, accused the Saudis of incompetence – and in effect warned Riyadh that unless it became more open to a southern state it would lack the cohesive forces required to prevent the Houthis becoming a permanent security threat.

He said: “When there is no strategy and no plan, it is inevitable the Houthis would take advantage. The STC will defend the south to the last man but we are fighting with one hand behind our backs since January after Saudi Arabia attacks on the STC.

“The STC forces retain a credible capability on the ground. But we need practical and political support from the region to counter the Houthis and create the basis for a negotiated settlement, one in which the pathway towards southern independence is fully recognised. We cannot continue like this or else the Houthis will keep expanding.”

The breadth of support for the STC, backed by the United Arab Emirates, however, remains disputed. The movement suffered a near fatal setback in January and since then some of the Giants Brigades have switched to backing Riyadh.

Saudi Arabia continues in effect to keep as many as 10 STC leaders hostage in Riyadh, and has refused to let al-Zubaidi to return from exile in Abu Dhabi. Al-Bidh argued the priority should be defensive, avoiding counteroffensives that backfire, improving command and control, and widening the coalition.

Al-Bidh said reconciliation would require Riyadh to acknowledge the political declaration al-Zubaidi made about a timetable for a southern state in January, and that al-Zubaidi’s return would boost morale and help unify chaotic southern military factions.

He said a recent Yemeni government cabinet reshuffle, including a new information minister, was designed primarily to try draw southerners into fighting the Houthis and not to change policy toward the south.

He also urged the UK to use its diplomacy to bring in other actors rather than simply backing Riyadh. A group of southern Yemenis have written to the foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, seeking a review of UK policy. “We recognise that the longstanding advice from your office is to continue following the Saudi line while calling for unity among the anti-Houthi forces,” the letter said. “But you must surely recognise that every time Britain calls for ‘the unity and territorial integrity of Yemen’, it is asking the southern people and their armed forces to fight for a political outcome in which they do not believe”.

The former US special envoy to Yemen Tim Lenderking said the US administration was treating Yemen like a sideshow adding “world powers do not understand the gravity of what it is at stake here”. He warned “the problem is only going to get bigger through apathy and lack of attention”.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

#64. Taking Korea's toughest exam from behind bars

The boys in Mandela have been convicted of serious offences

Students intently focus on maths formulas written on a board before lowering their heads and taking notes as the teacher works through a sum.

The teacher's voice and the scratch of pens fill the room.

At first glance, this could be any classroom in South Korea.

This is Mandela Boys' School, an education facility for young prisoners inside the Seoul Southern Correctional Institution in Guro, south-west Seoul. In South Korea, young people who are sentenced to imprisonment by a criminal court must serve their time in prison.

"The boys here have committed serious offences and received criminal sentences," Jeong Yeo-gyeong, a correctional officer in the Korea Correctional Service's social reintegration division, told the BBC.

"As well as theft, there is a high proportion of serious offences, including violence, bodily injury, sexual violence and robbery."

But by opening educational institutes like Mandela, authorities are hoping to give these young offenders a chance to better their lives after they serve out their sentences.

Opened by the justice ministry in March 2023, it allows boys who were aged 17 and under when their sentences became final to prepare for school equivalency exams as well as the Suneung - the country's notoriously difficult university entrance examination - while still serving their sentences.

Held every November, the Suneung is an infamous eight-hour marathon of back-to-back exams.

Students attempt about 200 questions across various subjects, which include Korean, maths, English, social and natural sciences, among others.

The country itself comes to a standstill - construction work halts, planes are grounded and military training gets suspended to facilitate an optimal testing environment.

A total of 551,864 people have applied to take this year's Suneung on 19 November.

Around 30 prisoners are enrolled in the Suneung preparation class at Mandela Boys' School, and 11 of them have applied to take this year's exam.

Several boys are sitting the Suneung exam this year and are taking intensive preparation classes

Park Soo-young (not his real name), a 17-year-old inmate, has been at the Mandela Boys' Suneung preparation class for two months and hopes to study social welfare.

He tells the BBC that he had dreams of becoming a police officer before committing the crime that landed him behind bars.

He says he could not bring himself to look properly at his mother during his trial. When he turned towards her after his sentence was announced, she was crying.

"Seeing her like that, I broke down too. I wondered how I was going to live from then on, and how I could look after my mother, who was on her own. I think I kept crying for about a week while I was in the detention centre."

Looking back on his offence, he says he feels "very ashamed that I couldn't control that impulse". If he could meet his younger self, he would tell him: "Control that impulse. If you do, everything will be different."

"I'd grab myself by the collar and shake myself as I said it."

He hopes the Suneung and university will offer him a chance to start again.

"Once I'm out, I need to cut ties with the friends and people I used to know and start a new life. I think university is the only way to do that. Going to university would let me leave the area where I used to live, move somewhere else and start something new. That's why I began studying again."

But he describes beginning Suneung preparation as "a struggle every day".

"But the teachers don't let anything slip past. They keep helping us until we understand. If I don't understand something, I put my hand up and they come over and explain it.

Sometimes, if I've missed something, I keep working at it on my own. If I still can't get it, I ask the students next to me to explain," he said.

Park's day begins at 06:30 with a morning check by prison officers. He starts memorising English vocabulary and working through Korean reading passages early in the morning.

"I learn English words whenever I can, even before breakfast. From 07:00, I work through a Korean passage, something like literature or speech. We have to come down to the classroom at 08:00, so we have another check and then come down for lessons."

During breaks, students make calls to their families or leave the classroom for scheduled visits. In the evening, they return for supervised study. Once the day's activities are over, they must go back to their cells together.

Park says he studies until 22:00 before finishing for the day. If he wants to study longer hours, he needs permission from a prison officer.

But even if gets a good result this year, Park will not be allowed to leave prison and attend university straight away. He is only due to be released in 2028, but plans to use this exam as experience and continue studying next year.

"This time, I want to experience how the Suneung works and how competitive it is. Then, next year, I'll work hard from the start so I can get in the year after that."

By opening educational institutes like Mandela, authorities are hoping to give young offenders a chance to better their lives

Some former students from Mandela Boys' School have managed to get into university, according to the justice ministry.

Others have continued their studies after leaving prison, including by enrolling in residential exam preparation academies.

The inmates are taught by a team of seven prison officers, all of whom hold teaching certificates. Each is responsible for a subject.

Visiting instructors provide classes in the humanities, arts and sport, while university student volunteers help with learning. Professional psychological counselling is also available.

Park Min-yong, who applied to work here in 2023, teaches science. And alongside Suneung lessons, he works with the students' to change their habits and behaviour.

One thing he often tells them is that they need to "learn how to become adults".

He believes that while studying in itself does not necessarily turn inmates away from a life of crime, the experience of having worked hard at something: staying with a concept that makes little sense, revisiting a wrong answer and continuing even when grades do not improve straight away, is valuable in itself.

"Studying properly means pushing yourself to your limits, and that takes considerable stamina and patience," he says.

"Whether they chose to do it or not, the experience of persevering, enduring and not giving up easily may help them to some extent when they face the difficulties of life after release."

He says the material they encounter while preparing for the Suneung also gives them new things to think about.

Korean reading passages cover literature, society, science and philosophy. Students have to consider a character's perspective, examine the evidence behind an argument and understand unfamiliar concepts. They also encounter explanations that differ from their own and have to reconsider what they think.

"They gradually learn that it's not just about their own personal view - that these concepts and relationships have been developed through a shared understanding among many people," Park said.

"They cheat as if it's nothing. They give each other answers, even when I'm watching them. Some repeat it after being told off. The boys also have a strong desire for recognition, he says.

"It's all the time. 'Look how much I've done. Look how much my grades have improved. I've done this much - haven't I done well?' They're always like that. They're hungry for it."

This poses a fresh challenge for Beijing, which has become deeply dependent on compute from abroad in the wake of US export restrictions. At least 30% of China's advanced AI computing power is projected to come from renting overseas or smuggling in 2027, according to research by the Institute for Progress, external, a US-based think-tank.

China's foreign ministry called the new rules "economic coercion" and said Beijing "will take necessary measures to firmly safeguard its own legitimate rights and interests".

This is also a problem for South East Asian economies where large numbers of data centres have sprung up recently. Some of the firms behind them are accused of helping China evade US export controls on advanced chips.

One of those is Singaporean data centre firm Megaspeed, whose former director Alice Huang is suspected of being a Chinese spy. Calls for tighter export controls have grown after Nvidia's Jensen Huang admited to an affair with her and resigned as CEO.

Batam in Indonesia is emerging as a major regional data centre hub

"This is despite US AI companies having significantly better compute access and financing," it said.

Still, China needs advanced US chips to ensure its best AI models are competitive, experts tell the BBC.

The new restrictions on South East Asia and Brazil have taken away "one of the more practical routes Chinese AI developers had to accessing frontier computing power," Bhattacharya said.

That has been a longstanding dilemma for Washington, McDonagh said: whether to keep selling chips to China to retain its reliance on the US, or restrict access and risk further spurring Chinese innovation.

#14. European-made pesticides named in 1,605 poisoning reports in Brazil last year

About a third of cases involved chemicals banned for use on EU farms, according to investigation by Unearthed and Public Eye

Pesticides sold by European chemical companies were named in more than 1,600 poisoning reports in Brazil last year, an investigation has found.

About a third of the cases involved chemicals banned from use on farms in the EU because of the risks they pose to people and the environment. But European companies are not banned from manufacturing these chemicals, as long as they export them overseas to countries where they are legally allowed to be used.

Analysis of Brazilian health ministry data by Unearthed and Public Eye in a report published on Tuesday found that of 2,857 poisoning reports that named an identifiable agricultural product, 1,605 named brands sold by European pesticide companies.

Farm workers in Brazil have described their flesh rotting, skin burning, difficulty breathing and vomiting after using the chemicals at work, according to the report. A small number have died accidentally or killed themselves by drinking the pesticides.

Under Brazilian law, reports must be submitted by healthcare professionals to Brazil’s national disease notification system in all suspected pesticide poisoning cases. These reports can be marked “confirmed” poisoning or “exposure” only by the doctor or nurse who saw the patient.

Clinicians sometimes record cases as exposure when they lack the training or support to confirm a diagnosis, but are presented with a patient who says they have been exposed to pesticides or present symptoms of pesticide poisoning. Laboratory confirmation is rare, so recording a diagnosis of “confirmed” poisoning usually depends on clinical judgment.

Experts say clinicians can be reluctant to confirm cases because of a lack of training or support. A brand being named in a poisoning report is not proof that it caused poisoning or death.

“The insufficient training of healthcare professionals to recognise and manage these cases is a major limiting factor,” said Vanda Garibotti, a public health biologist at the State Centre for Health Surveillance in Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil.

High staff turnover and a lack of institutional support meant poisoning cases were rarely confirmed through laboratory tests and often relied on clinical judgment, Garibotti added.

In more than 6,700 medical reports involving agricultural pesticides last year, 2,857 named an identifiable product. Of those, 1,420 were classified as confirmed poisonings. A further 1,108 were classified as exposures; for the remainder the classification was unknown or other diagnoses.

Some of the products are made in Britain. Reglone is the most frequently cited UK-made weedkiller. It contains diquat, a chemical that was banned in the UK in 2020 after experts concluded it posed a high risk to people living near fields where it was sprayed. Diquat is also found in other pesticides.

Despite the usage ban, Reglone is still manufactured by the Swiss-headquartered pesticides company Syngenta at its factory in Huddersfield for export to countries with more permissive regulations, including Brazil.

Nelson Castoldi died last year after accidentally ingesting Reglone on his family farm in Rio Grande do Sul. To save money, his family had bought the weedkiller in bulk, decanting a single litre into an empty cola bottle to avoid hauling a heavy 20-litre canister into the fields.

Working under the scorching sun, Castoldi mistook the black chemical for a soft drink and took a sip. Though he spat it out immediately, the chemical caused his kidneys to fail before attacking his lungs. He died two days later.

“We fell apart,” Ivan Castoldi said of his father’s death. “Four months after our father passed away, our mother also passed away. She cried day and night. She became depressive and had a stroke. She was left alone.

“That poison, Reglone, should be banned in Brazil,” he added. “Not because of what happened to us, but because of its toxicity.”

The Brazilian health ministry recorded 75 cases involving diquat last year, compared with 36 over the five years from 2018 to 2022.

The investigation found Reglone was specifically named in 44 reports, including four deaths. Of those cases, 26 were classified as confirmed poisonings and the rest were classified as exposure, unknown, or adverse reaction.

Dormex, a pesticide manufactured in Germany by Alzchem and distributed in Brazil by the German chemical company BASF, is another product workers said injured them or made them ill.

Sidnei Souza de Oliveira, 49, suffered a severe injury after a jug of Dormex spilled on his leg while he was filling a sprayer. The handle broke as he was transferring the chemical into the tank. At first, his leg turned red, and he continued spraying.

He carried on working for four days before going to hospital. “After 15 days, my leg was black. Chunks of flesh were falling off,” Oliveira said. A doctor said the injury was serious and had to remove dead tissue to stop it spreading towards the bone.

Oliveira said the protective trousers supplied by his employer were thin and allowed the chemical to seep through. He said he had been afraid of losing his job if he stayed away from work for too long.

Dormex contains cyanamide, which was banned from use in the EU in 2008 because of concerns about its effects on human health, particularly among farm workers spraying the chemical. Analysis shows the Brazilian health ministry database recorded 17 confirmed poisonings involving Dormex last year.

Finale, a BASF product identified in the investigation, contains glufosinate, which was banned in the EU in 2018. Roundup – a glyphosate-based weedkiller manufactured by the German chemical company Bayer – was frequently cited in Brazilian poisoning reports, including 12 deaths.

Across all the poisoning reports identified by the investigation as involving products sold by European companies, there were 32 reported deaths. Of these cases, 31 were classified as confirmed poisonings, and 26 were recorded as suicides.

Syngenta products were cited 425 times in the poisoning reports, and BASF 179 times. The company figures are not mutually exclusive: some poisoning reports named products sold by more than one manufacturer.

Nina Schrank, a senior campaigner at Greenpeace UK, said the findings were a “deadly double standard”.

“If a pesticide is too dangerous to use in Britain, it should be too dangerous to export. Ministers must stop allowing Britain to be part of a production line for toxic chemicals that are putting lives at risk overseas.”

The EU’s pesticide approval system requires chemicals to meet strict standards for human and animal health and the environment. Diquat has been banned on EU and UK farms since 2019 and 2020 respectively, after experts concluded it posed a high risk to workers, people living nearby and birds. Cyanamide was withdrawn after an assessment found exposure among agricultural operators could exceed safe levels. Glufosinate, meanwhile, is classified in the EU as toxic to reproduction, meaning it can harm fertility or the development of unborn children.

Suicide prevention is also a factor. A 2020 study found national bans on highly hazardous pesticides could be a cost-effective intervention for reducing the number of people who kill themselves in countries with a high burden of suicides attributable to pesticides.

A Syngenta spokesperson said almost half the Brazilian cases attributed to the company were not classified as confirmed poisonings, adding: “Public Eye and Unearthed are aware of this but have explicitly chosen to disregard the dataset’s final classification.” The company accused the investigation of disregarding the dataset’s final classifications and identified “numerous other inconsistencies” that it said raised “serious concerns” that the evidence was “being selectively interpreted to support a predetermined conclusion”.

“There are real people behind these statistics. Every genuine case of poisoning matters, and every family affected deserves our concern,” the spokesperson added.

Syngenta said Brazil had its own “robust regulatory system” and that products banned in Europe could still be independently assessed and legally registered in the country, citing differences in crops, growing conditions and pest pressures. It said it trained 95,000 people in Brazil in the safe use of its products in 2025, including through free online courses.

Bayer said its products were safe when used according to label instructions, citing what it described as rigorous testing and assessments by global health authorities. The company said it took pesticide poisoning seriously but questioned the reliability of the data, arguing that products and circumstances of exposure had not been independently verified, some chemicals may have been incorrectly identified and symptoms such as headaches and skin irritation could have other causes.

It said “Roundup” was “widely used as a generic term” that could refer to weedkillers made by other manufacturers or counterfeit products.

The company said it provided training on the safe handling and use of pesticides, including protective equipment, and that its programmes reached more than 4 million people globally in 2025. It also argued that a pesticide being banned in the EU did not necessarily mean it was unsafe, saying other countries had robust regulatory systems that took account of local agricultural conditions.

It added: “While the data presented helps to provide information on broad trends of exposure, the data has limitations and warrants caution when drawing conclusions, particularly when looking at individual cases.”

BASF said it was deeply concerned by reports of poisoning that might be linked to its products, adding that it took all such incidents seriously.

A spokesperson said publicly available information suggested some incidents might have involved the intentional ingestion of crop protection products, describing such acts as “deliberate misuse” of products intended exclusively for agricultural use.

BASF said it had global and local procedures to assess and investigate incidents potentially involving its products, and worked with authorities and experts where appropriate to understand cases and identify ways to prevent similar incidents. It said it invested in education and training to promote the safe handling and use of its products, with initiatives in Brazil reaching more than 14,475 participants in 2025.

The company said it was confident in the safety of its products when used in accordance with label instructions and applicable regulatory standards.

It added that Brazilian law required crop protection products to be handled and applied only by authorised and properly trained individuals, and said it took its responsibility for ensuring their safe use seriously.

The NDLEA said the player told them he had come to Nigeria to “build networks” for his basketball career. The agency said evidence on his phone suggested he had “knowledge of and involvement in the drug trafficking activity”. The NDLEA said it detained the player when he attempted to leave the airport with his hand luggage after abandoning two checked-in bags. The agency said those two bags were found to contain 32 parcels of marijuana, and a tracking device.

Nigeria has strict drug laws, and trafficking carries a sentence of life in prison.

A basketball player by the name of Damonte Greene is listed on the roster of the Pattani Raptors in the Thai Premier Men’s League. Greene, a Georgia native, played college basketball for Reinhardt University and South Georgia Technical College. He played semi-professional basketball in the US before representing a number of teams in Thailand.

WNBA star Brittney Griner and former Utah State player Jarred Shaw were imprisoned in Russia and Indonesia respectively for marijuana offenses. Griner was released in December 2022, while Shaw remains in prison.

#1. Five EU states announce plans for immigration ‘return hubs’ in Rwanda

Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Denmark and Greece likely to face challenges to policy criticised as ‘grave moral failing’

Five EU member states have announced plans to set up an offshore centre in Rwanda for people with no legal right to stay in Europe, in a pilot scheme that is expected to face legal challenges and has been denounced as “a grave moral failing”.

The five countries – Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark and Greece – are the first to seek to use the EU’s newly agreed returns law that allows people who have exhausted all avenues of appeal against their removal from Europe to be sent to a third country.

In a statement released on Monday the five EU countries said “concrete negotiations with partner country Rwanda will begin for implementation”, saying the scheme would make “an important contribution to further curbing illegal migration, dismantling the business model of smugglers and relieving the burden on our population”.

Officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

In theory, according to diplomatic sources, migrants would stay in a reception centre in Rwanda for a couple of months, before choosing to integrate into life in the Great Lakes country or return to their home nation.

Unlike the failed scheme pursued by the British Conservative government in Rwanda, or Italy’s agreement with Albania, it will not apply to people pursuing asylum claims.

The reception centre, which would be an open facility, was not intended to house people long term but to accommodate regular flows of people, the countries said. Diplomatic sources said the EU countries were not using British-funded facilities built in Kigali.

The governments involved argue it will protect the European asylum system while removing the incentive for irregular migrants to come to Europe.

Denmark’s immigration minister, Morten Bødskov, whose government has pioneered a tough line on migration widely emulated across the EU, said it was “a huge breakthrough”.

The German interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt, said: “Return hubs offer a positive approach for people without the right to stay and with no prospects in Europe, whose countries of origin do not cooperate in the repatriation process.”

Andy Burnham told reporters last week that the UK had not had direct discussions with European governments involved in return hubs but “we will watch that with interest, learning from what went on in the past. The public want us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration.”

Critics have highlighted the ethical difficulties of striking an agreement with Rwanda’s repressive government, which was found in 2024 by the US state department to allow state security forces and civilian officials to commit human rights abuses with impunity, while also raising concerns about freedom of expression and forced labour.

Spain’s socialist prime minister, Pedro Sanchez, said on Monday the move had no “future or humanity”. “I don’t see any future or humanity in that. It’s not the solution,” Sanchez, an outspoken defender of immigration in a Europe that has tilted to the right in recent years, told Telecinco television.

The French Green MEP Mélissa Camara denounced the agreement between the five countries and Rwanda as “an agreement of shame” and “a grave moral failing”. She said: “Under this agreement, people will be deported thousands of kilometres away from the European Union, with no guarantees regarding the compliance with their rights.”

Alain Berset, the secretary general of the Council of Europe, the continent’s human rights body, said it was not possible “to be so precise a priori” about what any return hubs proposal meant. Speaking to the Guardian before the announcement, he said all members of the Council of Europe were committed to the European convention on human rights “and this must be the lighthouse” for all discussions on return hubs.

“On concrete cases, then we will have to see if this is compliant with the convention,” he said. He stressed that “all countries must be able to define their migration policy”.

Officials involved in setting up the migration centres expect legal challenges, but say the scheme is grounded in human rights law.

Rights organisations say the scheme is bound to fail. “Member states may call them ‘return hubs’ or even ‘welcome centres’, but no euphemism can hide the violence of uprooting people from the life they’ve been building in Europe, and sending them to a country they don’t even know, without family, networks or meaningful prospects,” said Chiara Catelli of Picum, a pan-European organisation that campaigns for the rights of undocumented migrants.
