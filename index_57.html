“Pernicious” and “sagacious” bring two more students down. And then there are five: Afeef Ahmed, Ivaneza, Kahfandi, Caroline Wang, and Maximilian Lumley, all in year 6. Two are returning finalists.

Ivaneza, visibly devastated, slumps into his seat and places his head in his hands. The remaining students spell their words correctly, and Ivaneza tearily returns to his family in the crowd and the comforting embrace of his father. The heartbreak is palpable.

Then, in an instant, after Ahmed correctly spells “immemorial”, the remaining three competitors fall. Kahfandi misses “antepenultimate”, Wang gets tied up by “parmigiana” and Lumley is one letter off getting “genealogy”. Ahmed has won.

The crowd bursts into cheers, and Lumley returns to his seat and stares reflectively at the ground, looking like he has endured a war. His grandmother, Sue, is bursting with pride. “It was so stressful,” she says, “and he looked so calm.”

The UK currency has been boosted by indications the country’s economy is holding up better than expected. Gross domestic product growth in January has been estimated at 0.3% after dropping 0.5% in December.

This resilience is bolstering expectations the Bank of England will maintain aggressive interest rate hikes despite concerns about the health of the global banking sector. Rising rates can boost the domestic currency because they help attract foreign investors searching for higher returns.

Inflation in the United Kingdom also jumped to an annual rate of 10.4% in February, underscoring the need for the Bank of England to maintain its tough approach.

The pound plunged close to $1.03 in September 2022 after the Truss government unveiled plans to boost borrowing while slashing taxes, unleashing panic in financial markets that fueled fears of a recession in the United Kingdom.

The International Monetary Fund predicted in January that the UK economy would contract by 0.6% this year, while all other advanced economies would grow, if only slightly.

“There was a lot of pessimism being priced into the pound,” said Francesco Pesole, a currency strategist at ING.

“There was a big re-rating of growth expectations around Europe, and that impacted the UK,” Pesole said.

“In a volatile market environment, moves are exacerbated,” he said.

#119. Armenia's selective prosecution: A threat to EU relations?

While Brussels has refrained from harsh criticism, Armenian civil society has warned of selective justice against opposition figures. Could the EU's pragmatic stance risk repeating past mistakes?

In early October, Artur Danielyan, leader of Armenia's opposition party Adekvad, was arrested following a Facebook post criticizing Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan.

The incident is part of a growing trend: Over the past two years, Armenian authorities have brought charges against multiple opposition figures, largely based on their social media activity.

Civil society representatives warn that such selective justice signals a worrying turn toward democratic backsliding.

The grounds for Danielyan's detention were Facebook posts in which he criticized both Pashinyan and the country's security services.

One point of contention was Pashinyan's stance on a possible prisoner exchange involving an Azerbaijani soldier accused of breaking into the home of a 39-year-old woman in the Armenian village of Shaki and injuring her and her children.

In an interview with Armenian news outlet Infocom, Pashinyan ruled out swapping the soldier imprisoned in Armenia for detained Armenians in Azerbaijan. Danielyan responded with a scathing attack, calling the prime minister a "scumbag" and accusing him of being unwilling to bring his fellow citizens home.

Armenia and Azerbaijan were in conflict for three decades over Nagorno-Karabakh, a breakaway Azerbaijani region populated by Armenians that declared independence in the 1990s. After Azerbaijan's military offensive in 2023, Baku regained control over the territory, forcing over 100,000 Armenians to flee. Currently, 19 Armenian individuals are being detained in Baku, including former high-ranking officials from Nagorno-Karabakh's de facto administration.

Over a dozen Armenian civil society organizations have condemned the prosecution of the Adekvad party leader, calling it "patently illegal, disproportionate, and politically motivated."

"It is punitive in nature and sends a direct signal to the broader public aimed at silencing dissent, critical views, and undesirable speech", said the joint statement.

Several other criminal proceedings have been launched against activists who criticized the ruling establishment or Pashinyan.

Another prominent case emerged ahead of the parliamentary elections in June. Artur Osipyan, a displaced activist from Nagorno-Karabakh, was arrested after a verbal clash with Pashinyan over his Nagorno-Karabakh policy.

Following the incident, Armenian prosecutors indicted Osipyan for disrupting public order and obstructing the ruling party's campaign. He was also charged with inciting violence against Pashinyan in a social media post from March.

On April 15, 2020, Article 226.2 was added to the Criminal Code of Armenia, penalizing public calls to violence, as well as the public justification or promotion of violence.

After strong outrage by civil society, these offences were subsequently decriminalized. According to a 2025 report by Freedom House, "nearly 95% of court cases concerning incitement to violence under a 2020 criminal code amendment had involved alleged threats against Prime Minister Pashinyan and his supporters, whereas potentially prohibited statements by ruling party members were apparently not subject to the same scrutiny."

Throughout the 2026 pre-election period, the Regional Center for Democracy and Security (RCDS), an independent think tank based in Yerevan, monitored online hate speech targeting Nagorno-Karabakh refugees.

Their reports documented how pro-government bloggers and influential social media users used their platforms to direct hostile rhetoric toward displaced Karabakh Armenians, labeling them with derogatory terms. Despite these findings, law enforcement agencies in Armenia largely refrained from pursuing criminal prosecutions against these activists.

Despite Armenia's efforts to strengthen its ties with the EU, Brussels has given no clear response to the silencing of opposition voices, observers in Yerevan told DW.

"The EU, by and large, has adopted a very pragmatic and geopolitically driven approach," said political analyst Tigran Grigoryan.

"Given various regional developments, including what is happening in Georgia, Armenia has now become the EU's primary partner in the region," he added. "Coupled with the fact that the alternative to Pashinyan is a pro-Russian opposition, there is ultimately no serious response to these criminal cases."

Stefan Meister, Eastern Europe expert at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP), echoed this view. European support for Armenia appears to be "mainly driven by the European Russia policy," Meister said. "So it's not so much about Armenia itself, but it is more about strengthening those forces that are undermining Russia's influence in the country and in the region."

He believes that Pashinyan leverages this geopolitical dynamic to label internal political opponents as Russian proxies. However, not everyone who criticizes Pashinyan is pro-Russian, Meister added. He pointed out that "the EU is somehow falling into this trap by accepting that this is the right narrative."

Democracy, the rule of law, and fundamental rights are at the core of the EU-Armenia Comprehensive and Enhanced Partnership Agreement (CEPA) and the Strategic Agenda for the EU-Armenia Partnership, an EU spokesperson said in a written response to DW's request for comment.

It also pointed out that "the EU is closely following developments in the country, including as regards criminal investigations. In line with Armenia's international commitments and legal framework, such investigations should be carried out in an independent and impartial manner, free from political considerations."

#110. What’s the point? Ten years after the Paris treaty, are climate talks getting anywhere?

Its key temperature goal has been missed but experts say the agreement is still proving a vital moral compass for progress

It was a curious way of conducting negotiations. Ahead of the 2025 climate summit, in the Amazonian port of Belém last year, the Brazilian hosts had taken off the table the issue that most had assumed would be at its heart: concrete plans from each country laying out exactly what they would be doing to cut their greenhouse gas emissions.

Brazil was insisting, instead, that these plans – the bedrock of the Paris agreement – were the business of national governments and not up for negotiation. “In that case, it will be a very short Cop,” one seasoned global south negotiator told the Guardian shortly before, reflecting a widespread unease. “There is nothing to negotiate.”

“They have decided that there is no appetite for negotiation, with the US out of Paris,” said another developing country diplomat. “Countries won’t engage.”

The summit ended with a deal, although few loved it. But, as the faultlines from the Brazilian decision deepen going into this year’s summit, it is clear that the Paris agreement, and climate action around the world, has reached an impasse.

And it is not just Cop insiders who are worried. For many outside the vast white air-conditioned conference halls, the question, as this year’s summit begins, will be: what is the point?

Those same governments have pledged year after year since the Paris agreement was signed in 2015 – and entered into force a year later – to limit global heating to 1.5C above preindustrial levels. Yet temperatures for two years have exceeded that threshold, and the UN revealed a few weeks ago that its most optimistic estimate was now for about 1.7 to 1.8C of heating.

There could be no clearer evidence that the Paris agreement, on the face of it, has failed. It is impossible not to wonder whether the Cop process itself is just not fit for purpose.

The charge sheet against Paris is long, with the central issue being the failure to achieve its goal. Beyond that immediate failure, the negotiation processes are endlessly convoluted and cause their own problems. It took more than five years just to negotiate the Paris “rulebook” under which the treaty is interpreted and put into effect.

The convention that all resolutions must pass by consensus has given bad actors free rein. Donald Trump’s allies – in Saudi Arabia, Russia, Venezuela and some other petrostates – frequently veto measures and obstruct progress.

It took more than 30 years of negotiation since the UN framework convention on climate change was signed in 1992 for fossil fuels even to be addressed directly. This was at Cop28 in Dubai in 2023, where countries agreed for the first time to “transition away from fossil fuels” but failed to set a timeframe or any policies to back up the resolution.

The US, the world’s biggest economy and responsible for about one in every eight tonnes of carbon dioxide emitted today, has withdrawn from Paris under President Trump.

Even parts of the developed world which support the process have mostly failed to come up with convincing plans: Australia, co-host of Cop31, has set no end to fossil fuel exports; its fellow co-host Turkey plans to increase its carbon output. The UK is opening a gasfield and mulling a new oilfield, Germany has dropped its electric vehicle sales targets and scrapped a renewable heating law. Meanwhile, most rich countries are reducing their contributions to climate finance for the poor.

Most of all, the slow pace of the Paris timetable might have seemed fine in 2015, when temperatures were rising at 0.2C a decade. Today, the rate of rise is 0.4C a decade and still accelerating.

“The structures of Cops and Paris are labyrinthine, bureaucratic, and ultimately dispiriting,” said Paul Bledsoe, a former Clinton White House climate adviser, now with the American University. “It may be time to streamline them to focus more deliberately on national emissions reduction goals.”

As ordinary people watch these failures outside and inside the process, some are losing faith. The Paris agreement was a sensation, celebrated around the world. In the years since, the aura of Cop has faded, as meetings seem to disintegrate into arguments over wording and compromise.

“A lot of the commitments, the promises that were made, just turned out to be a bunch of hot air,” said Juan Carlos Monterrey, former climate envoy for Panama, now chief executive of the Vanglabs consultancy. “Under that measure, we have failed.”

Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, said: “The fact that global warming is accelerating, that we inevitably will be breaching the 1.5C limit within three to five years, that tipping points are coming closer fast, that Earth’s resilience is showing worrying signs of decline, and that we still allow ourselves to follow a global path that will take us to a catastrophic 2.6C-3C, and that this is allowed to happen within the framework of the Paris agreement, cannot be considered as anything but a global failure.”

But, like most involved in international climate efforts, both refuse to concede that Paris is done for. “On the other hand,” said Rockström, “Paris is all we have. And we know that the global climate crisis can only, in the end, be solved if all countries in the world simultaneously move away from planet-damaging heat injection.”

Monterrey agrees. The most important fact about the Paris agreement, and the Cop process, he says, is that they exist. “Paris is our moral compass. It has told us where we should go. The speed of travel is not what it needs to be, but Paris has set the direction.”

This year’s Cop is the first to have a split presidency and host. Being held in Turkey but presided over by Australia, Cop31 is a messy compromise between two countries whose other chief point of commonality is an admiration for coal.

Unexpectedly, however, the skies are a little brighter over this year’s meeting than anyone might have expected. Brazil has decisively re-elected Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva who has already overseen the biggest reductions in deforestation for decades.

The US midterms delivered a drubbing for the right, while in Serbia and Hungary this year democracy has won out over authoritarianism. Though the far right is still on the march across much of Europe, and autocrats still rule in most of the countries with the biggest fossil fuel industries and highest emissions, the replacement or weakening of anti-climate authoritarians by pro-climate parties is a positive sign. Globally, a large majority of people support climate action.

Action beyond the framework of Cop is marching on. After the failure of last year’s Cop30 in Brazil to provide a strong statement on phasing out fossil fuels, a group of nearly 60 countries held an alternative meeting in Colombia in April and agreed to draw up their own national roadmaps for the “transition away from fossil fuels”.

Three major countries have so far done so: France, Germany and the Netherlands. Bledsoe wants to use this model for more side agreements on the “super pollutants” such as methane, refrigerant chemicals called HFCs, ground-level ozone, nitrous oxide and black carbon, or soot.

Cutting methane by 40% could alone hold down temperatures by about 0.3C in the next decade, and deeper cuts beyond that could yield a 0.5C decrease by 2100.

“Paris was never intended to do all the hard work itself,” Bledsoe said. “We need to use the G7, the G20 and other forums too, and we need to elevate the climate to the level of national security concern, and see it as an economic threat.”

While the failures of Paris might seem obvious, its “moral compass” has also translated into greater success in the real world than many people realise.

“The solar panels on my roof and the BYD Dolphin [electric car] my neighbour drives are exhibit one and exhibit two of Paris’s success,” said Claudio Angelo, of the Observatório do Clima thinktank. “Will Paris prevent overshoot of 1.5C? No way. Will it prevent 2C of warming? Unlikely. But the transformation it has triggered can’t be exaggerated.”

Perhaps the clinching argument lies in China, the world’s biggest emitter of greenhouse gases and second-largest economy.

“China is taking multilateral climate efforts more seriously than many other countries, and certainly more seriously than it did a few years ago,” said Li Shuo, director of the China climate hub at the Asia Society Policy Institute. “The reason is simple: its economic stakes have grown enormously. If the world abandons decarbonisation, where will China sell its solar panels and electric vehicles?”

And, stung by high fossil fuel prices, many developing-country consumers are grasping clean technology without waiting for assistance. #101. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

#94. Ebola: Why is there still no vaccine?

Ebola has been known about for decades, but there is still no vaccine against the Bundibugyo strain of the virus causing the current outbreak. New research raises hopes of protection against the various types of Ebola.

1976: A hitherto unknown disease breaks out near the Ebola river in the country then known as Zaire, now the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). This was the first scientifically documented Ebola epidemic.

Now, 50 years on, the DRC once again finds itself at the center of a major Ebola outbreak. This time the pathogen is the less common Bundibugyo type of the genus Orthoebolavirus. The virus was identified in 2007 during an outbreak in Bundibugyo, Uganda. Another outbreak occurred in the DRC in 2012.

How can a virus that has been known for decades continue to infect thousands of people, yet there is still no licensed vaccine or official treatment?

Torsten Feldt, a doctor of tropical medicine at Düsseldorf's University Hospital, sees this as a missed opportunity. "We had come across the Bundibugyo virus in two previous outbreaks: 2007 in Uganda and 2012 in DR Congo. So we had enough time to develop vaccines and therapeutic options. But we didn't make use of this opportunity, meaning crucial time was lost during the current outbreak."

Leonie Mayer from the German Center for Infection Research (DZIF) and the Institute for Infection Research and Vaccine Development at the University Medical Center in Hamburg-Eppendorf agrees. "They could of course have developed a vaccine and run pre-clinical trials before," Mayer told DW. She stressed, however, that their effectiveness can only be assessed and confirmed during an actual outbreak.

Mayer is the lead author of a recently published study that examined whether Ervebo, an Ebola vaccine that has already been licensed, can be used against other types of Ebola as well.

To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

From an immunological point of view, and compared to — for example — influenza or HIV, Ebola is a very worthwhile candidate for a vaccine. Influenza viruses are constantly mutating, and the human immunodeficiency virus is particularly good at evading our immune defenses. Ebola is far more predictable.

"I think — and perhaps it's kind of a blessing in disguise — that it will be relatively straightforward to develop a vaccine against Bundibugyo," says Mayer.

Herein lies the paradox. From a scientific point of view, Ebola is a good vaccine candidate, yet there is still no licensed form of prevention against the Bundibugyo variant that is currently circulating.

Mayer explains that vaccines cannot be developed as a precautionary measure against every potential pathogen. Lack of funding or the prioritization of other pathogens often have a role to play. Many researchers had assumed that a fresh outbreak of the Sudan Ebola virus was more likely, and vaccine development for this strain is more advanced.

Another part of the problem is that Ebola is not one single virus. The known, highly dangerous orthoebolaviruses include Ebola virus (EBOV), previously called the Zaire Ebola virus (ZEBOV), the Sudan virus (SUDV), and the Bundibugyo virus (BDBV). The existing vaccine, Ervebo, was developed and licensed for use against the Zaire variant. But there is still no approved protection against the others.

This is why the existing Ebola vaccine has been of only limited use to people in the areas of the current outbreak.

Another factor may also be relevant: The Bundibugyo virus appears to be less aggressive than the Zaire variant. At first, that sounds like good news, but it may make it harder to contain, as people remain mobile for longer after being infected, and can pass on the virus without realizing.

In the recently published study, Mayer's research team from the DZIF investigated whether the already licensed Ebola vaccine could trigger an immune response not only against EBOV, but against other types of Ebola as well. They found cross-reactive T-cell responses against Bundibugyo, and also, to a lesser extent, against the Sudan virus.

For Mayer, this is already an important finding: "We now know that the immune cells can most likely also recognize the Bundibugyo virus." But she also observes: "Our data does not enable us to answer the question of whether this immune response actually provides protection, or if it results in a milder progression of the disease." This is currently an important gap in our knowledge.

If there's a vaccine that has already been licensed, and indications that it could protect people, why isn't it simply being deployed?

Mayer explains the dilemma. For one thing, she says, it's not ethical to deploy a vaccine if you're not sure whether or how well it works. Also, if it were used on a large scale, it would no longer be possible to establish whether it actually works: A reduction in cases could also be a result of other measures, or the epidemic might already have passed its peak.

Jürgen May, a specialist in tropical diseases at the Bernhard Nocht Institute, also warns against mass vaccination: "Mass vaccination without proof of efficacy could create a false sense of security, forfeit trust, and, if it does not provide sufficient protection, make study impossible."

It's very important for there to be acceptance on the ground. "If we inoculate people with a vaccine on a large scale, and afterwards it turns out that it was nothing like as effective as we expected, it's very difficult to communicate that to people on the ground," Mayer says. In a worst-case scenario, people could subsequently refuse inoculation with more effective vaccines.

But the current outbreak also shows that medical innovation alone is not enough. Conflicts, difficult transport routes, attacks on healthcare facilities, and gaps in contact tracing all make it harder to contain.

Torsten Feldt says identifying Bundibugyo is no longer a problem in itself. What is often limiting is the practical availability of diagnostic tools. "In remote or conflict-affected regions, samples often have to be transported over long distances," he explains.

Here we see another Ebola paradox. The world is getting ever better at diagnosing infections, but it still struggles to contain outbreaks fast enough. The authors of an article in the current issue of Nature come to the same conclusion: The problem is often not identifying cases, but the speed with which people react. Finding Ebola is often easier now than stopping Ebola, they write.

Many researchers believe the current crisis could potentially also provide a great opportunity. The so-called Tumaini study, which is testing different vaccine candidates, has now been launched in the DRC.

These include Ervebo, developed by Oxford University, and a Moderna mRNA vaccine. The study aims to provide the first reliable data about their efficacy against the Bundibugyo virus.

Whether these vaccines could still help control the current outbreak is uncertain. There is hope, but experts estimate that, realistically, it will be several months before any such vaccine is available for widespread use; perhaps not until mid-2027.

"Ideally, we will develop cross-reactive pan-Ebola vaccines," Christof Geldmacher, a co-author of the DZIF study, says of their long-term plans. This is also something that is being worked on. For now, though, the focus is on developing an effective vaccine against the Bundibugyo variant of the virus.

#60. Paraguay’s president quits after millions of people’s savings frozen in bank crisis

Santiago Peña’s rightwing administration had authorised Ueno Bank to drastically expand operations before collapse

Paraguay’s rightwing president, Santiago Peña, resigned on Thursday after a political and financial crisis collapsed the South American country’s banking system in recent days.

At the centre of the crisis is Ueno Bank, which in just a few years under Peña went from an obscure digital bank to one of the country’s largest lenders, claiming to have about 60% of Paraguay’s adult population as customers.

Last Monday, the central bank ordered Ueno into resolution over insolvency, freezing the deposits of 3 million customers, leaving hundreds of thousands of families without access to money for food and basic daily expenses. Bank executives were arrested, and people took to the streets in massive protests.

In power since August 2023 and with a term running until 2028, the 47-year-old economist was already facing impeachment proceedings that had broad support.

“Dear compatriots, today I address you at one of the most difficult moments of my life. With my heart set on Paraguay and with the conviction that our republic must always come before any person or circumstance, I have decided to submit my resignation as president of the republic,” said Peña in a video.

Thousands of Paraguayans gathered outside Congress on Thursday to protest against Peña. Lawmakers voted unanimously on Thursday to launch impeachment proceedings against Peña over the crisis, alleging misconduct and a conflict of interest in dealings with the bank to which he is tied.

Ueno Bank began as a finance company in the 1970s and, in 2021, was bought by Grupo Vázquez, one of Paraguay’s largest business and industrial conglomerates. At the end of 2023, it received authorisation from the central bank, already under Peña’s administration, to operate as a digital bank.

The bank rapidly expanded its operations, attracting customers with generous cash-back offers on groceries and sponsoring Paraguay’s national football team. On Wednesday, the players released a letter “demanding” a “forceful response” from the government and the justice system.

The bank came to manage between $800m and $850m in deposits from state and pension entities, including retirement funds, prompting criticism over conflicts of interest because of Peña’s previous shareholding ties to Ueno Holding.

Peña said that, as an economist, he had merely made an “investment”, but that he had already disposed of all his shares. When the local press pointed to possible improper favouritism in directing state and pension funds to the bank, he called it a “false media campaign” aimed at weakening his government.

On Wednesday, opposition lawmakers and even members of Peña’s Colorado party filed an impeachment motion holding the president responsible for the collapse. He had been a shareholder in the group that ran the bank until early last year and, in recent months, had reportedly been ignoring warnings from the central bank and the media about its operations.

Political pressure intensified on Thursday morning when the ex-president Horacio Cartes, his former ally, posted a message endorsing the impeachment.

“We will not stand on the side of the theft of public, private and pensioners’ funds. We must think of the people and of the values we uphold in the Colorado party. We support the impeachment of the president of the republic, Santiago Peña, in order to restore the institutional order of the republic,” he wrote.

Other lawmakers followed suit and began debating impeachment when Peña released a video announcing his resignation.

“I do so thinking of the stability of our country, social peace and the need to preserve our democratic institutions,” said the now former president.

“I also want to apologise to the people if at any point I failed them … But today my priority is only one thing: the peace of mind of Paraguayans who trusted their savings to our financial system … My greatest wish is that the return of those funds is guaranteed,” he added.

Since the scandal broke, Peña has insisted in interviews and statements that he committed no wrongdoing.

Opposition senator Rafael Filizzola called the case “the biggest fraud carried out from political power in the democratic era … This is a bank created from political power, to use public money for private businesses linked to political power, with the president of the republic at the head of it,” he told 1020 AM radio.

Peña, a former finance minister who promised to rebuild a “great Paraguay”, was a key ally of the US president, Donald Trump, in the region – one of the few Latin American presidents to attend Trump’s second inauguration. US officials, including the secretary of state, Marco Rubio, repeatedly praised him as a “strategic partner”, and Peña recently flew to Washington to discuss critical minerals and economic cooperation. Neither Rubio nor Trump has yet commented on Peña’s resignation.

It is still unclear who will replace him or when, because his resignation must, in theory, be accepted by Congress – in which case the vice-president, Pedro Alliana, would take over. Alliana is also suspected of involvement in the Ueno Bank scandal, which he has denied.

About an hour after Peña’s announcement, the lower house unanimously approved the impeachment motion, which now goes to the senate. If approved, even after his resignation, he would lose his senate seat and, with it, parliamentary immunity, leaving him to face any criminal proceedings in the ordinary courts.

#59. Texas mayor instructs police to limit co-operation with ICE

John Whitmire is also calling on Congress to change immigration laws

The mayor of Houston, Texas, has directed the city's police to limit co-operation with federal immigration officials.

The move by John Whitmire, a Democrat, comes on the heels of stunning victories by the party in Texas during this week's midterm elections. It breaks with policy set by Republican Governor Greg Abbott, who lost his re-election bid.

"I do this to begin a healing process," Whitmire said on Friday, and to "reduce fear and anxiety across our community". He added: "I also do this knowing that elections matter."

There has been months of public outcry in Houston after an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) officer fatally shot a man who was driving to work.

Houston police chief Noe Diaz said on Friday that officers will now only contact ICE if someone they arrest for a jailable offence also has an administrative ICE warrant, which are issued internally by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), within which ICE operates.

"For example, if our police officers pull someone over for a minor traffic infraction and they write a citation, that person receives a citation and goes about their day," Diaz said.

Under the previous rules, Houston police officers were required to inform ICE if anyone they were dealing with came up as having one of these warrants attached to their name when they were searched on the National Crime Information Center (NCIC) - a digital database that includes records on wanted persons, stolen property, certain missing people and those arrested and fingerprinted for serious offences.

Whitmire said on Friday that he had directed the Houston Police Department to make the changes through an executive order.

He also called on Congress to change federal immigration rules, saying "ICE needs better training. They need body cameras. They need to be trained in de-escalation."

He added, "But this can only be done in Washington. I can only control what I can control."

#21. Sinkholes, scarred land and scapegoats: the scourge of illegal gold mining in Johannesburg

There is no end in sight for the drivers of illegal mining, while police action appears ineffective

Cora Bailey has been encountering illegal miners ever since she first started treating injured animals in the townships around Roodepoort, west of Johannesburg, in 1991. But as the price of gold has soared, so too has activity in the abandoned mine shafts that mark the area.

In late September, dozens of zama zamas – the name given to illegal miners in South Africa, meaning roughly “to take a chance” in Zulu – arrived to dig for gold across the dirt road from her charity, Claw (Community Led Animal Welfare).

Soon, they were taking water for their panning and processing from the organisation’s two 25,000-litre water tanks, which are filled irregularly by the city government for the 100 or so rescued and injured animals on site.

“We’ve asked them really nicely and haven’t ever refused them water to drink … But, for the first time ever, we closed the gate at 12 [midday], because we literally had about two buckets of water left,” Bailey said in mid-October.

Police told her that the scale of the illegal mining was too big for them to do anything about, she said at the time. About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. But within a few days a smaller group had returned, although Bailey said they were now taking Claw’s water only for drinking.

South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.

Zama zamas have stepped into those voids left in the earth. While there is no comprehensive data on them, experts agree that a majority are from neighbouring countries, principally Lesotho, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

The skyline south of Johannesburg is punctuated by the pale yellow ridges of mine dumps. Increasingly, the ground is also caving open.

For politicians seeking votes in local elections on 4 November, the culprit is clear: illegal miners. In May, Kenny Kunene, the Johannesburg council member responsible for transport, took journalists on a tour of sinkholes that he claimed were the fault of zama zamas, including a section of Wemmer Pan Road that had completely collapsed.

The road was once the main artery linking the city’s central business district to the south-eastern suburbs. In one section it has completely disappeared, now raw earth pitted with the holes where illegal miners have been digging.

However, when it comes to sinkholes, experts say zama zamas are being scapegoated.

Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, who has worked extensively with illegal miners, noted that the porous dolomitic rock to Johannesburg’s south and west becomes unstable when water is removed.

“The biggest culprit of sinkholes in South Africa is formal industrial mining, which dewaters at a scale of hundreds of millions of gallons of water removed from underground every day,” she said.

“There are areas where there is a lot of residual gold, which are being very intensely mined by zama zamas, and some of that definitely causes structural collapse, there’s no doubt about it. But the idea that this was invented or caused for the first time by zama zamas is completely fallacious.”

Zama zamas are also frequently blamed for violence in mining areas, including a September mass shooting in Wedela township, west of Johannesburg. However, David van Wyk, the lead researcher at Bench Marks Foundation, said culpability laid with the often-feuding gangs that controlled access to the shafts.

“The guys working in the mine are actually super-exploited cheap labour … The guys with the guns are the guys who take the gold,” he said. “They don’t go underground.”

#1. Kenyan doomsday sect leader jailed for 200 years for murder of 190 children

Paul Mackenzie convicted over deaths of children and one adult after followers were ordered to starve themselves and their families

The leader of a Kenyan doomsday sect has been sentenced to 200 years in prison for the murders of 190 children and one adult in one of the world’s worst cult disasters in recent history.

The sentence, comprising 100 years for murder by starvation and 100 years by direct homicide, came two weeks after the court in Kenya’s coastal city of Mombasa found the cult leader guilty of the crimes.

Prosecutors said Paul Mackenzie and his Good News International church ran ​a Christian doomsday sect whose members were ordered to starve themselves and their children to death to go to heaven before the world ended.

His accomplices threatened and beat victims to ensure they followed his orders. The emaciated bodies were found scattered in shallow graves and dumped in latrines. Some had been buried alive.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after police received a tipoff, and weeks later authorities began uncovering bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest near the coast in south-eastern Kenya.

In total, more than 400 bodies have been recovered. Many were severely decomposed and only 26 could be identified.

Mackenzie denied murder charges, saying he did not force the victims to starve, but the court rejected that argument.

The Shakahola tragedy has gripped Kenya since the discovery of mass graves in the forest more than three years ago.

It raised questions about how Mackenzie was able to continue operating despite being on the radar of the authorities since 2017, when police searched his premises.

In 2019, authorities shut down Mackenzie’s church and he relocated ‌to the Shakahola forest, police said.

The case has also led to calls for tougher oversight of churches and other religious organisations in a country where religious faith plays a prominent role in public life.

Mackenzie, a former taxi driver, forbade cult members from sending their children to school and from going to hospital when they were ill, describing such institutions as satanic.

Cult members lived in makeshift homes of polythene sheeting and thatch that Mackenzie divided into areas with biblical names such as Jerusalem and Judea.

The plight of his followers began ​to emerge in March 2023, when a man told ​police his brother and wife had starved their children ​to death in the forest on Mackenzie’s orders.

Mackenzie was arrested but released on bail, after which relatives said he brought forward the date ​on which he told his followers the ‌world would end. Police rearrested him and ​started combing through the forest more systematically and began exhuming mass graves.

Last month’s judgment covered 191 deaths while investigations into more than 200 other deaths are continuing.

Justice Diana Kavedza-Mochache said in a hearing lasting more than eight hours that in some cases children were isolated and denied food while their parents watched.

Mackenzie faces other charges over the forest deaths, including those of terrorism, which he has denied.

Prosecutors said that while he was in detention he also directed the deaths of 52 people in Kwa Binzaro village, about 18 miles from Shakahola. Mackenzie has pleaded not guilty in that case.
