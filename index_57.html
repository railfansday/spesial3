The checkered or arctic skipper, carterocephalus palaemon, has been named the 2027 butterfly of the year by BUND, the German Federation for the Environment and Nature Conservation.

As so often, the animal has a more literal and descriptive moniker in German, where it's known as the gelbwürfelige Dickkopffalter, or "yellow-checked thick-headed butterfly" — an homage to two of its most distinguishing features, its coloration and its large head.

BUND said that with its choice for 2027 it wanted to "raise awareness about the loss of valuable water-based ecosystems." It said that the loss of transitional areas between woods and open land with long grasses were crucial to both the butterfly and the caterpillars and were being lost.

"Forest and path edges are often mowed short. With this, the butterflies' feeding and wintering locations are lost. The climate crisis is also weighing on the checkered skipper: for the caterpillars' development, carterocephalus palaemon needs damp living spaces or at least a damp microclimate."

The species is not classified as endangered in Germany at present, but its numbers are in decline.

One of the two Iraqi nationals arrested yesterday on suspicion of plotting an attack on Cologne synagogue had been placed on a list of people who should leave the country in 2024, according to North Rhine-Westphalia's Refugee Ministry.

The 34-year-old Ameer J. appears to have been placed on a list of people from Iraq who were nominally supposed to leave the country but who were granted a dispensation to stay known in German as a "Duldung," stemming from the word for tolerate.

"The first information available to us indicates that one of the two suspects was included on the so-called Unna List that our ministry compiled in the autumn of 2024," NRW's Refugee Minister Verena Schäfer told regional broadcaster WDR.

This followed similar reporting in German media, including from the Kölner Stadt-Anzeiger newspaper and Der Spiegel.

WDR cited authorities in the city of Cologne as saying the decision to tolerate the man staying was based on him having regular employment at the time, and because he was not in need of replacement passport documentation given his valid Iraqi passport.

The regional broadcaster also reported, citing security sources, that the alleged Islamist had been under observation for several months, partly after he was noticed by investigators looking into an attack on a Jewish site in Munich in April of 2026.

The two men arrested on Thrusday were accused of plotting attacks against the synagogue in Cologne and one other, unnamed, European city. They are thought to have been working for or in conjunction with a pro-Iranian terrorist group. Prosecutors said that, for reasons that were not yet clear, the planned attacks were not ultimately carried out.

French hard-left presidential hopeful Jean-Luc Melenchon visited Berlin on Friday, and said he sees "a need for a thorough overhaul of the Franco-German relationship."

Melenchon, who is hoping the radical economic platform he's championed for decades in France might finally resonate with voters in the vacuum left by Emmanuel Macron as he hits term limit in April's presidential elections, said he opposed the German government's spending and defense policies.

"I am hostile to the liberal [economic] policy of German governments that dominates the European Union," the 75-year-old said.

Melenchon advocates drastically increasing public spending in France, already the much more debt-laden of the EU's top two economies, and canceling French debt held by the European Central Bank in order to do so — a step that critics argue would risk damaging both France and Europe's economies more generally.

The 75-year-old said he also rejected Chancellor Friedrich Merz's plan to increase German defense spending.

"We have the duty to ask: to make war against whom," Melenchon said, warning that a stronger Germany could make nuclear-armed France and the rest of Europe a "target" for other countries.

"They need to tone it down, because this one-upmanship leads nowhere," he said.

Melenchon has enshrined leaving NATO and nationalizing defense companies in his party's election platform, prompting allegations of him cosying up to Russian President Vladimir Putin.

He rejected the notion that he was "Germanophobic," citing his political or musical affection for Karl Marx and Beethoven.

He touted his party's ties with Germany's Left Party (die Linke) and met with party chair Ines Schwerdtner in the capital on Friday while plugging the German translation of his book.

The Left, too, has criticized Germany's defense spending plans. It's increasingly vague on the question of NATO, no longer explicitly calling for an immediate exit as it once did, but still advocating "replacing" NATO with some other kind of collective European security architecture.

European military backing for Ukraine in its fight against the ongoing Russian invasion continues to enjoy widespread support in Germany, according to the results of a new survey published on Friday.

The representative "Politbarometer" poll published by public broadcaster ZDF found that 37% of Germans consider the current level of European support for Ukraine to be just right, while another 31% think it should be increased. Fewer than 30% were in favor of a reduction in military support.

The survey also found that almost two thirds of Germans (64%) think Russia poses a large (39%) or very large (25%) threat to Germany, while the rest consider the threat posed by Moscow to be not so big (23%) or non-existent (11%).

When broken down politically, the survey found that supporters of almost all parties consider the danger posed by Russia to be big or very big. Only far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) voters bucked the trend with 75% believing that Moscow poses either no threat at all or only a minor one.

The ZDF survey results differ slightly from those published by the Körber foundation on Tuesday, which suggested for the first time that German military backing for Ukraine no longer enjoyed majority support among the population.

The ZDF survey, however, asked respondents about "European" support for Ukraine rather than about Germany specifically.

German Labor Minister Alexander Schweitzer (SPD) submitted his draft pension reform legislation on Friday in which he reportedly intends to maintain the option of retiring two years early without benefit deductions for those with 45 years of pension contributions – a policy often referred to as the "pension at 63."

At the same time, however, eligibility requirements are set to become more stringent and more closely tied to an individual's actual lifetime work contribution.

In practical terms, this means that individuals with a long working life will still be able to retire two years early without deductions.

From 2029 onwards, however, only periods of employment, child-rearing, and the care of relatives will count toward the 45-year contribution requirement.

Periods during which an insured person received unemployment benefits, sickness benefits or transitional allowances – or held only a low-wage, part-time "mini-job" – will no longer be credited.

For weeks, Germany's coalition government partners, the conservative Christian Democrats (CDU) and Schweitzer's center-left Social Democrats have been struggling to reach a compromise on this issue.

A pension commission had proposed abolishing this popular type of pension, a proposal opposed by the SPD.

A group of leading coalition politicians, including Schweitzer, reportedly held final discussions regarding the plans as recently as Wednesday evening.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz will have a private audience with Pope Leo XIV at the Vatican on Monday before meeting Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni in Rome later, a government spokesman confirmed on Friday.

Germany's left-wing populist BSW party is holding its annual conference on Friday and Saturday as it looks to remain relevant following a series of electoral defeats and internal splits.

The BSW was founded in 2024 by former Left Party politician Sahra Wagenknecht who leant her initials to the party's original name, the Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance or Bündnis Sahra Wagenknecht (BSW). The founder herself stepped down as party leader in 2025 and the party officially changed its name last month to "Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason" – also still abbreviated as BSW.

After initial successes in European and eastern German state elections in 2024, the BSW failed to reach the minimum 5% required to enter parliament in the 2025 federal election. In September, the BSW crept into the state parliament in Saxony-Anhalt with 5.3% of the vote but fell short in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and Berlin.

Ahead of its party conference in Dresden this weekend, the BSW has been split over its approach to the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) — with which it shares some economic and geopolitical policies, such as support for trading relations with Russia and opposition to military support for Ukraine.

Like most other German political parties, the BSW has ruled out entering into any coalition with the AfD but, in Saxony-Anhalt, has already voted with the far-right on certain policies and has held talks over support for certain issues which an AfD minority government would want to implement.

The BSW intends to abstain from the vote for the AfD's Ulrich Siegmund as state premier for Saxony-Anhalt on December 8 – but ultimately cannot force its five lawmakers to do so in what will be a secret ballot.

The softening of the BSW's position on the AfD has the backing of founder Wagenknecht and the party's lead candidate in Saxony-Anhalt, Claudia Wittig, who has slammed the policy of refusing to work with the AfD as "firewall idiocy."

"We're not cozying up to the AfD," she insisted, but said: "Let's not get tied up in knots over lies in the mainstream media."

Wagenknecht added: "Vibrant parties should have vibrant discussions about their future course, but we shouldn't accuse ourselves of things which aren't true. The BSW is not team AfD. Rather, we take voters seriously and want to implement political change to improve the lives of the majority."

But other founder members are resisting, writing in the Zeit newspaper this week that a "clear political distancing from the AfD" was a "foundational consensus" for the BSW.

"Just as it was right to reject the firewall, which only served the AfD anyway, so it is also wrong to make common cause with the AfD," they wrote, warning that the approach could morph into "political cooperation with AfD" which would "compromise the BSW and make it irrelevant in the long term."

They demanded: "No tolerance of an AfD government, no cooperation agreements, no abstentions from the vote for the state premier."

Germany's socialist Left Party (Die Linke) is on Friday hosting an international summit on femicide and violence against women.

The number of female victims of domestic violence in Germany in 2025 rose by 2.8% to 197,667.

#95. S&P considers Nvidia credit downgrade over governance and ‘key man’ risks

S&P Global Ratings, one of the three major credit rating agencies, is considering downgrading Nvidia’s rating, citing concerns about the company’s management and governance practices.

Despite Nvidia’s strong financial performance, the ouster and reinstatement of CEO Jensen Huang has raised concerns about “weak executive oversight and lack of succession planning” and “key man risk,” meaning heavy reliance on Huang for its future success, S&P said in a statement.

The agency expects to finish its review within 90 days and could cut Nvidia’s rating from AA to AA-, the fourth-highest on its scale.

Although Nvidia is certain to keep its investment-grade rating, a downgrade could raise its borrowing costs. Because Nvidia backstops neoclouds and other infrastructure companies that lack strong credit on their own, a lower rating could make their debt costlier or force them to post more collateral.

In August, Nvidia announced plans to arrange $500 billion in financing for AI infrastructure with six financial institutions, including Goldman Sachs, Apollo, and Blackstone. Under the original terms, Nvidia would guarantee 25% of the loans. But even before Huang’s ouster and return, lenders had been demanding that Nvidia guarantee more, Bloomberg reported.

A downgrade would likely prompt lenders to demand even larger guarantees.

#90. Grand Theft Auto VI proves why efficiency isn’t a virtue

It should have been impossible for “Grand Theft Auto VI” (GTA VI) to live up to the hype. Fans waited 13 years for the newest installment in the crime-adventure franchise, and Rockstar Games exceeded even those inconceivable expectations when the game finally came out last week.

GTA VI launched as the highest-rated game of all time on Metacritic and made $3.79 billion on release day to mark what could be the biggest entertainment event ever – more massive than “Avengers: Endgame” ($2.9 billion) or Taylor Swift’s Eras tour ($2 billion).

The 13-year wait certainly fueled the frenzy, and sales, around GTA VI, but some fans craved GTA VI because of what it represents in the emerging era of AI.

Even the biggest workaholics among us – startup founders – scheduled time off to play the game, including Spiry co-founder Dima Beseda, who told TechCrunch, “I reshuffled my workload to free up time around the weekends and even booked vitamin IV drips to keep my energy up.”

Yet some players felt a sense of anticipatory nostalgia as they wiped away a layer of dust on their Xbox to play the game they’d been looking forward to since they were kids.

“We do have a view that GTA is likely to be the last game of its kind to be made this way,” Ben Feder, managing partner at TIRTA Ventures and former CEO of Rockstar parent company Take-Two Interactive, told TechCrunch.

That’s because GTA VI was created entirely without generative AI.

“We’re kind of at this technological moment where this way of developing has sort of reached its natural frontier,” Feder said. “I don’t think you can say that games like this will always be around.”

As millions of people immersed themselves in Vice City, the tech industry witnessed another long-awaited moment: Anthropic became the first major AI lab to go public. At Nasdaq HQ, Anthropic executives gathered to celebrate the Claude maker’s $81.3 billion IPO. At virtually the same moment that Anthropic broke the record for the largest IPO ever, GTA VI was also breaking records with the highest-rated and best-selling game launch of all time.

But when the Anthropic team rang the bell on Thursday, the sound also signaled the end of an era in video game development, and perhaps even in the arts at large.

The AI industry operates with efficiency as its north star. The promise of this technology is that we will be able to automate so much work that we can expand the capabilities of humanity, avoiding needless time spent tinkering with ultimately fruitless ideas. Instead, a prompt goes into an LLM, and a response is spat out.

This technological revolution exploded into the world with some soul-crushing side effects: children are inundated with mind-numbing slop on YouTube, lawyers cite nonexistent cases in court, and tech companies like Meta lay off tens of thousands of people as part of a corporate-branded “Year of Efficiency.” But against the backdrop of the AI industry’s first major IPO, Rockstar has unveiled a game that about 5,000 people labored over for more than a decade.

The final product is breathtaking, looking so lifelike that it can be hard to distinguish gameplay footage from real life. These sprawling open-world games are usually made possible by procedurally generating new landscapes and objects, but Rockstar has taken the time to painstakingly craft every detail of the world, even developing 30,000 different nonplayable characters with unique appearances and personalities.

The video game industry is already grappling with the threat of AI. Google’s Project Genie, which launched earlier this year, allows users to create and wander around 3D worlds generated from prompts to an LLM. The stock prices of several video game companies faltered around the time of Project Genie’s release, perhaps because of investors’ uncertainty about the future relevance of video game developers.

“The notion that somehow new tools would allow an individual to push a button and generate a hit, market a hit, and bring it to many millions of consumers around the world, it’s a laughable notion,” Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick said at the time. “It’s just never been the case with entertainment.”

Zelnick’s fellow tech CEOs see different future, though. On a Meta earnings call this year, CEO Mark Zuckerberg promised that, “soon, you’ll have an AI that understands you and [can] even generate great personalized content for you.”

Elon Musk even went as far as proposing that a game like GTA VI could be made with AI.

“You won’t even have to ask,” Musk wrote on X. “AI will figure what video game you’d like best.”

This is the paradox of “Grandthropic” week. GTA VI is so impressively vast and captivating because its developers could make mistakes, experiment, and rework ideas, even if that meant delaying the game (twice). GTA VI was incubated with the exact inefficiencies that investors hope AI companies like Anthropic can help eradicate.

“Plenty of fantastic art — video games and elsewhere — almost by definition has to be created slowly, and there’s something to having that kind of inefficiency around it,” Feder said. “Creative output requires deletion, right? Getting to kind of a masterpiece oftentimes requires an enormous amount of deletion. And when you’re talking about a game of the kind of complexity we’re talking about, that kind of deletion ends up being very expensive, and it is by definition not efficient.”

GTA VI reeks of its own humanity – every small detail is loaded with intention, but it’s this impressive precision that keeps the world abuzz, triggering the kind of monocultural moment that has become increasingly rare in a media landscape that revolves around highly personalized social media algorithms.

The game forces the player to slow down, which is either frustrating or genius, depending on your sense of humor. People waited more than three presidential terms for the next installment in a high-octane action franchise, only to download a 212 GB behemoth of a game in which your earliest missions involve driving around doing errands and putting up drywall to make your girlfriend happy. Don’t worry — there are still car chases and shootouts — but in a moment when AI feels suffocating, GTA VI is such an artistic achievement that it is reaching audiences that don’t even like violent video games.

The ultimate fantasy of GTA VI is no longer to role-play as a criminal — the fantasy is just taking your time to relish the mundane and explore an expansive world that developers were given the time and space to perfect.

#88. A month with the iPhone Duo has changed how I use my phone

For years, the definition of a premium smartphone depended on its cameras, processors, and how light it felt when you stowed it in your pocket. The iPhone Duo challenges that concept by asking consumers to pay more for a fundamentally different smartphone experience, even if it means sacrificing what they’ve long thought of as high-end features.

In a little under a month of using the Duo, I’ve found that the trade-offs I was initially concerned about matter far less to me than I had expected. What’s been more interesting is how the Duo’s foldable form factor has changed my everyday habits, including how I interact with apps, which apps I use, and how I organize and personalize the phone itself.

I also easily forgot a number of my initial concerns about switching to the Duo. The apps, for the most part, are now optimized for the Duo; I’ve reacquainted myself with Touch ID; and I don’t miss the Pro Max camera on most days.

That’s not to say the Duo doesn’t have its shortcomings. I still miss that telephoto lens sometimes, and I’m still longing for Face ID — though not as much since Apple shipped a face unlock feature in the latest update. But after a month of using the Duo, I’ve found these compromises have had less of an impact on my day-to-day experience compared with the benefits that the new form factor brings.

I was surprised by how much I’ve grown to appreciate the Duo’s multitasking capabilities. Initially, I didn’t think I’d regularly use it to view two apps side by side, given the still fairly limited screen space even while the phone is unfolded, especially compared with an iPad. But I’ve found myself snapping apps to both halves of the screen more often than I had imagined.

For starters, Apple has made this side-by-side mode incredibly intuitive and easy to use — you simply drag the app over to the left or right side of the screen and drop it into the target box that appears.

I have regularly taken advantage of this feature to do all sorts of things.

I really enjoy keeping X open as I read through the news, which allows me to switch back and forth between the article I’m reading and real-time reactions to it. Sometimes I’ll keep email or Slack open on the side while I’m working on something else so I don’t miss an important message or notification.

And much like the overstimulated members of Gen Z (which I am not), I sometimes like scrolling social media while engaging with some other media off to the side.

Case in point: I passively watched Netflix’s “Love is Blind” reunion while reading the gossipy tweets and Reddit posts about the main characters on the other side of the screen. This sort of TV watching behavior used to be called second-screen viewing, but now it’s morphing into something new. Everything is happening on one screen, so should we call it “Duo screen viewing,” perhaps? It makes me long for even more interactive TV experiences where commentary and content are combined.

I’ve noticed another shift in my behavior since adopting the Duo as my main device. When I’m out and about, the Duo feels more like an (arguably heavier) iPhone mini. For those who still miss smaller iPhones, the Duo is the next best thing. Despite its weight, the phone feels small. You can cover it with one hand; you can type with a thumb; you can reach the top and bottom easily; and you can even fit it into your front and back pockets. The Pro Max now feels unbelievably strange in my hand — how was I ever comfortable wrangling this giant rectangle?

The Duo’s small screen works well when you’re on the go, especially for short interactions like responding to a quick text or pulling up directions to a destination. It’s not as great for mindless scrolling, which I appreciate, as it forces me to engage with the real world instead of killing every moment of boredom or unease by opening up apps.

But when you’re home or somewhere with time to kill, you can unfold the screen and enjoy your apps super-sized. It’s the best of both worlds.

I find that I instinctively unfold the phone when I’m preparing to watch videos, because the larger screen is best for that. It’s also unfolded if I’m shopping, as it’s more convenient for looking at products, zooming in to examine the details, or complete checkout forms.

Generally, I also unfold the Duo when I’m actively using apps. This setup has also allowed me to rethink my Home Screen layout: With the Duo closed, I prefer to see just widgets on the small screen with key information like weather, time, battery life, and so on. Meanwhile, the only app icons on the Home Screen are those in the dock.

In the weeks since its launch, most of the apps that weren’t yet optimized for the Duo have been upgraded to support its foldable form factor. In mid-November, Apple said there were over 200,000 Duo-ready apps, and in my experience, that certainly feels true. A majority of my so-called “daily drivers” — with the glaring exception of key Google apps like Gmail and Drive — have dropped the black band on the side of the screen that was frustrating early testers of the phone.

Most apps now take advantage of the available space, whether that’s on the Duo’s small front screen or the wider, unfolded one.

I’ve also found it interesting to see the different interpretations developers have had for how to best optimize their app for Duo.

Some have used the form factor as a way to bring in some sort of novelty: there’s a note-taking app that opens and closes like a notebook, with pages you can write on with the Apple Pencil; a retro Walkman-like music player; a tiny typewriter; a gaming handheld; a game where you close the phone to make grilled cheese.

As much as I love the whimsy of this group of apps, I appreciate how my everyday apps feel better to use on the unfolded screen. Spotify, at last, feels less cluttered when it has more screen real estate. A number of note-taking, list-making, and calendar apps also feel somehow more usable when spread out.

The Netflix app is actually fantastic with its support for multiple viewing modes, which include playing video on the closed outer display, the “laptop” orientation with its big player controls, and the “tent” mode.

Other developers have similarly reimagined how their app would work if it had originally been designed for the Duo’s small and larger screens.

One good example of this is Sofa, a handy utility for organizing the books, movies, TV shows, podcasts, albums, and games you want to check out. When the Duo is closed, its navigation is tucked off to the right side in a vertical bar, while the main screen focuses on the items you’ve saved. But when the Duo is unfolded, the number of items displayed on the screen also expands into a two-column layout, with more of your items featured on the screen and your lists on the left side. It doesn’t feel like two different apps, but like you’ve opened up the app itself and given it more space to spread out.

In terms of your daily social media scroll, apps like TikTok and Instagram have been updated to be perfectly usable on the Duo, whether the phone is open or closed. When closed, TikTok now stretches the video to cover the screen, instead of showing the ugly black bar. Scrolling short-form videos on the Duo, while unfolded like a slightly smaller iPad mini, has become my preferred way to watch.

Here’s another surprise: I’ve spent less time with my Kindle, and am instead reading books on my Duo. Unfolded, the Duo is a decent enough replacement for an e-book reader, albeit it can be a bit more stimulating as it’s not an e-ink device. However, the matte inner screen isn’t quite as bright and distracting as your typical glossy OLED display. There’s less glare and softer reflections, but there’s also something about the display that just feels calmer than a smartphone, allowing you to more fully engage with a book’s text.

Unfolded and held vertically, reading on the Duo feels a lot like reading on the standard Kindle, at least in terms of hand-feel. And putting your phone into the “Do Not Disturb” focus mode can help you not be distracted by incoming notifications and messages while you read.

You can also unfold the device while keeping it in landscape orientation, with the screens bent slightly inward like a real book. For even more fun, you can turn on Kindle’s page-turn animation, which makes it seem as if you’re turning the page in a physical book. And I love how you can “performatively read” using an app like Book2Life or Actually Read — they display the book’s cover on the Duo’s outer screen as you read the text on the inner screen.

E-book readers will likely find they can carry one less device when they’re out and about, because the Duo can stand in for whatever Kindle or other reader you typically keep in your purse or bag. You can save the Kindle for other times, whether that’s at home, on long travel days, or when you don’t want to worry about sand getting into your $2,000 phone.

On the big-screened iPhone 17 Pro Max I used to carry, I tended to keep a PopSocket of some sort on the back because the phone was both heavy and large, which made it hard to hold for longer periods of time. The Duo is similarly heavy, but its shape makes it far easier to hold in one hand without the extra help.

Folded, you can do the usual one-handed hold where the bottom edge of the phone rests on your pinky, your other three fingers cradle the back, and your thumb is free to navigate the screen.

Unfolded, the Duo’s like using a small Kindle or iPad mini, where two hands are required. You also don’t need a PopSocket for balancing the phone on the side, because it can be propped up like a tent or opened like a tiny laptop. Or, if you prefer, Apple and others offer Duo cases with a built-in stand. (Apple’s case comes in navy and taupe, though, so if you’re not boring, you’ll probably want to shop from third-party vendors).

If you think you still might need a PopSocket at times, or you just aren’t ready to give it up, the brand now makes a slimmer version: its Low-Pro Grip. There are other competitors, like the OhSnap grips, which aren’t a bad alternative either.

There’s something about the Duo that seems to have kicked off a new iPhone personalization trend, at least judging by what I’ve been seeing on social media.

Unlike a regular iPhone, where every phone case is simply a shell that offers a degree of protection based on the materials used, the Duo case lineup provides far more variety.

You can buy cases with and without built-in screen protectors, and some use adhesive to better stick to the outer screen, while others don’t. Some cases let you customize the band around the outer screen with a different color than the case that protects the rest of the phone.

Then you have cases that cover the hinge, while others leave it exposed. There are cases that offer holes where you can attach straps, wristlets, and charms. Some of those have attachment points at the bottom and others on the side.

Essentially, there are a lot of options for customizing how you want to carry the Duo and how you want to express your own style.

I’m also now starting to see whimsical cases that come with attached pens. (Thank goodness — most of the pen cases so far have been kind of plain!)

I also believe there’s something about the Duo that particularly appeals to women. Maybe because it’s small, or maybe it’s because you can attach a strap to its side and carry it like a little purse. Or maybe because, like a purse, you can deck it out with chains and charms — a choice that’s grown increasingly popular as young people have embraced whimsical, retro tech as a way to express their individuality and sense of fashion.

The Duo easily lends itself to this trend, perhaps even more than a regular iPhone. It becomes an accessory you want on display, not one buried in a bag.

Before switching to the Duo, I spent a lot of time thinking about what I’d be giving up by moving away from the Pro Max.

I’m surprised to find that, a month later, I only think about what I’d have to give up if I switched back to a traditional iPhone. It’s not just the extra space of the unfolded screen I’d miss — it’s all the little habits I’ve developed around having different screen sizes for different purposes.

The Duo makes me pause and think about how I want to use an app. That small moment of deliberation, before I even open the phone, has helped me break some of my more mindless smartphone habits, particularly around social media.

That’s not a benefit I could have anticipated if I’d judged the device by its specifications alone.

#84. Canada: Supreme Court largely upholds Quebec secularism bill

Quebec's government passed Bill 21 to ban religious symbols for public servants, arguing it was necessary to protect its secularist society. Critics argue the bill weakens legal protections for fundamental rights.

Canada's Supreme Court on Friday upheld Quebec's ban on religious symbols for public servants.

However, it ruled that provincial lawmakers should be allowed to wear religious symbols in the legislature, Quebec's National Assembly.

Quebec's government passed the bill, known as Bill 21, in 2019, arguing it was necessary to protect its secular society.

Under the law, anyone receiving public services like teachers and police officers has the right to do so without being exposed to overt religious symbols, including hijabs, turbans and yarmulkes.

Chief Justice Richard Wagner said that the law "is rooted in Quebec's legitimate and historical commitment to ensuring the (secularism) of the state in a free and democratic society."

Mathieu Levesque, the interim the right-wing Quebecois nationalist Future Quebec Coalition (CAQ) that passed the bill, hailed it as a "victory" for the French-speaking province.

"This is a victory for the Quebec nation and for [CAQ], whose actions have today been fully validated," he said.

CAQ ruled Quebec in two parliamentary terms from 2018 to 2026, after which it failed to enter parliament and the pro-independence center-left Parti Quebecois (PQ), which also supported Bill 21, formed government.

The case was seen as a major test of how far provinces can go in defying provisions of the federal constitution by invoking the "notwithstanding clause," which effectively allows provincial governments to pass laws that may breach Canada's constitution.

Quebec invoked the clause when passing the secularism bill, known as Bill 21, expecting it to be challenged over constitutional provisions guaranteeing freedom of religion.

Critics have argued that the bill unfairly targets Muslim women and weakens legal protections for fundamental rights.

"This decision is an unrestricted license for every government in this country to pass legislation taking away the fundamental freedoms and the legal rights of every Canadian ," Stephen Brown, CEO of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, was cited by Reuters news agency as saying.

He said the law forced many Muslim women to stop working as teachers or even prevented them from volunteering in schools.

Emmett Macfarlane, head of political science at the University of Waterloo, was cited by Reuters as saying that the law amounted to "Islamophobia as a state policy."

#79. Nigeria's 'ultimate hard man' who become an online meme

For generations of Nigerians, Pete Edochie was more than a celebrated actor.

He was Okonkwo â the fierce, proud and troubled warrior whose portrayal in Things Fall Apart made him a household name and earned him a place in the hearts of millions.

Even in death, many Nigerians still refer to him by the character's name, a testament to the enduring impact of a role that defined his career.

Edochie died on 25 November, aged 79, in the south-eastern Nigerian city of Enugu following a prolonged battle with prostate cancer.

In 1987, Edochie starred in a television adaptation of Chinua Achebe's acclaimed novel. His portrayal of Okonkwo would change his life, establishing him as one of Nigeria's most respected actors and bringing him international recognition.

The series depicted the struggles of an African community as British colonial rule and Christianity began to transform its traditional way of life.

Joy Nnamdi, a fan of both the novel and its television adaptation, believes Edochie was the perfect choice for the lead role.

She said nobody else could have brought the character to life as convincingly.

"His character Okonkwo gave a face to the highly celebrated novel," she told the BBC in Nigeria's biggest city, Lagos.

The role also cemented his reputation as a hard man, a screen persona he would maintain through much of his career.

"It was very rare to see him laugh in movies. He was the ultimate tough man," Nnamdi added.

With his deep, authoritative voice, imposing presence and effortless command of African proverbs, Edochie had a way of dominating a scene without raising his voice.

Whether playing a village elder, a wealthy businessman or a feared traditional ruler, he became an enduring embodiment of patriarchal authority on screen.

Edochie went on to become one of the most recognisable faces of Nollywood, Nigeria's film industry, which has grown into one of the world's largest.

From Kenya to South Africa, MultiChoice's Africa Magic channels helped take his films into millions of African homes as Nollywood became a cultural phenomenon across the continent.

Although many of his films were rooted in his Igbo culture, their themes of family loyalty, inheritance disputes, spiritual beliefs and the abuse of power resonated with audiences far beyond Nigeria.

Before becoming a household name in the film industry, however, Edochie had built a successful career in broadcasting.

Born on 7 March 1947 in Zaria, northern Nigeria, he attended primary and secondary school in Kaduna before studying journalism and television at the School of Journalism and Television in the UK.

He joined the Eastern Nigerian Broadcasting Corporation in 1967, the year Nigeria's civil war broke out.

He joined the pro-separatist Voice of Biafra, and recently described the war years as the greatest personal challenge he ever faced.

"During the war, the experience that we had as Biafrans, you know, survival was difficult. Hunger was driving us into eating all sorts of nonsense," he recalled.

He rose through the ranks to hold senior management positions but then switched to acting. Over a career spanning several decades, he appeared in numerous films, including Igodo, Rituals and Billionaire's Club.

Pete Edochie starred in Netflix's first original Nigerian film, Lionheart

His career also bridged the transition from home-video productions to global streaming platforms.

The 2018 romantic comedy Lionheart, which became Netflix's first original Nigerian film, introduced his work to a wider international audience.

He also worked in Ghana, where he starred alongside some of the country's biggest film stars in the 2017 comedy John and John.

His contribution to the arts was recognised in 2003 when he received one of Nigeria's national honours, the Member of the Order of the Niger (MON).

He also received several other accolades, including the Industry Merit Award at the Africa Magic Viewers' Choice Awards.

In recent years, Edochie found an unexpected new audience online.

His distinctive facial expressions, witty remarks and memorable scenes from his films became popular social media memes, introducing his work to a younger generation of Africans.

For many who had never watched Things Fall Apart or his early Nollywood films, his stern expressions became a humorous way of conveying disbelief, disapproval or exasperation at everyday situations.

But his reputation as a formidable screen presence was no joke to those who grew up watching him.

Sani Muazu recalls that parents in the northern Nigerian city of Kano would sometimes invoke Edochie's name to keep misbehaving children in check.

"They would tell you: 'Okonkwo will come and visit you if you don't stop' and we would begin acting right," he said.

"To be honest, we grew up scared of him. Even though he was on the screen and not physically present, some of his roles added to our fears because he played some popular occultic roles, and villain roles suited him."

Edochie's expressions were sometimes used as online memes

But beyond the fame and formidable character he portrayed on screen, his family remembers a man whose greatest legacy was the importance he placed on honesty and speaking the truth.

Leo Edochie, his son, told the BBC that his father was a strong-willed man.

"Of all his legacies, I think the whole world would agree that these two were the most important: telling the truth and remaining honest," he said.

#75. The Indian 'Tamasha queen' whose fans paid for a show that never happened

Vithabhai Narayangaonkar was a legend in Maharashtra's folk theatre

Few things could keep Vithabai Narayangaonkar, one of the greatest stars of Maharashtra's folk theatre, away from the stage.

Not even, it would seem, the birth of her child.

In the 1960s, the singer, dancer and troupe leader was performing in Shikhar Shingnapur, a village in the western Indian state, when she went into labour.

"She quietly stepped behind the stage into a makeshift tent while the rest of us continued performing," her daughter, veteran folk artist Mangala Bansode, told BBC Marathi.

Outside, the restless audience kept shouting: "Where is Vitha?"

When Vithabai returned to the stage, she was cradling her newborn son, the seventh of her eight children.

"The audience was stunned, but they quietly left, saying, 'We will watch your performance later'," Bansode recalled.

Outspoken and quick-witted, Vithabai held audiences across Maharashtra in thrall for decades, earning the title of Tamasha Samradnyi â the empress of Tamasha, the state's traditional travelling folk theatre.

Now, more than two decades after her death, her life has inspired a Bollywood musical, Eetha, starring Shraddha Kapoor, and due for release on 4 December.

Directed by Laxman Utekar, Eetha is based on Yogiraj Bagul's Marathi book chronicling Vithabai's life.

Rooted in devotional poetry, Tamasha evolved into a lively mix of music, dance, comedy and drama. At its heart is Lavani, a tradition that blends music and dance, with lyrics that can be bawdy or politically incisive, set to the rhythms of a double-headed drum called the dholki.

Eetha evokes the world of Tamasha in its heyday. Kapoor, who had never performed Lavani before, learnt to dance while wearing musical anklets weighing 4kg each.

"The lives of Tamasha artists have been very tough, so in their dance you see pain as well as joy," Kapoor recalled at a promotional event.

Many Tamasha performers, including Vithabai, came from oppressed castes, facing discrimination even as their artistry drew large crowds.

How much of that history Eetha will capture remains debated. Utekar says it is not a biopic, but his decision to rename the protagonist Vithabai Bhau Gavkar instead of Vithabai Bhau Mang Narayangaonkar has drawn criticism.

For critics, much of Vithabai's history was contained in her name â Mang identified her with a historically oppressed Dalit community, while Narayangaonkar spoke of her family's roots in Narayangaon village. Both featured in the name of the troupe she inherited from her father â Bhau-Bapu Mang Narayangaonkar Tamasha Mandal.

Vithabai spoke openly about caste and the indignities Tamasha artists faced, themes that also surfaced in her songs. In a 1960s Lavani documented by historian Shailaja Paik, she rebuked men for lewd gestures, reminding them she danced to earn a living, not to please them.

It was a livelihood she had inherited from generations of performers in her family.

Like her son, Vithabai was born during a Tamasha performance, in 1935. Her mother, Shanta, had continued touring with her husband Bhau's troupe throughout her pregnancy.

Vithabai joined a theatre group at eight and her father's troupe at 13, soon becoming its star. After her father and co-leader Bapu died in quick succession, she took over the troupe with her husband and cousin.

Her career flourished during Tamasha's golden age, when productions grew more elaborate, drawing on mythology, history and contemporary life, says folk arts scholar Sukhada Khandge.

Khandge, who watched Vithabai perform as a child, recalls her sparkling eyes and commanding presence. She was as expressive with her face as she was with her feet.

Vithabai was known as the empress of Tamasha in Maharashtra

During India's war with China in 1962, Vithabai performed for soldiers in the North-East Frontier Agency, now largely Arunachal Pradesh, braving freezing conditions just three months after giving birth, Bansode says.

At a conference in 1996, she recalled meeting soldiers from the historically oppressed Mahar and Mang communities at the frontier.

"Our Mahar-Mang soldiers were expected to fight and lay down their lives defending the nation, but the very same people would not even allow our bridegroom to pass through the village entrance," she said.

Despite offers from leading filmmakers, including Raj Kapoor, and roles in Marathi cinema, Vithabai refused to leave theatre. Film fame, she felt, would benefit her alone, while her troupe supported some 50 families, Bansode says.

There was a tenderness, too, in her relationship with audiences.

In his book on Vithabai's life, which inspired Eetha, Yogiraj Bagul recounts a performance in Mumbai during Diwali in 1971.

Despite the threat of rain, spectators insisted on buying tickets, saying the money would be their Bhau Beej gift to Vithabai if the show was cancelled â a festival celebrating the bond between brothers and sisters.

When the rain came, people left chanting: "Bhau Beej, Bhau Beej."

But Vithabai's fortunes failed to match her fame. Her husband, Maruti Sawant, mismanaged the troupe's finances and took loans in her name, family members say. By the time she left him in 1982, she was heavily indebted.

Bagul also writes about a 1986 column in a Marathi daily that revealed how the empress of Tamasha was now travelling on public buses and staying in cheap accommodation.

Despite drawing huge crowds, Lavni dancers were often disdained

Despite public support, Vithabai was forced to disband her troupe in 1990 â the same year she received the Sangeet Natak Akademi Award, one of India's highest performing arts honours.

Vithabai successfully campaigned for government pensions for Tamasha artists. But many of the hardships she knew persist today, says Marathi writer Pradnya Daya Pawar.

Troupes spend nearly eight months a year on the road, with performers struggling to feed their children and educate them, she says. Despite government aid, many remain indebted to private moneylenders.

Vithabai's financial struggles persisted until her death in January 2002. Her family could pay only 4,000 of the 5,900 rupees owed to the Pune hospital where she died, the Times of India reported.

Fellow artists and admirers chipped in to bring her body home to Narayangaon.

Maharashtra has since instituted a lifetime achievement award in her name.

Eetha could now introduce Vithabai to a generation that never saw her perform, though the changes to her name have left some uneasy.

Marathi writer Pragya Daya Pawar argues that omitting the reference to Vithabai's caste risks obscuring the difficulties she confronted.

"It erases the social location, struggle and resistance that shaped her remarkable journey, reducing an extraordinary artist's story to a purely individual achievement," she says.

But Bansode, who spent years performing alongside her mother, says there is much to celebrate.

"The name doesn't matter to me. I am simply proud that a film is being made about my mother," she says.

#71. A clinic is reassessing people with ADHD and autism diagnoses - but critics fear it could deepen stigma

Johanna, 24, began questioning her ADHD diagnosis when she discovered it could act as a barrier to becoming a police officer

Freya looks nervous as she walks into a psychiatric clinic in Sweden for an ADHD assessment.

Countries around the world have seen a huge increase in ADHD diagnoses over recent decades. For many people this can be life changing, helping them understand themselves better and access support.

But Freya (not her real name) is not attending a long-awaited appointment to secure a diagnosis.

This government-funded clinic offers autism and ADHD diagnosis reassessments - its doctors believe it is the first in the world dedicated solely to this purpose.

At 12, Freya was struggling with anxiety at school and sought out professional help. "They thought I had ADHD," she says.

But as she grew up, she felt that she did not experience ADHD symptoms. "I don't think I had it in the first place," she now says.

Her doubts about the diagnosis became more pressing as a teenager, when she discovered that in Sweden, it could be a barrier to pursuing her dream career of joining the police.

"I want to have every option open," she tells BBC World Service.

If the doctors at the clinic decide Freya does not meet the ADHD criteria, her medical records will be updated, and it will be easier for her to apply for a job with the police.

The clinic, known as Mottagning Neuropsykiatri, has opened at a time when rates of ADHD and autism diagnoses are under the spotlight. Many say this is because of greater awareness of the symptoms, and that previously neglected groups - particularly women and girls - are finally getting the diagnoses they need, while others argue that the growing numbers stem from overdiagnosis.

The existence of the clinic is controversial. Clinical guidance from UK governing bodies and the World Health Organization describe autism as a lifelong condition and ADHD as a neurodevelopmental condition that can persist into adulthood. The medical consensus is that both diagnoses last a lifetime, even if symptoms fluctuate.

And some neurodiversity advocates warn that a dedicated reassessment service could reinforce the idea that being diagnosed with ADHD or autism is undesirable â and deepen the stigma surrounding both conditions.

Despite this, hundreds of people have approached the clinic about reassessment.

Like Freya, other young adults have been prompted to reconsider their diagnosis after they felt it had become a barrier.

This is because historically, Swedes with ADHD or autism were barred from certain jobs, including joining the military, and becoming police officers, train drivers and pilots. This is despite research suggesting some neurodivergent traits can be strengths in certain roles. No such bans exist in the UK, although rules differ globally.

Since 2022, people with ADHD have been able to join the Swedish military if they are medication-free. And since 2021, people with ADHD or autism have been able to apply to join the police, but must submit a medical certificate, which can cost around 15,000 SEK (Â£1,100) privately.

Johanna was reassessed aged 21 during an early pilot of the reassessment clinic and was found not to meet its diagnostic criteria for ADHD. She now works for her father's company, and does not plan on becoming a police officer.

#68. Behind China's spectacular rise, voices who defied it say they are being drowned out

China's economic might is drowning out our voices, say activists and dissidents

For Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa, who has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades, the thinning of the crowds is the clearest measure of how much his cause is slipping off the world's radar.

"Back in 2018, we could easily get thousands of people out in the streets to protest against China's human rights violations. Nowadays, we have trouble finding even 500."

It was in 2018 that reports emerged that China had detained up to a million Uyghurs and other Muslims in mass internment camps in its north-western province of Xinjiang. Beijing, which said the camps were for "re-education" to counter separatist violence, denied the allegations detailing alarming rights abuses.

But an international outcry, including protests, followed. Western governments rolled out trade bans and sanctions against senior Chinese officials, and froze their assets, and at least nine countries issued parliamentary resolutions condemning the detentions.

It was a time "when we felt the world was finally listening", says Dolkun, 59, who served as president of the Munich-based World Uyghur Congress from 2017-2024 .

But today, "we find ourselves competing against economic interests and geopolitical calculations to capture the world's attention. For me, this is one of the most painful aspects of our struggle".

Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades

Few corners of the world remain untouched by China's rise. Its electric vehicles pack highways from Brazil to Belgium, made-in-China batteries, solar panels, AI models and microdramas fuel daily consumption in so many places, and Labubu dolls, Pop Mart's furry gap-toothed plush, dangle from backpacks almost everywhere.

China's immense economic clout now also has the flex of soft power, drowning out voices that say their fight against the Communist Party cannot compete with the country's gleaming skylines, advanced exports or futuristic ambitions.

Buoyed by its successes, Beijing is also swapping the "stick" for the "carrot", says Uyghur journalist Arslan Hidayat, who was born and raised in Australia, where he runs Kashgar Times.

Activists tell the BBC Beijing has been offering family reunions, money and the promise of safety - in return, they are asked for information about their networks and to promote pro-party narratives. The result, activists say, is a movement with fewer voices and less trust.

The Chinese embassy in the UK dismissed the activists' claims that they would be asked to spy on or disclose information about fellow exiles.

Shenzhen, a rich, buzzing city on the south-eastern coast, has emerged as one of China's star attractions for tech and tourism

China's "economic muscle" has earned it respect abroad and reshaped foreign policy, says Ian Johnson, a Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist who has long reported on Chinese civil society.

"At the same time, Western governments have become much more inwardly focused and populist, pushing human rights down their lists of priorities."

China's human rights record has not earned a mention in any of the three summits Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump held this year, including the one in Shenzhen last week. As Beijing's leverage on the global stage has grown, so has the cost of confrontation or condemnation.

This shift in Beijing's image and tactics is deeply worrying, dissidents say, because it is weakening both their networks and the international pressure they hope to build.

In July Tibetan activist Lobga Rangzen died after he set himself on fire outside the UN headquarters in New York. He was protesting against China's sweeping new "ethnic unity law" which, among other things, requires minorities, including Tibetans, to learn Mandarin. The UN called it an "absolutely tragic" incident, but attention quickly faded.

His death was "an appeal to the international community", says Tenzin Dorjee, a researcher at the Tibet Action Institute, but he thinks much of the world is reluctant to criticise China because of the power it wields.

That same month Dolkun tried to rally supporters against the same law but he met with a muted response.

"Many people have dialled down on their activism over the years because they are thinking about their loved ones back in China," Dolkun says.

The questions seem "fairly innocuous", he adds: "Who lives around you? Which Uyghur restaurants do you frequent? We believe these are used to build out the profiles of dissidents overseas."

Beijing's embassy in the UK said in its response to the BBC that "facilitating visits to China by overseas Chinese to reunite with their families" is a "normal part" of consular services.

"China respects and protects the lawful rights and interests of people of all ethnic groups, including the Uyghurs, and their freedom to maintain contact with family members overseas," it added.

These family visits often happen in large groups and are well documented on China's official platforms.

In Turkey, which hosts tens of thousands of Muslim minorities from Xinjiang, Chinese Consul-General Wei Xiaodong was photographed sending off some 30 participants from Istanbul in January.

Consul-General Wei Xiaodong (in the red scarf) pictured sending people off in January

"He expressed hope that the diaspora returning to Xinjiang would experience the region's development... and would actively promote these developments upon their return to Turkey," read a post on the consulate's WeChat page.

Beijing has repackaged Xinjiang into a traveller's haven. The region saw more than 300 million domestic and international tourist visits last year, more than double the number in 2018, Chinese authorities say.

The post from China's Turkish consulate concluded with a clip showing loved ones hugging and presenting bouquets as they reunite with family at the airport in Urumqi, the capital of Xinjiang.

"People are happy to stay quiet and lay low for the little communication they've been allowed to establish with their loved ones," Arslan says.

Though the trips are publicised, it is unclear what makes someone eligible to participate in them. Some suggest that even dating histories come under the microscope.

Alim*, a Uyghur exiled in Turkey, says a friend who made an appeal to the consulate to consider her for a trip was rejected, with staffers alluding to her past relationship with a Kazakh man who at one point was "quite active" in lobbying for his parents who were detained in China.

"She was very eager to go on one of these 'family visits' because she hasn't seen her loved ones back home in so many years... She was crushed that she was rejected," says Alim.

"The way I understand it, the 'ideal participant' is someone influential enough to amplify China's messages, yet not associated with anything or anyone too political."

Xinjiang has been sold as an exotic destination, bringing in huge numbers of tourists

The Uyghur businessman hoped a trip back to China would help him re-establish the links needed to "keep our business afloat". Far from the warm reunions Mehmut had expected, he says his engagement with friends and family "felt superficial".

"It was not like officials were actively monitoring me or restricting my movements. But there was a great dose of fear and self-censorship in my conversations with people," he says.

"Some were caught off guard to see me return, others appeared very cautious with what they told me. The experience just made me feel very uncomfortable."

These visits do not just serve Beijing's narrative; they also drive a wedge within communities, activists say.

Abduweli Ayup, a prominent Uyghur linguist based in Norway, has observed former activists going off the grid after returning from these trips.

"They get sidelined when they return... Very sadly, our suspicions that some of them are feeding information back to the Chinese authorities have turned out to be true."

#65. Weary of war but with no sign of backing down, Kyiv faces another punishing winter

Weary of war but with no sign of backing down, Kyiv faces another punishing winter

Something weird happens when you reach Kyiv after the eight-hour drive from the Polish border: the clock on your mobile phone goes to midnight. It's not really for phones - it seems to be part of a wider strategy to disorient the missiles and drones the Russians are sending over.

Aside from little things like that, though, Kyiv seems fairly normal at first sight. You become well aware there's a war on when sirens begin to wail and people check their phones for the notifications which tell them there's a red alert and missiles are on their way. You can even buy gadgets that show you which direction the drones are coming from, and what type they are.

The drones and ballistic missiles have been hitting in sizeable numbers over the last couple of weeks. On the morning of Monday 16 November, the battle of the bridges began. Ukraine hit the Kerch bridge which links Russia to illegally occupied Crimea. Then it launched a bigger wave of missiles and drones, damaging the central road-bridge arch. A few hours later, presumably in revenge, Russia fired seven ballistic missiles at Kyiv, disabling two of the nine bridges that cross the River Dnipro.

The war has dragged on for more than four and a half years, killing what some estimate could be 100,000 Ukrainian troops and 18,000 Ukrainian civilians, according to the United Nations, and probably more. Its infrastructure, too, has been battered, as well as its morale. While temperatures drop to freezing at night, and with Ukrainians regularly suffering from no light, heat or water thanks to Russian strikes, can the country survive another difficult winter under bombardment? And what would a peace deal, if one could be struck, look like?

Kyiv is mostly dark at night, thanks to Russia's campaign of targeting Ukraine's electricity generating capability. In the blackness last week, the air raid sirens started wailing across the city, and our hotel's warning announcements came over the loudspeakers. Within a few minutes, we heard the first explosions. I jumped out of bed and stared out of the window into the darkness. After a few seconds two more missiles landed, lighting up the entire sky momentarily in a blaze of white and red. They were uncomfortably close.

Many people prefer to go to the shelters when there's a red alert. Others just stay in bed and hope the law of averages will protect them. It usually does. During one of the heaviest attacks last week, the number of fatalities was in single figures.

I went to the shelter in our hotel one night. The clearly audible thumps and crashes above ground, the permanent lighting, the snores and grunts of other people, and the attentions of the little biting flies that seem to thrive in the warm, crowded shelters made sleep hard.

Kyiv is entering its fourth winter following Russia's full-scale invasion

To the people who live here in Kyiv, it's frightening, certainly, if a drone or a missile hits close by, but enraging too. Over the years I've been in several cities that were being bombed by high-tech weaponry and the reaction always seems the same, whether it's Lebanon, Iraq, Serbia, or Ukraine: a combination of weariness and fury. In Kyiv the attacks come by night and day, making normal life almost impossible. An uninterrupted night's sleep is a rarity. The morning after the bridges were hit, people in the streets looked tired and worn.

Yet although they long for it to stop, there's no sign they want Ukraine to surrender. On the contrary, just about everyone I spoke to felt they had to keep fighting. That's borne out by an opinion poll last month by the KIIS organisation, a Ukrainian market research company, which found that 62% of people were prepared to put up with the war for as long as it took. Of those polled, 79% believed that Ukraine would win the war in the long run, and only 7% thought Russia would win. These aren't the reactions of a country which is being beaten to its knees.

I don't have any doubt that Ukraine can survive the winter. Food is plentiful, though prices are high. Water is a problem, because the regular attacks on the power grid mean that it can't be pumped with any regularity. Kyiv underwent a large transformation under the Soviets, so there are large numbers of high-rise blocks of flats everywhere. Labouring up and down the stairs when there's no power is daunting, especially when you've got to carry quantities of water home.

The moment a red alert showed, it was his duty to shepherd everyone off the set to a nearby shelter. "People see our cuisine as a way of expressing their national identity," he said. It's one of the reasons the programme is so popular.

There are other ways in which people express their Ukrainian national identity under the bombs. There has been a boom for books being written and published about Ukraine and Ukrainian identity. Ukrainian and Russian are close as languages, though vocabulary and pronunciation differ. Nowadays the differences are emphasised. "Woe betide a writer who uses Russian spellings," a Ukrainian novelist told me.

The war will end at some point; wars always do. But there has to be a clear winner before anyone backs down, and a lasting peace can be negotiated. US President Donald Trump and his advisers, people like Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, with their business backgrounds, have approached the situation as though it's a form of mergers and acquisitions: make a deal attractive enough, and the other side will accept it.

So, last week, Washington was rumoured to be offering a very Trumpian deal, that Ukraine should accept the loss of the land in the east which the Russians now control, and in return it was rumoured it could join Nato. It was a non-starter, because Russia would never accept it. The sweetener, in this US plan, was that the West's sanctions against Russia would be lifted, and both sides would benefit from unrestricted trading once again.

But neither side sees this as being about trade and profit. For Russia, it's about the dangers of being humiliated if it doesn't win. President Vladimir Putin has made it his personal war. By illegally taking Crimea from Ukraine in 2014, he made himself very popular in Russia. If he doesn't win this second stage of a 12-year campaign against Ukraine, the consequences for him personally could be disastrous. He's already had to face down one attempt against his rule: his former associate Yevgeny Prigozhin's march on Moscow in June 2023. In a much-quoted remark, reported last August, Putin is said to have confided to his associates that if he didn't win the war "they'll hang me". My sense is he's not concerned about trade with the West and the profit that will bring, so much as about his personal survival.

Similarly, Ukrainians aren't interested in the precise details that have leaked out of the various negotiations between the Americans and Russians. Their contempt for the US is immense; "they just failed us," said one Ukrainian I spoke to.

And they don't believe Russia will stick to any future peace agreement anyway. "They signed a treaty to accept our borders, and Putin broke it," a young man told me as he sat hunched over his laptop in a bookshop. "The only solution is to defeat them."

Tens of thousands of soldiers have died since the outbreak of war

Other people talked about the need to get huge reparations from Russia for the terrible destruction it has visited on their country. According to one estimate, reconstruction and recovery will cost more than $500bn (Â£383bn), an unthinkably huge amount for a country whose GDP was $200bn before the war. President Trump and his advisers don't seem to have addressed any of these issues in their negotiations.

Here in Kyiv, peace doesn't seem at all close. Russia's efforts to make life intolerable for Ukraine's citizens are certainly having a major effect. The war is often frightening for ordinary people, and it can be appallingly destructive of property and lives. But short of hugely escalating by attacking Ukraine with nuclear weapons, which is something China and the US are both thought to have warned him not to do, Putin can't seem to strike the knock-out blow against Ukraine that he needs.

At present it seems to people in Ukraine that the war will never come to an end. Yet it will, at some point: and then the crucial requirement will be to work out ways to stop Russia coming back for another round. One way would be if Ukraine joins Nato, but that's something Russia would only accept if it had been comprehensively defeated. Ukrainians might think that can happen, but most Western onlookers are more sceptical.

The daily and nightly sirens, the explosions of missiles and drones, the shortages and the fear and the darkness are all terrible. But they aren't enough to destroy Ukraine's will to continue fighting.
