Tyner was based at Imjin Barracks in Innsworth, Gloucestershire, the headquarters of Nato's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps.

British police previously said he was being held in London after he was arrested under section one and section three of the National Security Act 2023, which relate to obtaining or disclosing protected information, and assisting a foreign intelligence service.

The day after Tyner's arrest on 10 November, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he was "unaware" of what it was connected to.

"We have nothing to do with it, and it has nothing to do with us," he told reporters in Moscow.

Tyner's arrest was the result of a long-running operation by MI5 - the UK's domestic intelligence and security agency - in collaboration with the FBI, the BBC understands.

He had previously served in Afghanistan and Iraq, but at the time of his arrest was working with NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps near Gloucester - where he was said to be entrusted with sensitive information which would have been extremely damaging if leaked to Russia.

#33. European-made pesticides named in 1,605 poisoning reports in Brazil last year

About a third of cases involved chemicals banned for use on EU farms, according to investigation by Unearthed and Public Eye

Pesticides sold by European chemical companies were named in more than 1,600 poisoning reports in Brazil last year, an investigation has found.

About a third of the cases involved chemicals banned from use on farms in the EU because of the risks they pose to people and the environment. But European companies are not banned from manufacturing these chemicals, as long as they export them overseas to countries where they are legally allowed to be used.

Analysis of Brazilian health ministry data by Unearthed and Public Eye in a report published on Tuesday found that of 2,857 poisoning reports that named an identifiable agricultural product, 1,605 named brands sold by European pesticide companies.

Farm workers in Brazil have described their flesh rotting, skin burning, difficulty breathing and vomiting after using the chemicals at work, according to the report. A small number have died accidentally or killed themselves by drinking the pesticides.

Under Brazilian law, reports must be submitted by healthcare professionals to Brazil’s national disease notification system in all suspected pesticide poisoning cases. These reports can be marked “confirmed” poisoning or “exposure” only by the doctor or nurse who saw the patient.

Clinicians sometimes record cases as exposure when they lack the training or support to confirm a diagnosis, but are presented with a patient who says they have been exposed to pesticides or present symptoms of pesticide poisoning. Laboratory confirmation is rare, so recording a diagnosis of “confirmed” poisoning usually depends on clinical judgment.

Experts say clinicians can be reluctant to confirm cases because of a lack of training or support. A brand being named in a poisoning report is not proof that it caused poisoning or death.

“The insufficient training of healthcare professionals to recognise and manage these cases is a major limiting factor,” said Vanda Garibotti, a public health biologist at the State Centre for Health Surveillance in Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil.

High staff turnover and a lack of institutional support meant poisoning cases were rarely confirmed through laboratory tests and often relied on clinical judgment, Garibotti added.

In more than 6,700 medical reports involving agricultural pesticides last year, 2,857 named an identifiable product. Of those, 1,420 were classified as confirmed poisonings. A further 1,108 were classified as exposures; for the remainder the classification was unknown or other diagnoses.

Some of the products are made in Britain. Reglone is the most frequently cited UK-made weedkiller. It contains diquat, a chemical that was banned in the UK in 2020 after experts concluded it posed a high risk to people living near fields where it was sprayed. Diquat is also found in other pesticides.

Despite the usage ban, Reglone is still manufactured by the Swiss-headquartered pesticides company Syngenta at its factory in Huddersfield for export to countries with more permissive regulations, including Brazil.

Nelson Castoldi died last year after accidentally ingesting Reglone on his family farm in Rio Grande do Sul. To save money, his family had bought the weedkiller in bulk, decanting a single litre into an empty cola bottle to avoid hauling a heavy 20-litre canister into the fields.

Working under the scorching sun, Castoldi mistook the black chemical for a soft drink and took a sip. Though he spat it out immediately, the chemical caused his kidneys to fail before attacking his lungs. He died two days later.

“We fell apart,” Ivan Castoldi said of his father’s death. “Four months after our father passed away, our mother also passed away. She cried day and night. She became depressive and had a stroke. She was left alone.

“That poison, Reglone, should be banned in Brazil,” he added. “Not because of what happened to us, but because of its toxicity.”

The Brazilian health ministry recorded 75 cases involving diquat last year, compared with 36 over the five years from 2018 to 2022.

The investigation found Reglone was specifically named in 44 reports, including four deaths. Of those cases, 26 were classified as confirmed poisonings and the rest were classified as exposure, unknown, or adverse reaction.

Dormex, a pesticide manufactured in Germany by Alzchem and distributed in Brazil by the German chemical company BASF, is another product workers said injured them or made them ill.

Sidnei Souza de Oliveira, 49, suffered a severe injury after a jug of Dormex spilled on his leg while he was filling a sprayer. The handle broke as he was transferring the chemical into the tank. At first, his leg turned red, and he continued spraying.

He carried on working for four days before going to hospital. “After 15 days, my leg was black. Chunks of flesh were falling off,” Oliveira said. A doctor said the injury was serious and had to remove dead tissue to stop it spreading towards the bone.

Oliveira said the protective trousers supplied by his employer were thin and allowed the chemical to seep through. He said he had been afraid of losing his job if he stayed away from work for too long.

Dormex contains cyanamide, which was banned from use in the EU in 2008 because of concerns about its effects on human health, particularly among farm workers spraying the chemical. Analysis shows the Brazilian health ministry database recorded 17 confirmed poisonings involving Dormex last year.

Finale, a BASF product identified in the investigation, contains glufosinate, which was banned in the EU in 2018. Roundup – a glyphosate-based weedkiller manufactured by the German chemical company Bayer – was frequently cited in Brazilian poisoning reports, including 12 deaths.

Across all the poisoning reports identified by the investigation as involving products sold by European companies, there were 32 reported deaths. Of these cases, 31 were classified as confirmed poisonings, and 26 were recorded as suicides.

Syngenta products were cited 425 times in the poisoning reports, and BASF 179 times. The company figures are not mutually exclusive: some poisoning reports named products sold by more than one manufacturer.

Nina Schrank, a senior campaigner at Greenpeace UK, said the findings were a “deadly double standard”.

“If a pesticide is too dangerous to use in Britain, it should be too dangerous to export. Ministers must stop allowing Britain to be part of a production line for toxic chemicals that are putting lives at risk overseas.”

The EU’s pesticide approval system requires chemicals to meet strict standards for human and animal health and the environment. Diquat has been banned on EU and UK farms since 2019 and 2020 respectively, after experts concluded it posed a high risk to workers, people living nearby and birds. Cyanamide was withdrawn after an assessment found exposure among agricultural operators could exceed safe levels. Glufosinate, meanwhile, is classified in the EU as toxic to reproduction, meaning it can harm fertility or the development of unborn children.

Suicide prevention is also a factor. A 2020 study found national bans on highly hazardous pesticides could be a cost-effective intervention for reducing the number of people who kill themselves in countries with a high burden of suicides attributable to pesticides.

A Syngenta spokesperson said almost half the Brazilian cases attributed to the company were not classified as confirmed poisonings, adding: “Public Eye and Unearthed are aware of this but have explicitly chosen to disregard the dataset’s final classification.” The company accused the investigation of disregarding the dataset’s final classifications and identified “numerous other inconsistencies” that it said raised “serious concerns” that the evidence was “being selectively interpreted to support a predetermined conclusion”.

“There are real people behind these statistics. Every genuine case of poisoning matters, and every family affected deserves our concern,” the spokesperson added.

Syngenta said Brazil had its own “robust regulatory system” and that products banned in Europe could still be independently assessed and legally registered in the country, citing differences in crops, growing conditions and pest pressures. It said it trained 95,000 people in Brazil in the safe use of its products in 2025, including through free online courses.

Bayer said its products were safe when used according to label instructions, citing what it described as rigorous testing and assessments by global health authorities. The company said it took pesticide poisoning seriously but questioned the reliability of the data, arguing that products and circumstances of exposure had not been independently verified, some chemicals may have been incorrectly identified and symptoms such as headaches and skin irritation could have other causes.

It said “Roundup” was “widely used as a generic term” that could refer to weedkillers made by other manufacturers or counterfeit products.

The company said it provided training on the safe handling and use of pesticides, including protective equipment, and that its programmes reached more than 4 million people globally in 2025. It also argued that a pesticide being banned in the EU did not necessarily mean it was unsafe, saying other countries had robust regulatory systems that took account of local agricultural conditions.

It added: “While the data presented helps to provide information on broad trends of exposure, the data has limitations and warrants caution when drawing conclusions, particularly when looking at individual cases.”

BASF said it was deeply concerned by reports of poisoning that might be linked to its products, adding that it took all such incidents seriously.

A spokesperson said publicly available information suggested some incidents might have involved the intentional ingestion of crop protection products, describing such acts as “deliberate misuse” of products intended exclusively for agricultural use.

BASF said it had global and local procedures to assess and investigate incidents potentially involving its products, and worked with authorities and experts where appropriate to understand cases and identify ways to prevent similar incidents. It said it invested in education and training to promote the safe handling and use of its products, with initiatives in Brazil reaching more than 14,475 participants in 2025.

The company said it was confident in the safety of its products when used in accordance with label instructions and applicable regulatory standards.

It added that Brazilian law required crop protection products to be handled and applied only by authorised and properly trained individuals, and said it took its responsibility for ensuring their safe use seriously.

#30. US loses measles elimination status after 26 years amid record case numbers

BBC's Madeline Halpert explains why the US lost its measles elimination status and what it could mean.

After more than two decades, the US has lost its measles-free status, following two years of record case numbers and deaths.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), a specialised service of the United Nations, determined the US has had uninterrupted transmission of the same strain of measles for more than 12 months.

Dr Jarbas Barbosa, director of PAHO, announced that Mexico and Bolivia have also lost their measles elimination status, attributing the resurgence in the region to persistent "gaps in vaccination".

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) said it was committed to "regaining measles elimination status" by expanding access to vaccines.

The US has seen 5,400 cases of measles this year alone, with 10 measles-related deaths, the most the country has seen since the 1990s.

The measles virus - with its signature blotchy red rash - used to infect millions of children a year before a vaccine was invented. The extremely contagious virus can be fatal, and some patients can experience long-term complications including encephalitis and immune amnesia, which resets the immune system, leaving it with only limited ability to respond to new infections.

The US recommends two measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shots for children between ages one and six, which is 97% effective in protecting against the illness.

PAHO, a group within the World Health Organization, revoked the US's measles-elimination after determining that the virus had circulated nonstop for more than a year.

"These figures are a serious warning for our region," PAHO's director Barbosa said at a news conference on Tuesday. "Measles is resurging globally amid persistent gaps in vaccination."

He added that Canada, which lost its status of having eliminated measles last year after seeing several large outbreaks, has made strides this year to interrupt transmission.

Daniel Salas, the executive manager of immunisations at PAHO, said that the US and others who lost elimination status would be required to submit corrective action plans outlining how they would attempt to improve vaccination rates.

US Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr Kennedy has attempted to make a series of sweeping changes to US vaccine recommendations and policies. The vaccine sceptic has also frequently cast doubt on the safety of MMR shot, as vaccination rates have declined across the US in recent years.

Kennedy has argued that the rise in measles cases started before he took the helm at HHS and that neighbouring countries have also seen more infections.

Responding to Tuesday's announcement, the CDC said the agency and its partners were "committed to stopping measles outbreaks, protecting Americans, and regaining measles elimination status".

"Together, we will encourage MMR vaccination, expand access to vaccines, and help communities close gaps in protection," the agency said.

CDC Director Erica Schwartz said the measles elimination designation was not what protected Americans.

"Vaccination does," she said. "We know where transmission is occurring and we've invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities."

In a post on social media, Kennedy said: "I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles."

"We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust," he also said. "We will answer families' questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment."

The UK, Spain and Austria were among European nations that lost their measles-free status earlier this year, along with countries including Russia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan.

Nuwan Gunawardhana, an assistant professor of medicine with Columbia University Irving Medical Center, said the US losing its elimination status was "a huge leap backwards".

The US was the first country to eliminate measles in the Americas thanks to vaccination campaigns, noted William Moss, a paediatric infectious disease specialist at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

Dr Gunawardhana said it took massive efforts from public health officials and clinicians to build trust with patients to increase uptake of the MMR vaccine, leading to decades in the US with few infections and deaths.

But that trust has been steadily eroding since the Covid-19 pandemic, when Dr Gunawardhana said patients were confronted with misinformation about the safety of vaccines and lost even more trust in public health systems.

The vaccination rate for MMR across the US has fallen to 92.4%, below the 95% level that provides herd immunity, which limits the spread of measles and protects those who cannot get vaccinated, including newborns.

An outbreak in Pennsylvania has infected over 1,400 people - likely a large underestimate - and killed seven people, according to state officials.

Kennedy, the health secretary, has sparred with Pennsylvania officials over how it is counting the measles deaths, with CDC declining for now to record some of the deaths in its database.

Last year, the health secretary fired all the members of a top vaccine advisory panel and replaced them with his own appointees, some of whom were vaccine critics.

The panel recommended a number of vaccine changes - including no longer requiring the Hepatitis B vaccine for all newborns - but those recommendations were blocked by a federal judge.

President Donald Trump this year also cast doubt on the safety of the MMR vaccine, issuing an executive order for the shot to be split into three separate immunisations for measles, mumps and rubella, a move public health experts say is costly and could sow confusion about an already safe vaccine.

Doctors and public health officials say the misinformation from Kennedy and Trump has played a major role in falling vaccination rates.

"It's confusing patients. It's confusing families," Dr Gunawardhana said. "We need a clear message from the top."

The federal government's vaccine manoeuvres have left public health officials and states to fill in the gaps, offering their own separate recommendations upholding decades-long practices.

The village they entered was within the Arariboia Indigenous territory in the north-eastern state of Maranhão, home to the Guajajara ethnic group.

Local community leaders said the villagers offered food to the group.

“They asked for yams and took them back into their forest,” said Survival International.

A video of the encounter, filmed by Guajajara villagers and released by Survival International, shows a group of men, women and children at the edge of the forest.

#18. Caribbean nations fear economic impact of shuttering citizenship program after EU threats

St Kitts and Nevis to wind down citizenship-by-investment program after EU warns of end to visa-free access

The decision by St Kitts and Nevis to end a lucrative programme offering citizenship to wealthy foreigners in exchange for investment threatens its economy and ability to support people in need, the premier of the island of Nevis has warned.

On Friday, Terrance Drew, the prime minister of the twin-island Caribbean nation, announced he would wind down the citizenship-by-investment (CBI) programme, after the European Union threatened to end visa-free access for St Kitts and Nevis.

Mark Brantley, the premier of Nevis, which has autonomous self-governance in some aspects, said he understood that the “country was given very little choice”, but did not “support the winding down of the program”.

The 42-year-old CBI programme, which St Kitts and Nevis pioneered, allows wealthy foreigners to obtain citizenship and ultimately a passport by investing in infrastructure and other development projects. It has brought billions of dollars into the country and in some years accounted for the largest share of government revenue.

In June, the European Commission wrote to the five Caribbean nations that currently operate CBI programmes: St Kitts and Nevis, Dominica, Antigua and Barbuda, Grenada and St Lucia. It warned that countries may lose visa-free access to its 29-country Schengen area if they did not phase out CBI.

In Friday’s statement, Drew blamed concerns over the programme on mismanagement by previous administrations, including years of underselling to undercut other countries, a controversial prison project, weak escrow protections and investment sums not being properly paid into the citizenship unit.

He said his government had led major reforms to the Caribbean’s programmes with international partners, including the US treasury, but argued that continuing the programme risked visa-free travel to parts of Europe and access to international banking. That could disrupt services allowing visitors to use overseas cards and families abroad to send money home.

Brantley, however, argued it was unfair for the EU to force St Kitts and Nevis to make a move without offering assistance to navigate the transition to other industries.

“I have not yet seen from the EU any corresponding suggestions of assistance. And we have to realise that these citizenship-by-investment programs, not only in St Kitts and Nevis, but … smaller countries of the Caribbean, have contributed significantly to our gross domestic product and also to ensuring that our people … can get adequate access to water and to education, to healthcare and the like,” he said.

Brantley described the 1 June 2028 deadline the EU gave the country to phase out the lucrative programme as “frankly ludicrous”.

In his statement, Drew said the CBI programme “did a lot of good, but it also created a danger”.

“When one source pays for half or more of everything, you are at its mercy. If it stops, everything stops. That is putting all of your eggs in one basket,” he added.

The prime minister said the government aimed to diversify its economy by strengthening tourism, yachting, and digital and creative industries, among others. He added that the sale of clean, geothermal energy was “at the heart of it” with drilling and exploration already planned.

“I will not pretend that the road is easy. Less CBI money means we must spend wisely and plan carefully. We will. Our plan is to make this change by growing our economy. Raising taxes is not part of that plan,” Drew said.

Antigua and Barbuda has also expressed willingness to end the programme, though it is trying to negotiate a longer transition period. The five Caribbean governments have taken differing positions on the EU demand. According to local media, Grenada has indicated it wants its programme to continue until 2032, while Dominica and St Lucia have responded to Brussels without publicly committing to ending their programmes.

#5. How the attack on Crimean Bridge unfolded

Footage shows Ukrainian missile approach Crimea bridge

Stretching across the Kerch Strait in the Black Sea, the Crimean Bridge was always a symbol: for Russia, a prestige project cementing its claim to sovereign Ukrainian territory, and for Ukraine, a hated piece of infrastructure emblematic of Moscow's occupation of the Crimean Peninsula.

A key section of the double-arched, 12-mile-long bridge now lies on the seabed, brought down by an arsenal of Ukrainian long-range weaponry in a seven-stage operation culminating in a dramatic collapse.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov has said that the bridge "will be rebuilt, and it will be even better than before".

But eight years after its unveiling, the bridge has now become material proof of Kyiv's ability to pierce through Moscow's military defences to hit even its most treasured and protected structures.

The attack on the strategic Crimean Bridge was carried out gradually to cut across several layers of defence, Kyiv confirmed on Tuesday.

For several weeks Ukraine's Security Service and its General Staff gradually destroyed air defence systems, radars, electronic warfare assets, and other designated targets, President Volodymyr Zelensky said.

A significant strike had already been reported on 16 November, when drones hit the bridge at night. Traffic was paused briefly then reinstated, and although the arch over the road section was visibly damaged, the structure remained standing.

But that hit turned out to have been only the penultimate stage of a complex attack.

In the early hours of 17 November, the final strikes dealt a fatal blow to the weakened arch structure.

One verified video - filmed from a camera on board a Ukrainian-made Flamingo missile - shows the weapon approaching the main span of the bridge and heading towards the steel arches, with the footage freezing just before impact.

The Flamingo had potentially been modified to be controlled remotely, which improved its accuracy enough to strike the bridge supports, munitions expert Justin Bronk, from the Royal United Services Institute said.

The missile's "very heavy warhead" made it "more suitable" for attacking the bridge's reinforced concrete spans and supports, Bronk added.

Zelensky later confirmed that several types of missiles and drones had been employed in the attack, which led the massive road arch - weighing thousands of tonnes and measuring about 227m in length - to collapse into the strait.

Daylight images taken from the shores of Crimea to the north show a section about 200m (660ft) long of the tarmac was too dragged below into the sea.

This is clearly critical damage to the crossing. Far from just being a decorative feature of the bridge, the arch played a structural role, transferring loads and supporting the span and helping to ensure the stability of the entire structure.

The parallel arch supporting the railway section has remained standing, although it may also have been damaged. All traffic on the bridge was initially suspended, though trains were later allowed to resume running.

But the damage to the bridge's main span is "catastrophic", Colin Capriani, a structural engineer from Monash University in Melbourne, Australia, told BBC Verify. It was "very hard to envisage" how the bridge could be rebuilt given it is clearly well within range of Ukrainian weaponry, Capriani added.

Major attacks in 2022 and 2023 had already caused serious damage to the road spans of the bridge, but the structure survived.

The difference this time is "the most critical and vulnerable part of the bridge has been hit", said David MacKenzie of engineering consultancy COWI, adding that "although the wreckage of the bridge can be cleared fairly quickly, reinstating full road traffic will take over a year".

Moscow uses Crimea as a military base, with airbases, troop concentrations, training grounds and artillery stockpiles. From there it also carries out strikes on the rest of the country and resupplies its troops in southern Ukraine.

The attack comes after a summer of frequent strikes on Crimea, dictated by Ukraine's stated goal of cutting the peninsula off from Russia. At the time, Kyiv vowed to turn the Crimean Peninsula "into an island".

There is still a land corridor running through the occupied parts of the Zaporizhzhia and Kherson regions. However, Ukraine has been systematically striking transport routes across northern Crimea, as well as bridges and other crossings in the area around Chongar, which has already caused problems with the delivery of fuel and other supplies, including military cargo.

Moscow can also still utilise the ferry crossing across the Kerch Strait. #1. Five EU states announce plans for immigration ‘return hubs’ in Rwanda

Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Denmark and Greece likely to face challenges to policy criticised as ‘grave moral failing’

Five EU member states have announced plans to set up an offshore centre in Rwanda for people with no legal right to stay in Europe, in a pilot scheme that is expected to face legal challenges and has been denounced as “a grave moral failing”.

The five countries – Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark and Greece – are the first to seek to use the EU’s newly agreed returns law that allows people who have exhausted all avenues of appeal against their removal from Europe to be sent to a third country.

In a statement released on Monday the five EU countries said “concrete negotiations with partner country Rwanda will begin for implementation”, saying the scheme would make “an important contribution to further curbing illegal migration, dismantling the business model of smugglers and relieving the burden on our population”.

Officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

In theory, according to diplomatic sources, migrants would stay in a reception centre in Rwanda for a couple of months, before choosing to integrate into life in the Great Lakes country or return to their home nation.

Unlike the failed scheme pursued by the British Conservative government in Rwanda, or Italy’s agreement with Albania, it will not apply to people pursuing asylum claims.

The reception centre, which would be an open facility, was not intended to house people long term but to accommodate regular flows of people, the countries said. Diplomatic sources said the EU countries were not using British-funded facilities built in Kigali.

The governments involved argue it will protect the European asylum system while removing the incentive for irregular migrants to come to Europe.

Denmark’s immigration minister, Morten Bødskov, whose government has pioneered a tough line on migration widely emulated across the EU, said it was “a huge breakthrough”.

The German interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt, said: “Return hubs offer a positive approach for people without the right to stay and with no prospects in Europe, whose countries of origin do not cooperate in the repatriation process.”

Andy Burnham told reporters last week that the UK had not had direct discussions with European governments involved in return hubs but “we will watch that with interest, learning from what went on in the past. The public want us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration.”

Critics have highlighted the ethical difficulties of striking an agreement with Rwanda’s repressive government, which was found in 2024 by the US state department to allow state security forces and civilian officials to commit human rights abuses with impunity, while also raising concerns about freedom of expression and forced labour.

Spain’s socialist prime minister, Pedro Sanchez, said on Monday the move had no “future or humanity”. “I don’t see any future or humanity in that. It’s not the solution,” Sanchez, an outspoken defender of immigration in a Europe that has tilted to the right in recent years, told Telecinco television.

The French Green MEP Mélissa Camara denounced the agreement between the five countries and Rwanda as “an agreement of shame” and “a grave moral failing”. She said: “Under this agreement, people will be deported thousands of kilometres away from the European Union, with no guarantees regarding the compliance with their rights.”

Alain Berset, the secretary general of the Council of Europe, the continent’s human rights body, said it was not possible “to be so precise a priori” about what any return hubs proposal meant. Speaking to the Guardian before the announcement, he said all members of the Council of Europe were committed to the European convention on human rights “and this must be the lighthouse” for all discussions on return hubs.

“On concrete cases, then we will have to see if this is compliant with the convention,” he said. He stressed that “all countries must be able to define their migration policy”.

Officials involved in setting up the migration centres expect legal challenges, but say the scheme is grounded in human rights law.

Rights organisations say the scheme is bound to fail. “Member states may call them ‘return hubs’ or even ‘welcome centres’, but no euphemism can hide the violence of uprooting people from the life they’ve been building in Europe, and sending them to a country they don’t even know, without family, networks or meaningful prospects,” said Chiara Catelli of Picum, a pan-European organisation that campaigns for the rights of undocumented migrants.
