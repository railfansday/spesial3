#91. World leaders head to Asia for back-to-back diplomatic summits

Tussle for influence as trade, tech and regional security to take centre stage in China, Philippines and Cambodia meetings

World leaders will fly in to Asia for back-to-back high-level summits this week against the backdrop of the tussle for influence between the superpowers China and the US.

Beijing will have the home advantage when it welcomes Donald Trump to its southern technology hub of Shenzhen for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) leaders’ meeting. It starts two weeks after the US leader lost midterm elections.

The summit also comes after an awkward-at-best revelation that the head of Nvidia, the US chipmaker that for a long time was the world’s most valuable company, was having an affair with a woman US intelligence suspects was a Chinese spy.

Apec represents more than 60% of global GDP and has primarily promoted free trade and multilateralism. This year’s meeting could be defined, however, by the confirmed trilateral meeting between Trump, the Chinese president, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin of Russia.

The three-way meeting has been likened to a modern-day Yalta, when in 1945 then US president, Franklin D Roosevelt, Britain’s Winston Churchill and the Soviet leader Joseph Stalin met in Crimea to carve up Europe after the second world war. However, analysts say a “Yalta 2.0” that issues edicts on Ukraine and Taiwan without their involvement is unlikely.

Xiaoyu Pu, an associate professor at the University of Nevada’s department of political science, said a three-way meeting “might be more symbolic than substantive, although symbolism itself matters in great-power diplomacy.

“Trump appears interested in nuclear arms control and denuclearisation. Xi’s priority is likely to maintain a stable relationship with Washington while preserving China’s strategic partnership with Russia. Putin, meanwhile, wants to maintain his bargaining leverage over Ukraine.”

Apec begins on Wednesday and follows a flurry of other diplomatic gatherings, chief among them the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Asean) meeting in the Philippines, starting on Sunday, which Trump will attend.

While he is in Manila, US officials will hold a sidelines meeting of the so-called Quad – Australia, India, Japan and the US. In addition, a Francophone summit, which includes the Canadian prime minister, Mark Carney, and the French president, Emmanuel Macron, is happening concurrently in Cambodia.

Critical global issues at all these summits will include AI supply chains and governance, tariff reductions and the green energy transition. Trump’s focus has been on denuclearisation. A key regional focus will be on the South China Sea, where Beijing has built massive military outposts in disputed waters, as well as the gigantic Indonesian forest fire haze blanketing the region.

The humanitarian and security crisis in Myanmar has become a perennial issue, as is status of Taiwan, which China does not recognise. The self-ruled island is sending the founder of the tech hardware company Acer as its envoy to Apec.

For well over a decade, Washington’s engagement in the region has been coloured by concern over the rise of China, with the then president Barack Obama telling Australia’s parliament in 2011 that the US was “a Pacific power, and we are here to stay” – a policy known as the “pivot to Asia”.

The strategy of intense diplomacy and trade deals was adopted by Joe Biden, who hoped to gather allies on the continent who wanted to balance their relationships with both superpowers.

Some analysts see Trump’s transactional style in Asia as a continuation of the pivot policy. Still, the president’s volatility and unpredictability have spooked smaller nations, who regard China as a more predictable partner.

Jayant Menon, an economist at Singapore’s Iseas-Yusof Ishak Institute, said that countries in south-east Asia had been “laying the groundwork for a future with reduced dependence on the United States” by seeking deals elsewhere, including with each other and the EU.

“Amid persistent uncertainty and an administration determined to levy tariffs by any legal means, south-east Asia’s ability to ride out another round of tariffs depends on deeper regional integration and diversification beyond the US market,” he wrote this month in the East Asia Forum.

Beijing’s more predictable policies do not necessarily make it any more palatable for its regional neighbours, however, and the Chinese premier, Li Qiang, could have tough talks when he goes to Manila for the Asean summit, especially on the South China Sea.

China’s top diplomat, Wang Yi, recently urged Manila to rein in Filipino politicians who he said had repeatedly “smeared” Beijing, after public outcries in the country over the disputed waters.

Brunei, China, Malaysia, the Philippines, Taiwan and Vietnam have been locked in decades-long territorial standoffs in the busy waterway, which could sit on deposits of oil and gas. Washington has sent military ships on “freedom of navigation operations”, despite Chinese outcries, although US-linked crises in Iran, Ukraine, Venezuela, and Palestine and Israel have sucked up Washington’s focus under Trump’s second term.

A decades-long attempt to sign a South China Sea pact is not expected to happen at this year’s Asean summit, as previously hoped. Despite monthly meetings, negotiations will have to continue next year, when Asean’s leadership rotates to Singapore.

Diplomats at the Asean summit will also be focusing on Myanmar, which is seeking reintegration. Each country in the group is assessing how to deal with their isolated neighbour. The timing of summits this week coincides with a judgment in the international court of justice on Thursday that could find Myanmar responsible for genocide. The coup leader, Min Aung Hlaing, remains barred from Asean but is allowed to send a representative.

Asia’s week of diplomacy will be followed next month by the G20 summit in Miami, Florida, which Xi is expected to attend if US and Chinese relations continue to hold steady.

Long-held beliefs in Venezuela about sovereign control over oil are fraying as the U.S. lays claim to the country’s oil reserves.

Sunrise over the Cardón Refinery in Punta Cardón, Venezuela. Oil has shaped Venezuela to an extent rare outside the Middle East, becoming a defining feature of the country’s social fabric.Credit...Adriana Loureiro Fernandez for The New York Times

Simon Romero is a former Caracas bureau chief who has covered energy politics across Latin America. Isayen Herrera reports on Venezuela for The New York Times.

Like generations before him, Leonardo Silva grew up steeped in beliefs about the extraordinarily abundant resource that made Venezuela special. All the oil under their feet was theirs, they felt. Virtually free gasoline was a birthright. To be born atop this bounty was like winning the lottery.

Mr. Silva, 38, said his generation viewed oil as a cornerstone of the country’s national identity, when a booming economy allowed Venezuela to occupy a privileged tier in the global order. His children, he said, have a far less rosy view.

“To them oil isn’t important, it’s secondary,” said Mr. Silva, who works at a foundation in Caracas helping people with disabilities. “They don’t have that culture of us being a rich country. They just know that if you don’t work, you don’t eat.”

Oil has shaped Venezuela to an extent that is rare outside the Middle East for more than a century, becoming a defining feature of the social fabric like baseball, beauty pageants or the veneration of the liberation hero Simón Bolívar.

But now the U.S. government is taking control of a huge portion of Venezuela’s oil. And, pressed by economic need, long-held nationalist convictions about oil, starting with the need for sovereign control, are giving way to a more complex web of perspectives.

The ways in which Venezuelans are re-evaluating oil, and by extension their reliance on it, are the result of immense upheaval over the last decade. Oil production plummeted because of mismanagement, political purges, corruption and sanctions.

#85. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

An interview with the four astronauts will air on “CNN This Morning” on Tuesday, which starts at 6 am ET.

CNN’s Kristin Fisher contributed to this story.

#81. Canada could work with Chinese automakers if US trade talks fail, says Carney

The prime minister of Canada, Mark Carney, said the country could turn to Chinese automakers if trade negotiations with the US president, Donald Trump, failed to preserve an integrated North American auto industry.

Carney’s comments in Cambodia on Monday came ahead of a planned meeting with Chinese leader Xi Jinping this week in Shenzhen, China, where auto manufacturing is expected to be discussed. The two leaders reached an agreement in January allowing tens of thousands of Chinese electric vehicles into Canada at reduced tariffs.

“We have the best auto workers in the world, and we want them fully deployed, full opportunity, in an integrated North American market,” Carney told reporters in Cambodia. “That market has become less integrated because of US trade actions.”

He said on Monday that negotiations with the US remained his priority, but their outcome could determine whether Canada pursues alternatives involving European, Chinese or Japanese automakers. A day earlier in Manila, in the Philippines, Carney said any trade agreement with Washington must leave Canada with a viable auto industry.

Asked whether potential Chinese investment was leverage in US negotiations, Carney replied: “It’s not leverage. But action, reaction.

“If they [Canada’s auto workers] are not fully utilized in a North American context, that provides other opportunities,” Carney said. “Canada is going to move forward either way.”

The Detroit Three – Ford, General Motors and Stellantis – operate factories across Canada, the US and Mexico, with parts sometimes crossing borders multiple times before a vehicle is completed. Trump has repeatedly pushed automakers to move production to the US from Canada.

Carney said any Chinese investment would have to involve substantial manufacturing in Canada rather than simply assembling imported parts. He said it would need proper labor standards, manufacturing facilities and domestic supply chains that expand over time, including Canadian critical minerals used in electric vehicles.

“It’s not just about kit cars,” he said. “There has to be a manufacturing aspect involved.”

Under a January agreement, Canada reduced its 100% tariff on Chinese electric vehicles to 6.1%, initially allowing up to 49,000 vehicles annually, with the cap increasing over time. In exchange, China agreed to lower tariffs on Canadian agricultural products, including canola.

Carney said at the time that he expected the agreement to attract Chinese investment in Canada’s auto industry and create jobs.

The deal drew criticism from Ontario premier Doug Ford, whose province is the center of Canada’s auto industry. Ford warned that allowing Chinese electric vehicles into Canada could threaten Canadian jobs and jeopardize access to the US market.

Trump’s tariffs have disrupted Canada’s auto industry, which relies heavily on cross-border production. Canada recently threatened to sue Stellantis after the automaker shifted planned Jeep Compass production from Brampton, Ontario, to Illinois and agreed to sell its idled Canadian assembly plant.

Carney said Saturday that he had spoken at length with Trump and senior US officials and that the two countries were making progress toward a possible trade agreement, although differences remained.

He could afford to stand up to the billionaires because Russia's economy was getting back on its feet, and the central government was getting stronger.

Some oligarchs developed a distinctive dress sense in the 1990s, as later satirised by this St Petersburg artist

Bill Browder, a financier who used to be the biggest foreign investor in Russia, and was banned from the country in 2005 after campaigning against corruption, says the Russian tycoons cowered and wilted in front of Putin - for example at the Davos economic forum, in the pre-war years when they were still welcomed there.

"I can remember seeing oligarchs walking around like they own the place, the most arrogant and self-confident individuals. And then Putin showed up, and it was just remarkable to see how these supposedly alpha-male-on-steroids individuals turned into absolutely subordinate, almost desperate schoolchildren," he tells the BBC.

Since the outbreak of war in 2022, the Kremlin has kept Russia's rich businessmen in line with a ruthless policy of carrot and stick. Critics of the war have seen their assets seized, while loyalists have been rewarded with lavish government war contracts: today there are more Russian billionaires than ever (155 in total, according to Forbes magazine). â¯Their collective wealth has reached a new high of nearly $700bn (Â£525bn).

But even for those tycoons who have stayed loyal and been rewarded, all of that money doesn't seem to be making them happy. Some are growing restless.

Their billions do not go as far as they used to: domestically, they cannot buy much political influence.

"They have no cards, they have no game," exiled politician and analyst Dmitry Nekrasov says. "They're rich because they've got something to do with Putin, not the other way round."

And outside of Russia, they are ostracised because of the Western sanctions imposed on Russia after its full-scale invasion.

"They're treated like low-level criminals. They can't fly their private jets. Their yachts can't park in all the best harbours in Europe. It must be very awful and humiliating for them," says Browder, the avowed Putin critic.

And so, quietly, some of their minds are turning to what happens after Putin leaves the scene. That's what I'm told by well-placed analysts, who are immersed in the world of these tycoons.

There's no immediate prospect of the president's departure, and there's no reason to think he's in bad health. But Putin, who turned 74 last month, has served in the Kremlin for a quarter of a century; people in Moscow know that his exit will come one day. When asked by Reuters last year if he intends to serve until 2036, when his constitutional term technically ends, Putin replied: "Only God knows if we have enough health."

If Putin resigned or died in office, the prime minister, Mikhail Mishustin, would take over. But Mishustin has not exhibited any presidential ambitions (that's probably a key reason he was appointed). That would create something of a power vacuum.

In that scenario, Russia-watchers I have spoken with envisage a few potential scenarios.

Some in the West, and no doubt within Russia, hope that democracy might flourish in a post-Putin Russia, with government led by the people, rather than by a new cadre of oligarchs or a single autocrat. But this seems like the least likely path, analysts say. Key democratic institutions have been eroded or destroyed over Putin's rule. "Russia is so used to violence, it has grown unaccustomed to standard democratic procedures," says Alexandra Prokopenko, an exiled Russian journalist and analyst.

Another path is more autocracy. A different figure could replace Putin and continue his culture of repression. But Prokopenko thinks this wouldn't be another strongman dictatorship - at least not right away. These things take time, she says: at first, Putin's successor would be weaker than him, with fewer resources.

And then there's the third option: tycoons re-assert their power and Russia returns to something resembling a 1990s-style system of oligarchy. Dmitry Nekrasov, a former Kremlin adviser and opposition politician who now lives in exile, says under this scenario, the country would be governed by a handful of powerful elite clans in alliance with business interests.

This last option, of course, is the one that many tycoons want. "They'd like to return to a time when they were listened to, when they were not stripped of assets and when they understood the rules of the game," says Prokopenko, who has spent a lot of time speaking to Russia's richest people.

Though their power has been seriously curtailed since the 1990s, it's still worth listening to the tycoons: given their immense wealth, it seems likely that sooner or later they will try to re-assert their political influence.

Already, some are "trying to be friends with the right sort of security officials or ministers", says Nekrasov, the exiled politician. But he says any further steps are "too dangerous to take".

He says the behaviour of the tycoons in coming years will depend in part on their finances. Those who have lots of liquid cash will be more confident to "play political games" and jockey for position, preparing for a post-Putin country. But those whose wealth is dependent on state contracts or government goodwill will feel too afraid, in case they lose everything, he says.

Prokopenko says there's already been a shift in how the businessmen talk about Putin. They used to nickname him "Nachalnik" (which translates as "boss"), as well as "Perviy" ("first"), or by his initials "VV" - all nicknames that convey a degree of respect. Now they call him "Ded", with what Prokopenko calls "a pinch of contempt". "Ded" translates as "oldie" or "granddad" - a much less reverent name.

Some rich Russians are pining after a "return of politics into people's lives", she says, with less repression, more open speech, and healthier competition between parties and politicians.

But at the moment, she says, they are too afraid to make that happen due to Russia's stifling atmosphere. Prokopenko interviewed one tycoon who physically sat on his phone during the whole conversation, because he thought it would make digital eavesdropping more difficult for secret police. "I thought that it's an absolutely Stalinistic type of fear. It's absolutely irrational," she says.

An interesting clue for what tycoons might want from a post-Putin future came from Andrey Melnichenko, who is known as the world's fertiliser king and is currently Russia's eighth richest man.

Earlier this year, he surprised observers by giving an interview to The Economist - a rare thing for a rich Russian to do, especially to a publication from a country the Kremlin considers unfriendly. They also published an essay written by him.

In parts of the interview, Melnichenko repeated Kremlin talking points. He said that Ukraine is a battlefield between Russia and the West, and he thinks the West ignored Russia's security concerns and threatens its sovereignty.

But then it got more interesting. Melnichenko did not call for Putin to go, but - and it's a big but - he argued for a change that would bring about an end to one-man rule in Russia. He said Russia needs to engage more with the world.

Prokopenko thinks the tycoon was trying to stir a public conversation. It was a "call on rich people like him to start talking", she says.

The journalist who interviewed him, Arkady Ostrovsky, tells me the pair had "tense and heated discussions" in "hotels and cafes, mostly drinking coffee, lots of coffee, sometimes smoking". He says the tycoon seemed to be "genuinely thinking" about his answers, rather than giving politician-style spin.

Nothing in Russia is simple, and most observers think the interview probably happened with some form of approval - implicit or explicit - from the Kremlin. We don't know why authorities would have been happy to authorise the unusual call for change; it's possible they were trying to show that some degree of pluralism still exists in the country.

No tycoons came out to support him. Still, the journalist feels that Melnichenko was not speaking for himself alone, but also for his fellow businessmen.

Some tycoons feel ostracised because of Western sanctions, unable to fly private jets and visit Western resorts like Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat in southern France, which used to be popular with rich Russians

Some of them are hoping that after Putin's departure, the country might become an "oligarchic republic", as journalist Ostrovsky puts it - something like how it looked in the 1990s, with the business elites in charge.

Based on his years of reporting on Russia, Ostrovsky says that many of the country's mega-rich currently feel a "sense of complete dead end, which this war has led Russia toâ¦ [an] absence of the future".

But even if the tycoons are thinking about the future, there's a slim prospect of them acting any time soon, it seems.

Browder, the Putin critic, says that although many of them are unhappy, they're unlikely to conspire against Putin while he is still alive. "They understand that if they were to do anything that would challenge Putin, he would kill them. And Putin is a very paranoid little man."

It's easy for people who are sitting in London to say that, 'why don't you just all go stand up and go straight to prison?' That's really what would happen."

Russia's richest men used to be able to pull strings at the highest levels of power. Once Putin is gone, there is no doubt they will try to claw back their past influence. The question is: will remnants of the current regime let them?

#48. ‘The blood of these brothers cries out’: Pope Leo demands justice for people killed protecting the Amazon

Pope Leo XIV has demanded justice for environmental defenders killed for protecting the Amazon and backed efforts by Indigenous peoples to stop their lands from being ravaged by logging and illegal mining.

On the penultimate day of his South American tour, Leo on Sunday travelled into the Peruvian Amazon and channelled one of Pope Francis’s core teachings that links care for the most vulnerable with care for God’s creation.

“The blood of these brothers cries out to God and demands justice,” Leo said to applause from the crowd, many of whom were wearing traditional feathered headwear and face markings of the region.

In addition to deforestation, illegal gold mining has become an increasingly lucrative business in the Amazon, devastating forests and polluting rivers. Illegal mining and coca production has also driven deforestation.

Leo addressed the combination of threats in one of his most forceful speeches to date, drawing on his years of experience as a Peruvian bishop and Francis’s constant preaching about the environment and the plight of Indigenous groups.

#1. Zelensky urges US and Musk to lift Starlink restrictions and be on 'right side of history'

Restrictions have affected Ukraine's ability to intercept Russian missiles and drones

A crucial US satellite internet service is still operating too slowly and affecting Ukraine's ability to stop Russian attacks, President Volodymyr Zelensky has said.

"We are counting on these restrictions being lifted. The protection of lives and Ukraine's energy infrastructure ahead of winter directly depends on this," he said.

The issue over the speed of the Starshield system - a military-grade service developed by Elon Musk's Starlink and provided to Ukraine by the US government - has been rumbling on for weeks.

Zelensky said he had asked officials "to work as closely as possible" with the US government and Musk's company. "It is important to be on the right side of history," he said. The BBC has asked the White House and Starlink for comment.

Ukraine uses the normal Starlink service for communications on the battlefield, while Starshield is used for the interception of missiles and drones in Ukraine airspace.

The issue of speed restrictions was first identified in October. It led to conflicting accounts by both Elon Musk and US President Donald Trump.

Musk said the US government was responsible as Starshield was controlled by them, but Trump denied being responsible and said he had told the billionaire owner to "put it back".

It appeared to be returning to normal after a week, following which Ukraine said the restrictions were still being implemented.

The connection issues coincided with a tense diplomatic stand-off between Zelensky and Trump over Ukrainian strikes on oil infrastructure within Russia.

Trump said the strikes on Russian oil refineries, which Kyiv says are crucial to stemming the main revenue source for Moscow's war effort, had pushed global prices higher.

On Sunday Zelensky said Ukraine was struggling to intercept Russian jet-powered drones targeting electricity substations because "restrictions on the operating speed of Starshield terminals along the front line and in the temporarily occupied territories are still there".

"Our interceptor drones lose connection with their operators at a speed of 161km/h (100mph) and simply cannot catch up with Russian jet-powered 'shaheds'," he posted, referring to the drones that follow an Iranian design.

On Friday Zelensky said speed limits of around 100mph had been enforced on the network since Monday, but only in Russian-occupied areas and not the whole of Ukraine.

"It already has political implications. If you impose restrictions there, you are already partially recognising the temporary occupied territories as not ours," the New York Times reported him as saying.

Ukraine has also been beset by a shortage of US-made Patriot interceptors used to defend against Russian missiles - a situation that Russia has exploited to intensify strikes on Ukrainian targets.

Overnight and into Sunday morning Russia hit energy infrastructure in Zaporizhzhia and the Sumy, Odesa, Zhytomyr, and Kirovohrad regions, Ukraine said.

It also struck railway infrastructure in the Dnipro, Kharkiv, and Zaporizhzhia regions.

Scores of people are said to have been hurt in those attacks, as well as two in the Donetsk and Kyiv regions.

Two people were killed in an attack on an infrastructure facility in Chernihiv Region's Mena, governor Vyacheslav Chaus said.

Multiple strikes were also delivered on energy facilities across the region, he added on Telegram.

Ukraine meanwhile said it had carried out strikes on a petrochemical plant and an oil refinery in the central Russian city of Nizhnekamsk.

"On the night of November 15, units of Ukraine's Defence Forces reached Nizhnekamsk in Tatarstan," the military said on Telegram.

"Two enterprises that feed the Russian war with raw materials and fuel came under attack."

Other Ukrainian strikes killed one person in Taganrog, an industrial port city near the border, regional governor Yuri Slyusar said.

In the occupied Luhansk region a lorry driver and an elderly woman were also killed, Leonid Pasechnik, the regional leader installed by Moscow said.

Meanwhile Estonia has announced it will shut its border crossing points with Russia from 1 December following a suspected sabotage attack on a defence company.

Eight people were detained over an attempted arson attack on drone maker Threod Systems.

The Estonian authorities say they had been ordered by Russia's GRU military intelligence service to try and carry out the attack. Russia has not commented.

"Russia must understand that hostile actions against Estonia come with consequences," Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna said.
