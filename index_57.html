#106. The young Somali woman changing how the world sees Mogadishu

Somalia has the longest coastline in mainland Africa

As the sun rises over the Indian Ocean, waves lap onto the golden sands while palm fronds rustle in the cool sea breeze. Welcome to... Mogadishu.

This is the image that one young Somali woman wants the world to have of her home city - in contrast to the one of suicide attacks and young men wielding AK-47s that many people currently associate with Somalia's capital.

Sucdi Abdul Fattah Abdul Khader is among a growing number of local guides taking visitors beyond the headlines to explore Mogadishu's beauty spots, historic sites and culture.

Her favourite spot is Jazeera beach, where visitors can enjoy the sea before heading to nearby sand dunes.

"It's one of the tourist attractions where we bring our guests. It's a nice place," says the 23-year-old.

The dunes, she adds, remind some visitors of the Sahara.

But her tours are about more than scenic views. She also takes visitors to the fish market, where they discover the variety of marine life available along Somalia's extensive coastline and to parts of the city that reflect its history as a busy Indian Ocean trading centre, with the influences of both its Ottoman and Italian former rulers.

Visitors can also sample Somali food, including camel meat, samosas and traditional tea.

Sucdi says many arrive in the city with expectations shaped by years of hearing about the insecurity.

"Mostly, they are surprised by how beautiful the country is and how welcoming people are," she says.

At the fish market, she says, visitors are particularly impressed by the variety of fish species.

Sucdi Abdul Fattah Abdul Khader wants visitors to experience the "beautiful, welcoming side of Mogadishu"

Guiding tours runs in Sucdi's family. Her father worked in the profession for over three decades, taking journalists, photographers and visitors from different countries around Somalia.

He introduced her to the business nearly three years ago.

"I started this job as a hobby, but it has become something that I like and am passionate about," she says.

She now works alongside her father and a team of guides, building on his experience while using social media to reach a wider audience.

She shares videos of her tours on TikTok, offering people outside Somalia a glimpse of places they may never have imagined visiting.

She is also considering producing content in English to reach audiences who do not speak Somali.

A wide variety of fish are available in Mogadishu's markets

Tourism remains a relatively new field in Somalia, she says, with only a handful working as guides in Mogadishu.

The business is seasonal, but Sucdi says this year has been particularly encouraging, although numbers are still fairly low. Having five tourists a week is considered a success but she is confident that numbers will grow.

However, she says many visitors are still concerned about security.

Since the collapse of Somalia's central government in 1991, Mogadishu has endured decades of civil war, destruction and displacement. Much of the city centre was reduced to rubble, and basic services and infrastructure largely broke down.

But following the withdrawal of the Islamist militant group al-Shabab from most of the city in 2011, and the establishment of the federal government in 2012, Mogadishu has undergone a gradual transformation.

Returning members of the Somali diaspora, local entrepreneurs and investors helped fuel a construction boom, with new hotels, restaurants, businesses and residential developments reshaping the capital.

Today, Mogadishu is a growing commercial hub, although insecurity and the legacy of conflict remain part of everyday life.

About 10,000 tourists visited Somalia in 2024, a 50% increase on the previous year, according to the country's tourism department.

Many visitors ask about their safety before travelling and often encounter security checkpoints around the city.

But she says their experience on the ground is generally positive.

Lido beach is one of the most popular places for people to relax in Mogadishu

"What they heard from the news and everywhere else is not the same as what they see in the country," she says.

Although some question whether Mogadishu is safe enough for a young woman to be working as a tour guide, Sucdi is determined to continue.

She wants visitors to experience a Somalia that is rarely seen.

"I would want them to see the beautiful, welcoming side of Mogadishu, and not the side that they heard from the media, which is explosions and bombs," she says.

Somalia has Africa's longest mainland coastline, stretching more than 3,000km (1,860 miles) along the Indian Ocean and Gulf of Aden.

Its beaches and sand dunes sit alongside historic coastal cities such as Mogadishu, whose culture reflects centuries of Indian Ocean trade.

For some tourists, that contrast between the country's reputation and what they experience can be striking.

American travel content creator Chris Reese, who goes by the name Authentic Travelling, has visited Mogadishu twice.

His first trip, almost a year ago, was a solo visit. He enjoyed it so much that he decided to return with a group of friends, who had seen videos of his visit on social media.

"I had such a positive experience - I really enjoyed seeing the country for the first time - that I opted to bring back a group of my friends," he says.

What stands out for Chris is the city's coastal setting, its food and atmosphere.

"It is such a beautiful tropical place. I love the vibe, the weather and the food," he says.

The sand dunes near Mogadishu remind some visitors of the Sahara Desert

During the group's visit, they enjoy lunch overlooking the Indian Ocean before heading out to explore the beaches and sand dunes.

"The desert is beautiful and picturesque. It is also amazing that it's barely a 30-minute drive from Mogadishu," he says.

Before his first trip, Chris says, much of what he knew about Somalia came from international news coverage.

"If you only read the news reports about Somalia, you would be scared to visit. And honestly, that's what I saw in the media before I came, mostly negative headlines."

But visiting the country challenged those perceptions.

"Coming here, on my very first trip, showed me that there is so much more to this country than what is shown in the headlines," he says.

Romanian Mihaela Patap says she prefers to visit new countries without preconceived ideas

Mihaela Patap, from Romania, loves Somalia's natural beauty.

Although she has spent only a few hours in Mogadishu, she has already visited the sand dunes and taken in the Indian Ocean coastline.

"The desert was so beautiful to see. I have also enjoyed the beach, and the ocean. It is inspiring," she says.

Having travelled to Somalia from neighbouring Ethiopia, she says she has been struck by the people's friendliness.

"I don't see anything dangerous. I feel safe and the people are warm," she says.

An experienced traveller, Mihaela says she prefers to visit new countries without having preconceived ideas.

"I travel the world to see new places, to see countries, to see the differences. I don't take a picture in my mind to compare the reality with. I'm just expecting the new to come to me," she says.

She hopes to share her experience and encourage others to visit Somalia.

"I'm going to post my pictures and say good things about what I'm experiencing. I'm going to promote it," she says.

She is continuing a family profession while building a business in an industry with few women and using digital platforms to reach audiences far beyond Mogadishu.

"Somalia still has a long way to go before tourism becomes a major industry," she says.

"Through my tours, I am giving visitors an opportunity to experience its landscapes, our culture and people beyond the familiar negative headlines."

For her, every visitor is another opportunity to tell a fuller story of Mogadishu.

#104. UK spent £100m on zombie Brexit border inspection posts

Five of seven facilities in England and Wales never reached operational stage as Tories vacillated over plans to check goods

The UK wasted at least £100m in taxpayers’ money to set up zombie Brexit border inspection posts that were never used, it has been revealed.

The overall cost of Brexit was put at an estimated £4.7bn by the National Audit Office last year but this included years-long transformation of the software used for customs declarations and other systems built to cope with the UK leaving the single market.

Now fresh information from HMRC on the exact spend on the seven “inland border facilities” has come to light. The sites were built but quickly decommissioned as the Conservative government at the time vacillated over plans to check goods coming in from the continent.

Five of the seven “inland border facilities” in England and Wales never got to operational stage because the Tories decided not to implement all the border checks after concluding food coming from the EU would be the same high standard as it was before Brexit.

Portsmouth port, which is owned by the city council, spent £23m on building its border control post, which has never been used at full capacity because of the decision to scale back the checks.

The port received £17.1m in government funding for the project, leaving the council to make up the £6m shortfall, which could otherwise have been spent on essential services.

The EU launched all the checks in Calais, Dublin and other European ports from day one as the UK was treated as a “third country” overnight. The most controversial British zombie facility was known as “Dover white cliffs”. Furious residents at the village of Guston were told of the plans for the inland border facility on 31 December 2020, just hours before a “transition year” ended and Brexit was due to be implemented with supposed checks on all lorries coming across the Channel.

Information from HMRC, released under a freedom of information request, shows the facility has so far cost the taxpayer £28m, even though it remains a field. The Department for Transport paid £21m to procure the site and spent a further £7m on initial ground works, including an archaeological survey for any roman remains.

Another £17m was spent on a border facility in Birmingham planned at the airport car park. Newly released information shows it cost £11m to build with £6m in “closure costs”.

Another £20m was spent on sites in Warrington and in Ebbsfleet, a town just south of the Dartford Bridge on the Thames estuary. The Warrington site, on the former Shearings holidays coach stop at Appleton Thorn, was chosen because there were no suitable sites available for lorries coming from Ireland into Liverpool, Holyhead and Heysham ports.

Local people vehemently opposed the site at the time. It cost £7m to buy, £9m to build and £1m to decommission.

Another £10m was spent preparing customs and other checks at the former car park at Eurostar Ebbsfleet International train station, which cost £4m to convert into a lorry park and £6m to close, according to HMRC data.

A further £5m was spent on another site tucked away off the M11, just south of Stansted, at North Weald airfield. This airfield was commandeered for the same purpose, to avert congestion on the M25. It cost £3m to build and £2m to close down.

HMRC said Birmingham, Ebbsfleet and North Weald “did not require the acquisition of land”. The decision to build Brexit lorry parks so far from Dover underlined the lack of preparation for the UK’s departure from the EU.

Most of the UK’s lorry traffic is concentrated in Dover, but as a designated area of outstanding beauty it was impossible to conduct the post-Brexit customs and standard checks near the port.

#99. This man refused life-saving treatment because of his religion - days later the rules changed

Isaac Valdez had an epileptic seizure and his condition deteriorated rapidly

In August this year, 26-year-old Isaac Valdez had an epileptic seizure while browsing in his local library, causing him to convulse and strike his head against the ground.

He lost consciousness. Later, in hospital, it transpired Isaac had hit his head with such force it had led to a brain haemorrhage.

Treatment was seriously complicated by the fact that, as a Jehovah's Witness, Isaac carried a "No Blood" card, an indication that he would not accept treatment involving any form of blood transfusion because of his religious beliefs.

As a result, a procedure to drain fluid from Isaac's brain did not take place.

But his family, from Waxahachie, Texas, were encouraged by the fact that he regained consciousness and even started to talk again. He was able to confirm that he stood by his "no blood" beliefs.

The family's relief at his apparent improvement was short-lived. Isaac slipped into a coma and his condition worsened rapidly.

A neurosurgeon told Isaac's family the only way to save his life was an emergency operation, but that it was one that required blood transfusion.

His family took the decision to abide by Issac's wishes as a lifelong Witness not to accept such a procedure.

Isaac - who his twin brother Jacob Valdez describes as an intelligent man who loved volunteering and helping the elderly - died on 26 August 2026 when doctors turned off his life support.

Jacob's "no blood" card which is like the one carried in Isaac's wallet

Just 23 days later, the Jehovah's Witnesses' Governing Body announced a seismic change in teaching that would, in effect, have given Isaac the religious green light to have the life-saving treatment he had refused.

The change suddenly means the vast majority of medical procedures using donated blood are no longer prohibited to followers. Now, decisions on whether to accept such treatments are to be left to individuals.

Jacob, already dealing with the death of his twin, was angered at the Jehovah's Witnesses' leadership for not coming to their conclusion sooner. He told BBC News he believes Isaac could well still be alive if they had.

Jacob (right) believes his twin Isaac could still be alive if the change in teaching had come sooner

The original rule on blood transfusions dates back to 1945, towards the very end of a war during which the use of blood transfusions had been increasingly widespread. The leaders of Jehovah's Witnesses announced that their believers were not permitted to accept transfusions.

This was based on their interpretation of Bible verses from both the Old and New Testaments prohibiting the consumption of blood, including one from the Book of Acts that instructs believers to abstain from blood, from food offered to idols, and from things that had been strangled.

So, why has the Jehovah's Witnesses' Governing Body, based in New York state, now decided to change the rules, 81 years after introducing them?

It comes after years of pressure from both within and outside the faith, but that is not how leaders of the faith describe the decision.

"The change is basically the application of Bible principles to modern medicine and its methods," says the Jehovah's Witnesses international spokesperson, Paul Gillies, who described it as both a fresh look at scripture and at medical advances.

Jehovah's Witnesses international spokesperson Paul Gillies says the ban on transfusion of whole blood remains but blood components can now be used

We meet Gillies at the Jehovah's Witnesses UK headquarters set in 85 acres of Essex countryside. It is a vast modern complex where hundreds of volunteer followers of the faith live and work without pay.

He insists the basic teaching has not changed and that the transfusion of whole blood is still prohibited. However, for decades, most transfusions have not used whole blood, instead using the specific components of blood that are needed by the patient.

The change is being justified by permitting the use of blood components, like plasma and red blood cells and platelets, in the huge numbers of medical procedures that were previously banned.

Gillies says the Bible references "abstaining from blood" but not "various components that could be extracted from blood".

He says the Governing Body felt it their duty to avoid scriptural "overreach" and only apply what was specifically described in the Bible.

But that explanation has stunned many and led them to ask if it was not a huge overreach 81 years ago to ban blood transfusions in the first place, when transfusions are not specifically mentioned in the bible.

Others are then also asking why it has taken so long to come to this landmark decision, given that transfusions that do not use whole blood have been the norm for decades.

These are questions Jacob asked as soon as the ban was lifted, just days after a memorial service for Isaac.

Jacob, who had already left the Jehovah's Witness faith he was brought up in, texted prominent figures from Isaac's congregation in Texas after Isaac's death. The messages are full of angst, and painful to read.

He asked them to consider "the part you played in encouraging so many people to sacrifice their life for something that was never God's will".

Most did not reply, but one "elder" responded saying he could not understand why Jacob was so angry and fixated on the policy shift.

This all further infuriated Jacob, who particularly wants the faith's decision-makers to be accountable for all the lives lost.

"I want to ask them if they could apologise directly, I would like not to have a deflected answer, just straightforward apology," he says.

It is extremely difficult to say how many Jehovah's Witnesses, among the estimated nine million followers of the faith worldwide, died each year having refused treatment involving blood transfusions. But research in one medical journal in 2016, The Obstetrical & Gynecological Survey, put the figure at around 1,100.

"We're sympathetic to their feelings, and if individuals have lost family members and are concerned about some of these questions, they deserve to be heard compassionately," says Gillies.

But there was little clarity about why the change did not come earlier.

"Why has it taken this time? Well, I guess because it takes time, it doesn't necessarily mean that there is some magic change that's taken place," says Gillies.

And when asked, Gillies deflects calls for the leadership to apologise, saying that many lives had, in fact, been saved because faithful Jehovah's Witnesses had not been exposed to blood contamination scandals.

He also insists that even after the new edict, some Jehovah's Witnesses could still decide they did not want to accept a transfusion, and that may have been the case even if the edict had come years ago.

#86. Cop31: what is at stake at the UN climate summit and what can it achieve?

Delegates from more than 190 countries are gathering in Turkey for climate talks, but the US will be absent

As the planet faces unprecedented human-caused global heating, heads of government are meeting for the next fortnight for the latest round of climate talks. We ask why it matters and what is likely to be achieved.

From 9-20 November, world leaders, ministers and high-ranking officials from more than 190 countries are expected to convene in Antalya in Turkey, for the annual UN climate talks. This year, the summit will be hosted by Turkey, but in an unusual twist the negotiations will be presided over by Australia in a compromise deal struck after the two countries vied for the presidency.

While nearly all of the 196 original signatories of the 2015 Paris climate agreement are expected to be present, one glaring absence will be the US, whose president, Donald Trump, has withdrawn from Paris.

Australia will take charge of the formal negotiations where countries thrash out the details of work programmes such as the “just transition”, climate finance and putting the world on track to stick as closely as possible to the Paris agreement goal of limiting temperature rises to 1.5C above pre-industrial levels.

Turkey will take charge of the “action agenda”, the voluntary agreements that work towards the Paris goals but are not legally binding: programmes on cutting waste and achieving a circular economy, cutting methane, improving food security, and – perhaps most important – the electrification of the global economy.

The Paris goal of 1.5C has been breached, but the UN argues that rapid action to cut greenhouse gas emissions would limit the “overshoot” and allow for a return to lower temperatures by the end of this century. For that to happen, countries need to break dependence on fossil fuels and adopt low-carbon technologies faster. The two roadmaps from Brazil – on the “transition away from fossil fuels” and on halting deforestation – will present ways of doing so.

Developing countries are also in dire need of help to cope with the hastening impacts of the climate crisis. After the disaster in Nepal, the country has been granted access to the global fund for “loss and damage” – the rescue and rehabilitation of communities stricken by climate-related disasters. But it is not enough, and poor countries also lack the means to adapt their infrastructure to worsening extreme weather.

Rich countries have been cutting their overseas aid, while rising interest rates have loaded further debt on to the poor.

It was not until 2023 at Cop28 that the final agreement directly addressed fossil fuels for the first time, due to long-term strident opposition from producing countries. The agreement included a resolution to “transition away from fossil fuels” but no deadline or plan for doing so. It is still tricky to get fossil fuels directly on to the Cop agenda, which requires consensus from nearly all parties, but this year the issue will be addressed through a non-binding “roadmap” for the global transition away from fossil fuels.

Given the tense and fragile geopolitical situation – some commentators believe we are already in the midst of an unacknowledged third world war, while inflation and a rising far right are upsetting economies – countries might be tempted to settle for a quiet meeting.

But there are hopes for a better outcome, which could include: acceptance of the plans Brazil has produced and a promise to carry them forward; progress on climate finance for developing countries; a resolution to the row over climate science that has plagued recent meetings of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change; and the fleshing out of the “just transition mechanism” designed to help workers reliant on fossil fuels to adjust to a low-carbon economy.

Progress is also possible through Cop-related means outside the formal negotiations. Earlier this year, more than 60 countries, including the UK, the EU and dozens of developing nations, gathered in Colombia to vow to “transition away from fossil fuels”. This “coalition of the willing” could be boosted at Cop31 by new members, as well as fresh impetus from the recent success of political movements in several countries – Brazil, Canada, Serbia, Hungary and the US in states that decisively rejected Donald Trump at the midterms.

Several prominent members of the US Congress may attend, along with businesspeople and US civil society groups, so there will be representation beyond the White House’s absence. Trump appears intent on boosting fossil fuel interests as much as possible in the US, but with oil prices still high after his war in Iran, the economics of low-carbon energy look ever more favourable.

The geopolitics and the technology. Although the winds of conflict have upended international relations, they have also shown countries can benefit from the energy security that results from decentralised renewable generation. China’s oil imports, for instance, are likely to be down about 12% this year owing to a massive uptake of electric vehicles, and its renewable generation capacity has now outstripped coal. This is saving billions of dollars for consumers and businesses as the oil price soars.

Solar is booming in Africa at last, while in Pakistan a consumer-led rooftop revolution now produces about a quarter of the electricity used. Close to half of new cars in countries from Vietnam to some EU members are now electric.

Electrification will be a key focus of Cop31, and the high efficiency of electric technology means it could reduce the amount of primary energy use by half across the world, according to University of Oxford professor Jan Rosenow.

Politically, the re-election of Lula as president in Brazil has also cheered climate supporters. Lula will attend Cop, his first international visit since re-election, and is likely to be a fiery presence, showing progressive parties around the world how to win while championing the climate.

#80. Who’s who at Cop31: key players at the climate talks

What are the relationships and dominant interests in play, as the summit kicks off after a bitter row over its hosting?

Hosting Cop was a key pledge from Australia’s prime minister, Anthony Albanese in 2021, while in opposition, before his successful election campaign. But arcane rules meant the country had to wait its turn to bid and was then embroiled in an angry row with Turkey over the hosting. The final compromise put Australia in charge of the negotiations which will take place in the Turkish city of Antalya. The bitter taste left by the row lingers and Australia has seemed to some a rather half-hearted host. The country’s media has been much quieter on Cop than previous host countries, and although Albanese will speak at the opening of the leaders’ segment, he is unlikely to stay long.

Chris Bowen, Australia’s climate and energy minister, will be in daily charge of the negotiations. As aspects of Australia’s energy supply also fall under his portfolio, he has been in the sometimes uncomfortable position of having to boost fossil fuels while insisting on the need for a clean energy transition.

Yet Australia has a positive story to tell on decarbonisation: while remaining one of the world’s major fossil fuel exporters, the country has also invested heavily in renewables, and solar power has now expanded so much that consumers are sometimes offered free electricity.

Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, Turkey’s autocratic president, showed little interest in climate and environmental concerns before bidding to host this year’s Cop summit.

In the last five years, Cop has also been held in Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, Azerbaijan, all in the same broad region. This has given an outsize role to the Arab group of countries, who are neighbours in the region and have a strong interest in ensuring that fossil fuel interests – including their lobbyists – are well represented.

Australia runs the overall direction of the negotiations, and Turkey’s government, with the environment minister, Murat Kurum, has control of the action agenda, a programme of voluntary initiatives. Erdoğan has used this to push forward a key interest of his wife, Emine Erdoğan, in cutting waste and promoting the circular economy. She has called for an end to “throwaway culture” and for recycling instead of landfill – though Turkey still has sites that leak vast quantities of methane.

Fatih Birol, the head of the International Energy Agency and one of the world’s leading energy economists, is understood to be one of the advisers behind this year’s emphasis on electrification, displacing fossil fuels with electricity for heating, transport and industrial uses. This could halve the amount of energy we need, and is the next biggest step, after boosting renewable energy generation, needed for a low-carbon world.

The recently re-elected President Lula da Silva will come to Cop31 for his first trip abroad after winning his historic fourth term, and will bring two roadmaps, one a global plan for the transition away from fossil fuels, the other for halting and reversing deforestation. These are non-binding, and contain no deadlines or firm requirements from governments, but show a series of steps that governments can take to achieve these goals.

Xi Jinping, the president of China, will not come to this year’s Cop, but his country’s rapid energy and industrial transformation will determine the course of the planet’s future. China is the world’s second biggest economy and the biggest emitter of greenhouse gases, making up nearly a third of global annual carbon output, owing mainly to the use of fossil fuels to power its explosive economic growth for the last 25 years. But it has also invested in renewable energy, which is reshaping the global energy business.

Xi may not be present in Antalya, but the top officials he sends will know that petrostates – including Russia, Saudi Arabia and the UAE – are his important allies. However, countries pushing for a low-carbon future may be more important still – as China’s customers.

For several months this year, India’s prime minister, Narendra Modi, mulled an intriguing proposition: to host the Cop33 UN climate summit in 2028. In the end, India withdrew its bid in April without specifying why, leaving the field open. India is torn between its burgeoning clean energy sector – it now has the world’s third biggest installed capacity of renewable energy, after China and the US – and its coal sector, which still supplies about 70% of electricity, and has close links to Modi.

Bhupender Yadav, India’s environment minister, who leads its Cop delegations, enjoys a mutually supportive relationship with the UK’s Ed Miliband, and a reputation as a forward thinker.

The attention of Donald Trump has been fixed on the crucial midterm elections, a verdict on his second term as president that has shown Republicans the electoral dangers of an unpredictable White House. No US delegation is expected at Cop31, and soon Trump’s withdrawal of the country from the UN framework convention on climate change will mean none can take part.

Trump’s shadow was felt at Cop30 in Belem last year, as he started to dismantle the US’s renewable energy sector and boost fossil fuels, setting the US on course to start increasing greenhouse gas emissions again, after decades in which they fell. But his administration’s unpopularity with the US electorate, and a drought and fire-filled summer, has bolstered the pro-climate stance of scores of Democratic congresspeople and state governors, despite a campaign of “climate hushing” before the midterms. Some of them, and a scattering of business leaders, may attend Cop31.

Andy Burnham will attend Cop for the first time this year, after a mixed record on the climate since taking office. During the record-breaking heatwaves and droughts of this summer, the British prime minister was criticised for failing to speak out on the climate crisis. He subsequently made strong interventions affirming his support for the UK’s target of reaching net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050, but then backed the Jackdaw gasfield. No decision has been announced on the Rosebank oilfield yet.

Far-right populists are on the rise, pushing climate denialism, and the bloc has backed down from some key regulations, watering down its green deal package, loosening the strings on corporate climate liabilities, delaying rules on trade in goods from deforested areas, and extending the phase-in of new car rules. At Cop30, the Saudi delegation refused to allow even an oblique mention of the pledge to “transition away from fossil fuels”, made at Cop28 in Dubai in 2023, in the final text until the final moments.

At recent meetings of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, Saudi led a small group of countries – including China, India, Russia and Kenya – that fought to delay publication of the next comprehensive assessment of climate science. Delaying it by a year or more, as Saudi wants, would mean a loss of urgency for the global stocktake, and could help countries to get away with inadequate measures on decarbonisation.

Justice Diana Mochache said Mackenzie bore the "primary criminal culpability" for ordering his followers to fast to death, in one of the world's deadliest episodes of cult activity.

"The sentence is appropriate and just, to send an unmistakable message that such atrocities warrant the maximum penalties allowed by law," the judge added.

The self-styled pastor was arrested in 2023 after the discovery of more than 400 bodies in shallow graves in Shakahola forest near the Kenyan coast. The sprawling investigation into the cases shocked Kenya and drew international attention.

The court sentenced Mackenzie to 100 years in prison for homicide and another 100 years for offences related to starvation.

The sentences within each category will run concurrently, but the two sets will run consecutively, bringing his total prison term to 200 years.

Mackenzie also faces separate manslaughter and terrorism charges, which he denies.

Prosecutors had sought the death penalty, arguing that the scale and circumstances of the killings warranted the harshest punishment.

However, Justice Mochache declined to impose the death penalty, despite ruling that the case met the threshold for its consideration.

"Nevertheless, the court declines the invitation to impose the death penalty on any of the victims," she said, adding that capital punishment would eliminate any prospect of rehabilitation and disengagement. The death sentence has not been carried out in Kenya since 1987.

Mackenzie was tried alongside 28 accomplices, including guards who enforced his fasting orders and gravediggers who helped conceal the victims' bodies in mass graves.

They received varying prison sentences of up to 160 years.

Justice Mochache described the killings as "cold and calculated", saying the perpetrators had shown no remorse and had even declared their willingness to fast to death themselves.

Before the hearing began on Friday morning, Francis Wanje, who lost eight relatives including two grandchildren in Shakahola forest, told the BBC's Newsday programme that he was "hoping for... a sentence which will match his [Mackenzie's] deeds in the forest".

Mackenzie and the co-accused had asked for leniency from the court.

Before their conviction on 19 October, they had all pleaded not guilty and repeatedly insisted that nobody was forced to die.

More than 400 bodies were dug up in Shakahola forest

Mackenzie's followers believed he was "Jesus's mouthpiece", Justice Mochache said as she delivered her eight-hour judgement last month.

Those who opposed Mackenzie's teachings were beaten to death.

One of his co-accused said he had told his followers that anyone who "resisted the directive to fast must be forced to go to heaven through kicks and blows".

Burials inside the forest where the cult was based were called "weddings" and those who died "were celebrated as having wedded with Jesus" the judge said.

The judge found that the deaths arose from an organised system, including religious teachings and directives that compelled followers to follow the deadly fasting.

Post-mortem results showed the cause of deaths to be starvation for most victims, while others died from suffocation, strangulation or head injuries.

The cause of death could not be determined for some, with their bodies having decomposed, while others died soon after their rescue as they were so emaciated.

His End Days sermons included conspiracy theories that described education as "evil", healthcare as "against the doctrine" and digital identity cards as "the mark of the devil".

The main church shut down in 2019 and Mackenzie moved to Shakahola forest, encouraging his devotees to follow him.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.
