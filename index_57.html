#107. Would Ukraine trade land for NATO membership or protection?

The US is exploring whether Ukraine would cede parts of Donetsk province still under its control in return for NATO membership or protection. Experts warn the idea is risky.

The US has asked Ukraine whether it would be willing to cede Donetsk province to Russia in return for NATO membership. Together, Donetsk and Luhansk constitute the so-called Donbas region. Russia currently controls most of Luhansk province, and large swathes of Donetsk, though Ukraine continues holding a number of major cities there, including Kramatorsk and Sloviansk.

So far, no concrete proposal had been put forward, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He said he would publicly discuss the matter once the US and NATO member states had agreed on the plan, stressing that he could not make any such decision "on his own." Zelenskyy also said any potential agreement would go ahead "without recognizing Russia's occupation of these territories."

The Ukrainian leader said the US was talking about guarantees similar to those enshrined in Articles 4 and 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, though did not envision US troops on Ukrainian soil. Article 4 provides for consultations among member states in the event of imminent threats, while Article 5 stipulates that an attack on one state is considered an attack on all. The US, however, has said it pictures bilateral guarantees for Ukraine similar to those established with Israel and certain Middle Eastern countries.

Russia has yet to comment on these proposals, with President Putin's spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stating that "we maintain our position that, in the interest of the peace process, it is not appropriate to disclose details of the talks between us and the Americans."

Experts interviewed by DW are skeptical of the land-for-security-guarantees idea, objecting that the US cannot uphold such an arrangement on its own.

"It seems to me that this is what happens to normal diplomacy when it's been run by real estate agents who are used to selling people something that doesn't really exist," Oleksandr Kraiev of the North America-Israel Research Program at Ukraine's Ukrainian Prism think tank tells DW. He says that at least half of NATO's member states have reservations about Ukraine's accession.

"We'll just end up with a second Budapest Memorandum, it'll just carry a different name," Kraiev told DW. The original 1994 Budapest Memorandum stipulated that Russia, the US and UK respect Ukraine's independence and sovereignty in its existing borders and refrain from the threat or use of force to change its territorial integrity. Ukraine in return committed to giving up all nuclear weapons.

The Budapest Memorandum, however, did not contain any commitment by the UK or US to defend Ukraine in the event of an attack.

Most experts interviewed by DW are critical of the idea of holding a referendum to let ordinary Ukrainians decide whether their country should hand over full control of Donetsk province in exchange for NATO membership.

This is because ceding territory would not only violate Ukraine's constitution and harm its national interests, but would also undermine a world order "in which borders cannot be redrawn by force," Oleksandr Khara of Ukraine's Centre for Defence Strategies told DW. "It doesn't matter whether the citizens of Ukraine vote for or against such a proposal," Khara said, adding that any such referendum would set "a very dangerous precedent."

A survey by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KISS) conducted between October 8 and 16 found that 61% of respondents categorically reject withdrawing troops from Ukrainian-controlled parts of the Donbas, such as cities like Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, even in exchange for US and European security guarantees. 35% of respondents said they would begrudgingly accept a land-for-security deal.

Defense analyst Khara doubts a bilateral agreement between Ukraine and the US, akin to those established between the US and Israel, would guarantee Kyiv any more support than it presently receives.

"This concerns financial support, joint weapons production and development, and the training of Ukrainian troops — everything that falls below the threshold of collective security," Khara told DW. "It is good that these commitments — security agreements, political declarations, and the allocation of funds in the budgets of the countries supporting us — are set out in writing, at least for the next five years." Yet to prevent another war, Khara believes that ideally, Ukraine needs "a nuclear shield," specifically, a "strategic one from the US" and a "tactical one from Europe."

Ukrainian experts already floated to the idea of an alternative security arrangement to NATO members in December 2023, says Sergiy Solodkyy of Ukraine's New Europe research center. The arrangement would see Ukraine join the alliance within its internationally recognized borders, though specific protocols would stipulate that only territories under Kyiv's control would be defended.

This arrangement could be modeled on the German case, where West Germany became a member of NATO first, while East Germany joined the alliance only after German reunification.

West Germany never gave up on reunification, Solodkyy told DW. "That means we, too, should not give up on our territory or recognize the border." That is why he says the land-for-security deal proposed to Ukraine is unacceptable.

"This is a trap that Russia and the US negotiators are trying to lure the Ukrainian side into," Solodkyy told DW.

#106. US loses measles elimination status after 26 years amid record case numbers

BBC's Madeline Halpert explains why the US lost its measles elimination status and what it could mean.

After more than two decades, the US has lost its measles-free status, following two years of record case numbers and deaths.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), a specialised service of the United Nations, determined the US has had uninterrupted transmission of the same strain of measles for more than 12 months.

Dr Jarbas Barbosa, director of PAHO, announced that Mexico and Bolivia have also lost their measles elimination status, attributing the resurgence in the region to persistent "gaps in vaccination".

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) said it was committed to "regaining measles elimination status" by expanding access to vaccines.

The US has seen 5,400 cases of measles this year alone, with 10 measles-related deaths, the most the country has seen since the 1990s.

The measles virus - with its signature blotchy red rash - used to infect millions of children a year before a vaccine was invented. The extremely contagious virus can be fatal, and some patients can experience long-term complications including encephalitis and immune amnesia, which resets the immune system, leaving it with only limited ability to respond to new infections.

The US recommends two measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shots for children between ages one and six, which is 97% effective in protecting against the illness.

PAHO, a group within the World Health Organization, revoked the US's measles-elimination after determining that the virus had circulated nonstop for more than a year.

"These figures are a serious warning for our region," PAHO's director Barbosa said at a news conference on Tuesday. "Measles is resurging globally amid persistent gaps in vaccination."

He added that Canada, which lost its status of having eliminated measles last year after seeing several large outbreaks, has made strides this year to interrupt transmission.

Daniel Salas, the executive manager of immunisations at PAHO, said that the US and others who lost elimination status would be required to submit corrective action plans outlining how they would attempt to improve vaccination rates.

US Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr Kennedy has attempted to make a series of sweeping changes to US vaccine recommendations and policies. The vaccine sceptic has also frequently cast doubt on the safety of MMR shot, as vaccination rates have declined across the US in recent years.

Kennedy has argued that the rise in measles cases started before he took the helm at HHS and that neighbouring countries have also seen more infections.

Responding to Tuesday's announcement, the CDC said the agency and its partners were "committed to stopping measles outbreaks, protecting Americans, and regaining measles elimination status".

"Together, we will encourage MMR vaccination, expand access to vaccines, and help communities close gaps in protection," the agency said.

CDC Director Erica Schwartz said the measles elimination designation was not what protected Americans.

"Vaccination does," she said. "We know where transmission is occurring and we've invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities."

In a post on social media, Kennedy said: "I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles."

"We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust," he also said. "We will answer families' questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment."

The UK, Spain and Austria were among European nations that lost their measles-free status earlier this year, along with countries including Russia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan.

Nuwan Gunawardhana, an assistant professor of medicine with Columbia University Irving Medical Center, said the US losing its elimination status was "a huge leap backwards".

The US was the first country to eliminate measles in the Americas thanks to vaccination campaigns, noted William Moss, a paediatric infectious disease specialist at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

Dr Gunawardhana said it took massive efforts from public health officials and clinicians to build trust with patients to increase uptake of the MMR vaccine, leading to decades in the US with few infections and deaths.

But that trust has been steadily eroding since the Covid-19 pandemic, when Dr Gunawardhana said patients were confronted with misinformation about the safety of vaccines and lost even more trust in public health systems.

The vaccination rate for MMR across the US has fallen to 92.4%, below the 95% level that provides herd immunity, which limits the spread of measles and protects those who cannot get vaccinated, including newborns.

An outbreak in Pennsylvania has infected over 1,400 people - likely a large underestimate - and killed seven people, according to state officials.

Kennedy, the health secretary, has sparred with Pennsylvania officials over how it is counting the measles deaths, with CDC declining for now to record some of the deaths in its database.

Last year, the health secretary fired all the members of a top vaccine advisory panel and replaced them with his own appointees, some of whom were vaccine critics.

The panel recommended a number of vaccine changes - including no longer requiring the Hepatitis B vaccine for all newborns - but those recommendations were blocked by a federal judge.

President Donald Trump this year also cast doubt on the safety of the MMR vaccine, issuing an executive order for the shot to be split into three separate immunisations for measles, mumps and rubella, a move public health experts say is costly and could sow confusion about an already safe vaccine.

Doctors and public health officials say the misinformation from Kennedy and Trump has played a major role in falling vaccination rates.

"It's confusing patients. It's confusing families," Dr Gunawardhana said. "We need a clear message from the top."

The federal government's vaccine manoeuvres have left public health officials and states to fill in the gaps, offering their own separate recommendations upholding decades-long practices.

#14. Myanmar faces historic genocide verdict at UN court over Rohingya massacres

When the judges of the International Court of Justice read out their verdict in the seven-year-old genocide case filed by The Gambia against Myanmar on Thursday, they could make history.

In the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in 1948, after the Nazi Holocaust, dozens of individuals have been successfully prosecuted for the crime of genocide, in conflicts such as those in Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia.

But no state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide.

"It would be a judgment of landmark importance if The Gambia were to win this case," says Arsalan Suleman, the lawyer leading the case against Myanmar at the court. "It would give renewed vitality to the 1948 Genocide Convention and to the notion of international legal accountability for crimes committed by a state."

The mass expulsion of the Rohingya population of Rakhine State in August and September 2017 was without question a military operation of exceptional brutality.

I was among a very small number of journalists who witnessed the deliberate burning of Rohingya villages in Rakhine, and also the dreadful human suffering of the hundreds of thousands who fled to neighbouring Bangladesh.

Their accounts of killing, torture and mass rape by Burmese soldiers have been thoroughly documented by human rights groups and a United Nations fact-finding mission, external.

I have on many occasions interviewed ethnic Rakhine people about their animosity towards the Muslim population, and I have documented the systematic obliteration of what remained of Rohingya villages by the Myanmar state.

But the question posed at the UN's top court, the ICJ, is whether all of this amounted to genocide.

Hundreds of thousands of Rohingya fled across the Myanmar border in 2017 as attacks on them grew

The ICJ was established in 1945 to help settle disputes between states, not to try international crimes. However, it was chosen to address the attacks on Rohingyas in Myanmar because there were few other avenues of redress for the victims.

Myanmar authorities have denied accusations of ethnic cleansing or genocide, saying they were only responding to terrorist actions by Rohingya militants with so-called military "clearance operations".

A UN report in 2018, based on hundreds of interviews, said the army's tactics were "grossly disproportionate to actual security threats". It said top military figures in Myanmar must be investigated for genocide in Rakhine State and crimes against humanity in other areas. Myanmar rejected the report.

As Myanmar was not a signatory to the International Criminal Court, it was not possible to file a case there against the officials accused of atrocities in Rakhine State.

Instead in November 2019 The Gambia, backed by the Organisation of Islamic Co-operation, filed a case at the ICJ, arguing that all states are bound by the Genocide Convention, so all have an interest in upholding it.

It accused the state in Myanmar, not just the military, of genocide.

This was a novelty in international law; a state not directly affected by an act, arguing that it had legal standing to make a complaint because it was indirectly affected by any violations of such an important convention.

Myanmar objected to this argument, but the ICJ judges ruled that The Gambia was eligible to file the case.

Min Aung Hlaing, the general who now runs Myanmar, was responsible for the military's operations in 2017

The case drew even more attention when the Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi, who at the time headed the elected government in Myanmar, chose to lead the initial legal defence of her country at The Hague in person, for which she was widely criticised.

She had very limited influence over the actions of the Myanmar military in Rakhine State, but her refusal to condemn them dismayed many of those who until then had admired her courage and adherence to democratic principles.

Since she was deposed by a coup in 2021 and placed under arrest the defence has been led by a new team appointed by the military.

Historically, proving that genocide has been committed has been very difficult. Judges apply a high bar to this.

Typically, where there is no explicitly genocidal programme, like that of the Nazis, the judges require that the evidence presented at court must be such that the only reasonable inference from the actions of the defendants is that they had genocidal intent.

When Bosnia-Herzegovina filed a genocide case against Serbia at the ICJ in 1993, it took the court 14 years to conclude that while incidents like the 1995 massacre at Srebrenica did amount to genocide, there was not sufficient proof that the state of Serbia had genocidal intent, though it was found to have failed to prevent genocide.

Myanmar's lawyers and Aung San Suu Kyi have argued something similar.

Suu Kyi said that The Gambia's team mischaracterised a complex conflict and military operation, and that while she accepted that war crimes may have been committed against the Rohingyas, that is not proof of genocidal intent.

Aung San Suu Kyi's refusal to condemn the Myanmar military's actions in Rakhine State dismayed her admirers

But Arsalan Suleman believes the weight of evidence his team gathered is enough to meet that high bar.

"What we presented to the court was the long history of discrimination targeting the Rohingya, and how that history, and the policies connected with it, provide a background to understand the government's language, rhetoric, propaganda, and policies that were part of the 2017 'clearance operations'," Suleman said.

"We discussed the 1982 citizenship law, and the tightening noose of discriminatory laws that restricted access to education, healthcare, livelihoods, and travel - all of which made life really impossible for the Rohingya and, we argued, was one of several indicators of genocidal intent."

What happens if the court does find in favour of The Gambia?

Myanmar would then find itself tarnished by having committed "the crime of crimes", as sitting on the same bench as Nazi Germany, the Rwandan Hutu militants and the Bosnian Serbs at Srebrenica.

That might seem a setback for Min Aung Hlaing, the general who now runs Myanmar, who above all was responsible for the military's operations in 2017, and who has been trying to improve his dismal international image in recent months.

There is no right of appeal against an ICJ verdict. However, there is also no penalty that follows an ICJ verdict.

The Burmese coup leader is focused mostly on rebuilding relations with neighbouring Asian countries, who are unlikely to pay too much attention to such a verdict, and much less on rebuilding relations with Western countries who do care.

Arriving at the tunnel entrance, the first thing to hit you is the lingering smell of gas and fetid air.

Both sides use openings in the pipeline to get to the surface, where most of the fighting takes place. They have made breaches in the ground, and used welding tools to break through the thick steel.

The Russians were the first to exploit the pipe to infiltrate the nearby city of Kupyansk in September 2025. They have used underground pipelines before, during the capture of the city of Avdiivka in 2024, and last year in their bid to recapture areas of Russia's Kursk region.

Officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

The spotlight is on €5bn in EU “soft” loans to Egypt – lending with easy repayment terms, known as macro-financial assistance, that is meant to be available only to countries that respect human rights and democracy. While the EU conditions on democracy and rights are vague, the loans impose much more specific requirements on Egypt to carry out economic reforms.

Separate data sources – from leaked EU documents and deleted from an Egyptian government website – raise questions about the European Commission’s claims that Egypt met targets linked to social welfare programmes, which enabled the EU to release €2.5bn in assistance.

In confidential documents, the commission told EU lawmakers that Egypt had “broadly fulfilled” and then “fulfilled” two separate targets to increase the numbers of poor people on social welfare programmes between 2024 and 2026. But data points to a net reduction of 85,734 households as the Egyptian government removed people from its welfare programmes rather than expanded coverage.

#2. Fears grow that US will put Nobel-winning judge under sanctions after Myanmar genocide ruling

A ruling against Myanmar could set precedent for court’s judgment on Israel’s actions in Gaza

Fears are growing that the US could impose sanctions on judges at the international court of justice, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court rules on Thursday that the Myanmar government committed genocide against the Rohingya.

The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide, and a significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove “intent” in a crime defined as acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

It is the role of independent judges to interpret the genocide convention. However, judges at the court have taken advice on how to protect their assets from potential US sanctions, as a genocide ruling could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime.

The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, an influential thinktank close to the White House, has predicted a genocide ruling will lead to the US imposing sanctions on the ICJ.

The US and Israel fear the final judgment may clarify how to establish a government’s intent to commit a genocide, and so set a precedent that makes it more likely the court will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza.

Myanmar, which has denied carrying out a genocide, claims its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ, sometimes described as the UN’s top court, is responsible for settling interstate disputes, unlike the international criminal court (ICC), which prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes.

The US has already imposed crippling sanctions on judges at the ICC after the court issued arrest warrants against the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. The US has also announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court”. Washington fears the ICC could go after US troops.

The Gambia, as a litigant, was entitled to appoint an ad hoc judge in the case against Myanmar and chose the South African lawyer Pillay, who in October was awarded the Nobel peace prize. She is required to act as a jurist and not an advocate for the Gambia.

Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, has sent a warning shot to Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges, saying Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponise the law against our troops”.

Waltz asserted last month: “The ICJ ad hoc judge Navi Pillay is tipping the scales on the ICJ’s current proceedings on Myanmar, intending to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponised against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.”

In remarks addressed to Pillay, he said: “We see your agenda and we’ll be watching the ICJ’s handling of these proceedings very closely.”

In a speech last Friday, the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, said: “For this global elite, international law … is an instrument to advance their interests under the pretence of universal principles.”

Israel has previously criticised adverse advisory opinions issued by the ICJ and refused to implement 2024 orders requiring Israel to admit more aid into Gaza, and to cooperate with Unrwa, the UN relief and works agency.

In the Myanmar case, a 24-page submission jointly filed by the UK and five other mainly European states, argued that the ICJ, in judging intent to commit genocide, should examine a range of “facts and circumstances” assessed “comprehensively and holistically”.

It also stressed that genocide was not solely defined by killings, and that the treatment of children, gender-based violence and forced displacement were also relevant.

The US until recently had taken a relatively more benign view of The Hague-based ICJ, and under Antony Blinken as US secretary of state determined that Myanmar had committed genocide.

The 15 ICJ judges are elected for nine-year terms by the UN security council and the UN general assembly.

Rubio has given the ICC until April to respond to his criticisms before he imposes sanctions.

On Friday, the ICC formally decided to drop plans to prosecute US soldiers and intelligence agents for potentially unlawful acts in Afghanistan. The shelving of the near-dormant inquiry came after it was agreed evidence would be hard to obtain, but one side-effect may be a greater willingness on the part of the US to abandon its plan to impose sanctions on the ICC.
