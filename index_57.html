#120. UK ministers told not to engage formally with Indian mega-zoo after lobbying

Guardian and Mongabay reveal approaches to officials and MPs on behalf of Vantara zoo ahead of UK-chaired Cites meeting on endangered animal imports

British government ministers were told not to engage formally with Vantara, the controversial mega-zoo run by the Indian billionaire Anant Ambani, after lobbying efforts by the private animal sanctuary in the run-up to a UK-chaired meeting on the trade in endangered species, according to documents.

This week, the UK heads a 32-country committee meeting in Geneva of Cites, the United Nations body that regulates trade in endangered species. The meeting will examine how India has responded to compliance concerns about imports of endangered animals.

Documents obtained by the Guardian and the news outlet Mongabay under the UK’s Freedom of Information Act reveal Vantara’s efforts to lobby British officials and ministers before the meeting.

Ambani, 31, is the heir to the oil and gas fortune of one of India’s richest men, Mukesh Ambani. He set up a 1,400-hectare (3,500-acre) private zoo near his family’s oil-refining facilities in Gujarat, where more than 150,000 animals have reportedly been amassed since 2022. Vantara boasts high welfare standards for animals in its care, including facilities such as an elephant hydrotherapy pool, where more than 260 elephants can also receive oil therapies and Ayurvedic massage.

But conservation groups have repeatedly questioned how such a vast number of species, many of which are endangered, were gathered so quickly. Last year, India’s supreme court cleared the zoo’s operators of any wrongdoing.

A subsequent Cites fact-finding mission looking into the zoo raised concerns about India’s compliance with international rules on animal imports in 2025. It recommended temporary restrictions on India, although the recommendation was later withdrawn.

Its November 2025 report said: “There is a need for caution to ensure that imports of large numbers of animals by these facilities are not inadvertently creating a demand for illegally sourced animals.”

In May, officials from the UK’s Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) met a representative from Vantara and the US lobbyist firm Checkmate Government Relations, founded by Ches McDowell, a hunting friend of Donald Trump Jr.

Before the meeting, internal briefing documents said Checkmate was “seeking to persuade the UK to take a more sympathetic view of India’s handling of imports to Vantara and of Checkmate’s argument that responsibility for verifying legality sits primarily with exporting or re-exporting parties rather than with India as importer”.

During the meeting, the Vantara representative invited UK officials to visit the zoo – an invitation that was subsequently declined to ensure the impartiality of the UK’s Cites chair role.

After the meeting, a representative from another lobbying firm, Atlas Strategy, working on behalf of Vantara, approached the then secretary of state, Emma Reynolds, and then nature minister, Mary Creagh, also raising the prospect of a visit to the zoo.

When a department official asked for advice, a note was issued recommending that ministers did not engage formally with Vantara.

The government documents also reveal concerns about an application by Vantara to import falcons and falcon hybrids, after an unnamed organisation made a £50,000 “donation” to a breeder in the UK, whose identity was also not disclosed. British Cites officials classified the transaction as commercial, according to the documents. The falcons were not exported to India.

A spokesperson for Vantara said it had not imported falcons from the UK, nor had the zoo hired or paid a lobbying firm. It is understood that Checkmate and Altas Strategy were working pro bono for Vantara and not required to register with the Office of the Registrar of Consultant Lobbyists.

After recent scrutiny, Vantara has sought to “reset” its activities by voluntarily suspending animal imports for a year and hiring John Scanlon, a former Cites chief, to oversee its operations. In an interview with the Sunday Times this year, Ambani said he only brought animals to Vantara legally to ensure their welfare, mentioning a group of hippos he had saved from a hunting lodge in South Africa and jackass penguins saved from a shopping centre in Qatar.

“I think since I was a baby I have been taking care of animals. It’s always been a passion,” he said.

Vantara was used to host Ambani’s lavish pre-wedding celebrations in 2024, which were attended by celebrity guests and politicians. In September, Newcastle University awarded the oil heir a professorship of practice for “his work in conservation, biodiversity protection, animal welfare and environmental stewardship”, sparking a backlash from conservationists.

A Defra spokesperson said: “The UK has strict rules in place to protect endangered species and ensuring international wildlife trade is legal and sustainable, with every application assessed individually against internationally agreed and domestic requirements.”

The British pound crashed to a record low last fall as investors rebelled against budget plans by former Prime Minister Liz Truss. Now, it’s enjoying a comeback.

Sterling hit its highest level against the US dollar in 10 months on Tuesday, topping $1.25 for the first time since June 2022. The pound, which has advanced about 3.3% versus the greenback since the start of 2023, is the best-performing currency among developed economies this year.

The UK currency has been boosted by indications the country’s economy is holding up better than expected. Activity is now thought to have expanded 0.1% in the final three months of last year, up from a previous estimate of no growth at all. Gross domestic product growth in January has been estimated at 0.3% after dropping 0.5% in December.

This resilience is bolstering expectations the Bank of England will maintain aggressive interest rate hikes despite concerns about the health of the global banking sector. Rising rates can boost the domestic currency because they help attract foreign investors searching for higher returns.

Inflation in the United Kingdom also jumped to an annual rate of 10.4% in February, underscoring the need for the Bank of England to maintain its tough approach.

The pound plunged close to $1.03 in September 2022 after the Truss government unveiled plans to boost borrowing while slashing taxes, unleashing panic in financial markets that fueled fears of a recession in the United Kingdom.

The International Monetary Fund predicted in January that the UK economy would contract by 0.6% this year, while all other advanced economies would grow, if only slightly.

“There was a lot of pessimism being priced into the pound,” said Francesco Pesole, a currency strategist at ING.

But the sharp pullback in energy prices and China’s reopening have provided some relief about the economic outlook since the start of the year.

“There was a big re-rating of growth expectations around Europe, and that impacted the UK,” Pesole said.

The euro has also been lifted by these dynamics, rising 2.3% against the US dollar in 2023. The pound’s rally has been sharper in large part because its 2022 declines were more severe, according to Pesole.

Jordan Rochester, a currency strategist at Nomura, said he thinks the pound could rise to $1.30 this year and “potentially higher.” But he still sees risks given the uncertainty surrounding the Bank of England’s plans and how rate rises will feed back through the country’s economy. And Pesole cautioned that currency fluctuations are often overdone when markets are choppy, as they are now.

“In a volatile market environment, moves are exacerbated,” he said.

#117. NASA's Swift space telescope plunges back to Earth in fiery demise

NASA's Swift orbital observatory fell back to Earth after running its mission for more than two decades. A rescue attempt to raise its sinking orbit had earlier failed.

A premier NASA space telescope met a fiery end as it crashed back to Earth —  two decades after its launch —  following the failure of a rescue mission.

The unmanned Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory re-entered the Earth's atmosphere over the North Sumatra region of Indonesia on Wednesday at 7.39 am EST (12.39 pm UTC), the US space agency said.

Most of the spacecraft was expected to have burnt up as it traveled through the atmosphere, NASAsaid, adding that some components may have survived re-entry.

NASA's prized Swift observatory — equipped with three telescopes — was launched into orbit in 2004.

The observatory was tasked with studying gamma ray bursts, the biggest ​energy blasts in universe for two years. But its mission spanned 11 times longer than planned, lasting just a few days short of 22 years.

The $500 million (€444 million) Swift satellite weighed roughly 1,452 kilograms (3,200 pounds).

It studied hundreds of supernova explosions, star-shredding black holes, stellar X-ray flares, asteroids and ​comets.

Swift also contributed to nearly 7,000 published scientific ⁠papers.

The satellite, which was in a good condition, fell out of orbit partly due to powerful geomagnetic storms set off by solar eruptions.

In July, NASA roped in private company Katalyst Space and launched a mission to raise the altitude of the Swift observatory.

But Katalyst Space's three-armed robotic servicing spacecraft — called LINK — faced issues with attitude control soon after reaching space, causing it to spin uncontrollably.

The rescue vehicle plunged back to Earth in September, while Swift ceased operations on October 20.

There were no immediate reports of damage on ground as the Swift observatory fell back to its home planet.

#116. The private creative world of Rabindranath Tagore, the man Yeats called ‘greater than any of us’

A previously unseen family archive offers a glimpse into the Indian Nobel prize winner’s artistic process and personal relationships in his final years

It’s easy to run out of words to describe Rabindranath Tagore. The first non-European to win the Nobel prize in literature, Tagore was a Bengali poet, musician, novelist, philosopher, songwriter, painter, humanist and educator.

Born in 1861, Tagore left an indelible mark on the subcontinent’s literature, music, education and art. Among his enduring legacies are the national anthems of India and Bangladesh.

As Nobel laureate economist Amartya Sen once wrote, Tagore was “a towering figure in the millennium-old literature of Bengal”, whose songs resonate across eastern India and Bangladesh, while his ideas on education, nationalism and freedom of the mind remain relevant today.

Yet his work also made a deep impression on the literary world far beyond the shores of India. WB Yeats is said to have described Tagore as “greater than any of us”. Gitanjali, the collection of poems that won Tagore the Nobel prize that same year, was praised by the committee for its “profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse”. Tagore’s English translation was published in London that March and reprinted 10 times by the time the prize was announced in November.

Tagore’s life and works have, of course, been well documented, but a collection of material preserved in a private family archive is now being brought into public view for the first time at an exhibition by Christie’s, opening in Mumbai this month. The archive draws from Tagore’s visits to the family home of his protege, Maitreyi Devi, in the bucolic village of Mungpoo in Darjeeling between 1938 and 1940. He died in 1941.

The collection on display – artworks, manuscripts, letters, photographs and personal effects – offers an intimate glimpse into Tagore’s creative process and personal world in his final years.

I spoke to the exhibition curator, Nishad Avari, head of south Asian modern and contemporary art at Christie’s, about the stories behind the archive.

One instructive document Avari points to is a manuscript from 1940. It appears to be a draft of Tagore’s memoir Chelebela or My Boyhood Days, in which he reflects on his life – from growing up in a large family in Calcutta (modern-day Kolkata) to the rapidly changing world ushered in by electricity and the motorcar.

For Avari, what stood out was how the ideas in the document echoed the educational philosophy Tagore put into practice in Santiniketan (or Abode of Peace), the experimental school he set up about 100 miles from the state capital.

“He speaks about how education is like being in prison and that it should be open and free … about the idea of wonder and even fear as inspiring creativity,” Avari told me.

“That’s a direct relationship to Santiniketan and the open model of the school where people sat under trees with their gurus and learned in the open. Education didn’t feel like a restriction, prison or imposition,” he added.

Avari points to another detail that makes this particular document valuable. Of the 10 complete manuscripts in the exhibition, it is the only one dictated to Devi, whose father was a close friend of Tagore’s. For Avari, this speaks to their close relationship and the trust he placed in her.

“If you look at the collection as a whole, she has copies of almost every single one of his publications signed to her by him,” he says. The collection includes hundreds of letters, too: first between Tagore and her father, and later between Tagore and Devi herself.

There are doodles among corrections, Avari explains, which show how his literary practice fed into his artistic practice. Alongside the manuscripts and correspondence are vintage photographs and personal effects, including a kurta and a lungi that Tagore left behind at the family home.

Among the artworks in the collection is a portrait of Devi, particularly notable because Tagore’s figure studies were not generally attributed to specific individuals in the way that portraits by his nephews, Abanindranath and Gaganendranath, often were, Avari said. There is also a landscape depicting the forests of Mungpoo, which Tagore embraced as a retreat.

On the international art market, Tagore’s paintings command significant prices. This year, Sotheby’s sold one of his watercolours for £1.02m (Rs 12.97 crore), nearly four times its low estimate.

Institutions such as Visva-Bharati in Santiniketan and the National Gallery of Modern Art hold much of Tagore’s archival materials, making the discovery of a private archive particularly rare.

He hopes that making these materials public will offer historians, scholars and admirers alike additional perspectives on Tagore. “We hope to add to the understanding of the artist, their career, and in this case, of course, our understanding of the man beyond his art as well.”

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

#94. Beijing promised to 'fight back' over Taiwan leader's US visit. But this time it has more to lose

An anticipated meeting between Taiwan’s President Tsai Ing-wen and US House Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California this week has sparked concerns of a repeat of the pressure campaign China launched last year when then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei.

At that time, Beijing encircled the island democracy with unprecedented military drills – firing multiple missiles into its surrounding waters and sending dozens of warplanes speeding across a sensitive median line dividing the Taiwan Strait.

It also cut off contact with the United States over a number of issues from military matters to combating climate change, in retaliation for what it viewed as a violation of its sovereignty.

This time, Beijing has already threatened to “resolutely fight back” if a Tsai-McCarthy meeting goes ahead.

It also slammed Washington for allowing Tsai to stopover in the US while en route to and from official visits in Central America, warning it could lead to “serious” confrontation between the two powers.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

But a meeting between Tsai and the leader of the Republican majority in the US House of Representatives, who ranks second in line to the Presidency, would mark another symbolic moment for Taiwan and the US, which only maintain unofficial ties.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

That increased visibility – and enhanced cooperation with the US – has followed China’s mounting pressure on the island, which sits fewer than 110 miles (177 kilometers) from the mainland coast.

China’s Communist Party claims the self-governing island democracy as its own despite never having controlled it, and has vowed to take the island, by force if necessary.

China thinks it's diplomatically isolating Taiwan. It isn't

The party has undertaken a sweeping expansion of its military capabilities over the past decade under leader Xi Jinping – and ramped up its pervasive economic, diplomatic and military pressure on Taiwan.

That’s driven concerns, among some in Washington, that Beijing is preparing for an invasion, though China’s official language still suggests that scenario is not its preferred option for achieving the claimed goal of “reunification.”

It is those pressures – and how to support Taiwan against unilateral actions by Beijing – that are likely to be on the table when Tsai, McCarthy and a bipartisan group of US lawmakers sit down on Wednesday.

Congress has been a pillar of increasing American support for Taiwan in recent years. Lawmakers regularly visit the island and drive bipartisan legislation enhancing support and cooperation.

While the US switched its diplomatic relations to Beijing decades ago, it maintains unofficial ties with Taiwan and is bound by law to provide the democratic island with the means to defend itself.

Under Washington’s longstanding “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, but has never officially recognized Beijing’s claim to the island of 23 million.

Though McCarthy does not have Pelosi’s decades-long record of advocacy regarding China, the California Republican is now a leading voice pushing for closer scrutiny of Beijing, and meeting Tsai could help him to burnish that image.

Last month, McCarthy told reporters that meeting Tsai in the US would not impact whether he travels to Taiwan in the future – something he had earlier said he wanted to do.

A meeting in California, on US soil, is widely seen as less likely to provoke Beijing than a McCarthy visit to Taiwan.

Pelosi’s trip – the first from a lawmaker of that rank to the island in 25 years – generated a fever pitch of nationalist and anti-US rhetoric in mainland China.

This time, so far, domestic conversation in China’s heavily controlled media sphere has been significantly muted.

As Taiwan prepares for a presidential election in January, a fierce response could push voters away from Taiwan’s main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT), widely seen as more friendly toward Beijing.

It could also jar with another high profile trip happening now: a tour of mainland China from former Taiwan president and senior KMT member Ma Ying-jeou, the first visit from a current or former Taiwan leader since the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949.

Ma’s tour is a “once in a half a century opportunity to send a conciliatory message between the two sides, Beijing shouldn’t want to tank that,” said Sung, the political scientist.

This week, as Tsai is expected to meet with McCarthy, French President Emmanuel Macron and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen will head to China – an important opportunity that Xi may not want to overshadow with military posturing.

An aggressive response also risks stoking confrontation with the US, not yet six months after Xi and US President Joe Biden called to enhance communication during a face-to-face meeting in Bali.

“(A less overtly aggressive response) would imply that Beijing does not wish to escalate tension with the US to a level that can risk getting out of hand,” said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London.

Italian film director Tinto Brass, who was once hailed as the “Antonioni of the 70s” but came to be better known for bringing Italian erotic cinema to a wider audience with films such as Caligula and Salon Kitty, has died aged 93.

His wife Caterina Varzi and children Bonifacio and Beatrice Brass announced the news of the director’s death to Italian press agency Ansa on Wednesday.

Born in Milan and raised in Venice, Brass first started working in cinema after moving to Paris, where he was an editor and archivist at the Cinémathèque. Upon returning to Italy, he worked as an editor and assistant director to Roberto Rossellini.

His experimental and anarchic approach to film quickly earned him a reputation, and in 1964 he was commissioned by Umberto Eco to create two short films experimenting with visual language for the Triennale di Milano. Warner Bros reportedly approached him to direct A Clockwork Orange, though scheduling conflicts meant the film was offered to Stanley Kubrick instead.

In the 1970s, Brass moved on to erotic cinema. Though crass and filled with nudity, his first steps in the genre were also politically minded explorations of the relationships between sex, power and money.

“I made so many erotic films because I believed eroticism to be a fundamental key to human expression and, above all, the key to portraying women,” he told La Repubblica newspaper. “That was what interested me most.”

His 1976 “Nazisploitation” film Salon Kitty was based on the true story of a Berlin brothel co-opted by Nazis to spy on foreign allies and fellow officers. Starring Malcolm McDowell in the title role, alongside Helen Mirren, Peter O’Toole and John Gielgud, the film was based on a screenplay by Gore Vidal, which Brass rewrote to include orgies, decorative phalluses, and plenty of female nudity.

Vidal disavowed the film, as did Brass after Guccione shot additional unsimulated hardcore sex scenes and spliced them into the film without the director’s permission. In recent years Brass lived in Isola Farnese, on the outskirts of Rome. He is survived by his wife, Varzi, whom he married in 2017 and who curated his archive. In an interview on the occasion of his 90th birthday, he expressed a wish for his ashes to be scattered in the Venice lagoon.

#56. Typhoons batter East Asia as El Niño makes Pacific 'burn with fever'

Fishermen return to sea under a crimson sky in August after China's Jiangsu province is hit by Super Typhoon Dolphin

Wild weather is a way of life for people living along the coast in the Philippines, Taiwan, China and Japan. But the last few months have been extraordinary.

A wave of intense and, at times, unpredictable storms exacerbated by the El NiÃ±o weather pattern has killed dozens, shuttered major airports and wiped out crops and infrastructure across a swathe of East Asia.

In October, nearly all of Taiwan shut down as a monster storm big enough to engulf the island of 23 million roared towards its north-eastern coast. Equivalent to a category five hurricane, Typhoon Champi's 295km/h (193mph) winds had the power to flatten homes and whip up destructive waves.

Thankfully it did not make landfall in Taiwan, skirting past the island while dousing it in rain. But it had far-reaching impacts: hundreds of flights were cancelled in Hong Kong, Taiwan and Japan. In the Philippines' Batanes archipelago, seaside highways shut as huge waves crashed onto the shore, and in southern Japan's Okinawa islands, power was cut to 15,000 homes.

El NiÃ±o is a naturally occurring climate phenomenon in which warm waters pooling in the eastern Pacific and altered wind patterns drive extreme weather events around the world.

It happens around every two to seven years, lasting for around a year. This year's El NiÃ±o, which the UK Met Office has forecast to be the "strongest in living memory", is likely to last until February or March.

In June, Japan watched two storms - Typhoon Mekkhala and Severe Tropical Storm Higos - race towards its eastern coast simultaneously, forcing one million people to evacuate. In July, Tropical Storm Narra grazed China's southern coast along Guangxi, submerging villages and displacing tens of thousands.

This riverside park in the south-eastern Chinese city of Nanning was submerged by Tropical Storm Narra

August brought Super Typhoon Dolphin, which churned across the Pacific for 16 days - before slamming into eastern China, triggering heavy floods and dozens of casualties.

Later that month Typhoon Saudel made landfall in three south-eastern Chinese cities over one week, shutting transport networks and forcing thousands to evacuate.

The Pacific Ocean, heated to record levels by El NiÃ±o, is now brimming with typhoon energy, according to the Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE) metric which measures the total intensity of a season's storms. This year, the ACE reading for South East and East Asia is forecast to be nearly double the 30-year average.

El NiÃ±o also affects wind patterns and in this case, that means typhoons are tending to form further east than usual. They are also becoming stronger as they remain over warm waters for longer.

The Pacific can be likened to someone "burning with fever", says Ana Liza Solis, head of climatology at the Philippines' national weather bureau. "It can go into convulsions soon," she said at a press briefing in Manila in September.

Forecasts suggest the worst may not be over. Last month, sea surface temperatures in the equatorial Pacific - the area monitored by the Japan Meteorological Agency for its El NiÃ±o predictions - soared to 4C above normal, the highest since records began in 1949. The previous record - 3.6C - was set in December 1997.

China's climate agency has warned of sea surface temperatures continuing to rise over the next three months because of the El NiÃ±o.

Despite typhoon season usually peaking around September to October, this is likely to raise concerns across a region that has already been battered by back-to-back storms.

In August, Typhoon Chan-Hom baffled storm watchers in Japan by crossing the country from east to west, as opposed to the usual south-to-north trajectory. Japan's well-resourced and prepared authorities were able to evacuate people swiftly, but this would be far more challenging in some neighbouring countries.

To get a better picture of how rising temperatures could amplify the risks, Manila-based climatologist Rafaela Jane Delfino and colleagues simulated the effects of warmer conditions on the Philippines' most recent monster storms, including 2013's Super Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda) which killed more than 6,000 people.

They arrived at what she describes as "very, very scary findings": higher temperatures increased total accumulated rainfall by 26%. It's a devastating prospect in a country where flooding from monsoon rains and some 20 tropical storms per year has emerged as one of the main disaster risks.

In recent years, floods have paralysed entire cities for days, and tired of the annual ordeal, Filipinos have taken to the streets to protest against corruption which they say is gnawing away at millions of dollars from flood-control projects.

Delfino, in an article on the University of the Philippines website, insists leaders and officials must gain a better understanding of climate hazards if communities are to be better prepared.

Countries are bracing for the immediate impact. Japan has urged citizens to stockpile food and essentials for the typhoon season, and to secure outdoor furniture that could become deadly projectiles when typhoon winds hit. China has asked local officials to identify vulnerable areas and is upgrading its warning and monitoring systems.

The Philippines and its South East Asian neighbours are preparing for disruptions to food production, especially rice, and have increased support for famers, including the provision of fertilisers.

But what happens in the long term is less clear. The window for governments to act on El NiÃ±o is "closing", the United Nations Development Programme in Asia and the Pacific said recently.

"How severely a community experiences an El NiÃ±o is decided years in advance, by where roads and settlements are built, how water is managed, who is covered by social protection and whether public budgets have room to absorb a shock.

"Every El NiÃ±o tests the resilience we have built; this one should also drive us to build more," it added.

#53. French blood storage impacted by freezer hacks as theories swirl about culprits

French authorities say that fridges used in medical facilities to store blood for donations were impacted by recent cyber-attacks by unknown hackers.

The French Blood Establishment (EFS) said cold rooms storing blood were impacted by hackers, with temperatures set to rise - but staff intervened to protect blood supplies.

"No health impact has been identified and no loss of blood products is to be reported," the EFS said.

Up until now, only supermarkets were known to have been impacted by the hacks.

Meanwhile rumours have been circulating online about Russian hacktivist groups claiming they worked together to hack one of the systems that was used in the attacks which have affected European supermarkets.

Noname057(16) and Z-Pentest Alliance posted that they successfully hacked Danfoss which led to speculation that they were the culprits behind the freezer hacks.

But analysts now question whether or not that meant they were behind the supermarket cyber-attacks.

While Russian groups are not being ruled out, experts say Iranian hacktivists or nation state hackers - or even teenagers pulling pranks - could also be the culprits.

Cyber-security researchers at multiple firms are poring over evidence gathered from so-called "honeypots" to provide clues as to who is behind the waves of freezer hacks.

Three separate cyber-security firms have deployed fake freezer software traps to monitor how hackers attempt to use them for attacks.

In cyber-security, a "honeypot" is a digital decoy - such as a fake computer network or file - deliberately set up to look like a vulnerable target to lure and trap hackers.

By monitoring these traps, security experts can safely study an attacker's methods without putting real systems at risk.

According to activity gathered after the first wave of cyber-attacks, the second wave which hit several European countries was highly targeted.

"The fact that all these fridge freezers were hit simultaneously suggests planning and coordination," says Ryan Dewhurst from Spanish cyber-security company Previdian.

The entry point to the affected freezer software products was not difficult to exploit as they were poorly protected, with known attack methods already floating around online.

But experts like Dewhurst say the hacker or hackers had to gather the information about the devices' public IP addresses to create a target list then take time to plan which methods to use and when to execute them.

"Given the reported timing of when the devices had their temperatures increased, which seems to have been overnight for maximum damage - this is further evidence that the attack was planned and co-ordinated," he said.

Another cyber firm which has deployed similar honeypot tech to lure the hackers into revealing their tactics is Strathclyde-based Lupovis.

It had honeypots which appeared to be located around the world, and started seeing activity 10 days before the second wave of cyber-attacks.

Intriguingly, the team saw hackers scanning and probing their fake freezer sites only in Europe.

"We saw none of our honeypots in places like Africa or South America targeted at all," said Lupovis founder Dr Xavier Bellekens.

A third company - French cyber-defence firm Sekoia - also noticed scanning activity prior to the second wave of cyber-attacks, but said it could have been automatic scanning without malicious intent.

In a report to its customers, it said evidence suggested the nature of the attack aligned with activity of Russian hacktivists or the Russian government, which is being accused of carrying out so-called "hybrid warfare" cyber-attacks across Europe.

But Sekoia's research also points to Iranian regime hackers or hacktivists too.

"Iranian-aligned actors have an established record of targeting internet-exposed industrial control systems and previous incidents involved the same equipment in Israel and the US - both adversaries of Iran," the report said.

According to Sekoia, about 40 Israeli supermarkets running Danfoss controllers were compromised back in 2022.

Meanwhile in late August, freezers in at least six US military bases were forced into defrost mode, as in France. The controller models remain unconfirmed and investigations are ongoing.

"To date, we have no technical evidence indicating the involvement of Russian-speaking groups," a Sekoia spokesperson told the BBC.

"Furthermore, as NoName057 is not known to effectively conduct operations beyond DDoS attacks and has a history of making false attributions, we remain sceptical of their alleged claim of responsibility."

Distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks work by flooding a website or online service with internet traffic in an attempt to throw it offline, or otherwise make it inaccessible.

Authorities in several countries are actively investigating the hacks and warning supermarkets about potential future attacks.

None of the three cyber firms which have been gathering honey pot data are ruling out that it could be a teenager.

But with the degree of planning necessary the experts all agree it would have to be a well co-ordinated group - and if so, they would expect online bragging to surface soon.

Sekoia also said the possibility remains that all three waves could have been carried out by different and distinct groups or copycats.

#46. The US-China trade war seems to have subsided – but the sense of calm is misleading

A truce has cooled the trade conflict, but neither country has done anything to limit the outbreak of new hostilities

Donald Trump’s trade wars have largely fallen off the headlines. Fears of new tariffs flare up from time to time when the US president goes off the rails – for instance, threatening to end US trade with countries with which it has a deficit unless the Federal Reserve cuts interest rates. But the sense that the world’s two largest economies are plowing ahead into an overt economic conflagration has taken a back seat to other, more salient fears, about Trump ending American democracy, AI killing us all and stuff like that.

The sense of calm is misleading. Under the radar, the world is marching toward an economic war of potentially epic proportions, one that, history suggests, could well spill over into the real thing.

The truce reached by Washington and Beijing last year in their escalating trade conflict, which has been extended until next January, lowered the heat between the two – putting an end to the escalation of tit-for-tat tariffs and opening a door to some as yet undefined future trade deal.

But neither China nor the US has done anything to establish the mutual assurances that could limit the outbreak of new hostilities. Rather, they are both focused on gaining the upper hand in what seems like an inevitable next round, in a standoff that both sides have defined as a national security priority, essential to ensure economic, technological and military supremacy.

The new arms race is different from those in the past. Rather than building weapons to blow each other up, China and the US are focusing on ensuring the resilience of the international supply chains on which they rely and figuring out how to constrain – and if push comes to shove, throttle – the supply chains essential to the economic and technological progress of their rival.

The paired strategies go back at least to the Covid pandemic, when China’s Xi Jinping called on the nation to “tighten international production chains’ dependence on China, forming a powerful countermeasure and deterrent capability against foreigners who would artificially cut off supply [to China]”.

Early in the Biden administration, national security adviser Jake Sullivan articulated the American goals: to limit China’s access to a narrow set of advanced technologies with military applications without upending the global economic order. He described the strategy as building a “small yard” of forbidden tech surrounded by a “high fence” of trade and investment controls.

By now, the conflict has breached the bounds of these initial rationales. Restrictions on American tech exports, investments and technology transfers to China have proliferated over the last half decade. And they are hitting not only China’s military capabilities but slowing its technological progress writ large. And Trump’s wanton deployment of tariffs is not helping.

China, on the other hand, is becoming increasingly assertive, leveraging its overwhelming dominance over the supply of critical minerals that are essential to hi-tech industries for civilian and military uses. Last year, it stunned the Trump administration by throttling exports of rare earths and magnets made from them to retaliate against US tariffs and efforts to restrict China’s access to high-end semiconductors and the lithography machines needed to make them.

It has relaxed some of these controls, but it maintains many other restrictions. While they have largely been imposed to retaliate against American actions, they have also been used to hit other countries for a variety of policies it dislikes. The stakes are high. The International Energy Agency has estimated that if China were to block the US’s and Europe’s access to rare earth magnets, it could cost their economies about $1.5tn per year.

Where does the supply chain war go from here? The Trump administration is pouring hundreds of millions into projects to mine and process rare earths and other critical minerals, hoping to close China’s lead. It is also doubling down on efforts to limit China’s access to frontier semiconductors and to bar it from buying the machinery needed to make them.

China, on the other hand, has announced a range of hi-tech export restrictions, covering things such as artificial intelligence chips and solar power technology. Moreover, it has threatened to punish countries that work to reduce their dependence on its mineral inputs.

One hopes there is a path to a peaceful entente. This would probably require some shared understanding of what constitutes a legitimate national security risk that may warrant actions to block supply chains. Defining the “yard” is essential. Frustrations of standard commerce should not be dealt with on a wartime footing.

China will probably dominate renewable energy technologies, especially the solar power industry, into the foreseeable future. It may also dominate the electric vehicle industry. This might hurt in Brussels and Washington, but it’s not worth a war. China, on the other hand, will have to accept that its mercantilistic economic strategy is not tenable for the rest of the world. Its combination of massive export growth with zero import growth is not sustainable.

This might seem doable. And yet the odds that the world economy will remain at peace seem slim. One might recall the Axis powers’ efforts to seize territories as the League of Nations curtailed their access to raw materials in the early stages of the second world war. Or FDR’s blocking of sales of American scrap steel and oil to Japan, which probably contributed to Tokyo’s decision to attack Pearl Harbor. Today, American fighter jets, submarines and missiles use rare earth magnets. What would happen if China blocked access to these materials?

The moment is strange. It’s not that long ago that trade was proposed as perhaps the most powerful tool to deliver world peace, building positive-sum interdependence among nations. That’s over. Interdependence has become a vulnerability. As China and the United States fight over who will control the next generation of technology, trade has become a theater of war.

#19. Fossilised red blood cells found in 115m-year-old dinosaur’s bone, say scientists

Chemical tests on Aratasaurus bone identify presence of heme, the organic compound that allows haemoglobin in blood to carry oxygen around body

It could be the opening of a Jurassic Park rip-off: scientists examining ancient dinosaur bones find what appear to be fossilised red blood cells, and traces of the molecule that gives the beast’s blood its distinctive crimson hue.

But that is what researchers are claiming to have discovered after analysing the hind leg bones of a small, meat-eating dinosaur called an Aratasaurus that lived and died in what is now north-eastern Brazil 115m years ago.

Electron microscope images of the dinosaur bones revealed small, round structures that resemble red blood cells in fine channels where blood vessels once ran, while chemical tests identified the presence of heme, the organic compound that allows haemoglobin in blood to carry oxygen around the body.

Such claims are controversial because biological tissues and molecules can break down fast, leaving little or no trace in ancient fossils. But if confirmed, it would open up entirely new avenues for scientists, allowing them to investigate dinosaur blood, metabolism and even their activity levels.

“We are seeing a mineral and molecular echo of the dinosaur’s original biology,” said Prof Kliti Grice at Curtin University in Western Australia who led the research team. “Fossilised red blood cells with distinct chemistry related to heme have never been found in situ in any fossil, let alone a dinosaur. This is a world first.”

The fossilised remnants of the Aratasaurus were discovered in a mine in 2008 and have since been held in the National Museum of Brazil, where researchers continue to study them. While analysing the dinosaur’s hind leg bones, Grice spotted the structures that resembled red blood cells.

The scientists’ suspicions that they were looking at red blood cells were bolstered when chemical tests came back positive for heme and its breakdown products. Tests also found traces of the amino acid building blocks for collagen, a key protein in connective and other tissues.

If the structures really are fossilised red blood cells, as the researchers contend, it raises the question of how they were preserved. According to Grice, a manganese compound called hausmannite, which formed in and around the blood cells, may have lent a helping hand by stabilising them prior to fossilisation. The same process might have helped preserve the amino acids, she added. The study appears in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

The work is already stoking debate. Prof Mary Schweitzer at North Carolina State University in the US, who has discovered remnants of blood vessels and soft tissues in dinosaur fossils, is not convinced Grice and her colleagues have found red blood cells. One reason for her doubt is that the dinosaur’s red blood cells should have a “nuclear bulge” making them appear dome-like on both sides. The structures Grice found are concave, suggesting they might be something else.

Schweitzer was more persuaded by the discovery of heme and collagen amino acids in the bone and said there was so much more to learn from exceptionally well-preserved fossils. Schweitzer added: “The conventional wisdom in palaeontology that all endogenous organics would disappear in a million years or less is being routinely challenged, and will continue to be as technological approaches to palaeontological specimens are more routinely applied.”

Another question the research raises is what other biological material might lurk in fossils collections around the world. On this, Grice urges extreme caution, adding that her work does not imply that large biological molecules such as proteins or DNA live on in ancient fossils. “Any extraordinary claim needs extraordinary analytical evidence,” she said. “The aim isn’t to find Jurassic Park DNA, it’s to determine how much genuine molecular information deep time can preserve.”

“The really exciting implication is that extraordinary molecular information may already be sitting in museum collections around the world, waiting for us to ask new questions of old fossils,” she added.

#16. Brazil publishes first global ‘roadmap’ for phasing out fossil fuels

Cop30 host sets out steps to break link with coal, oil and gas as it aims to move focus from negotiating to implementing

All countries can start to break their dependence on fossil fuels now without needing to wait for new technologies or economic changes, Brazil’s government has said ahead of a global climate summit.

Brazil, the host of last year’s UN climate conference, set out the world’s first global “roadmap” for phasing out fossil fuels on Tuesday, showing that countries could boost their economies and pull people out of poverty while breaking the link with coal, oil and gas.

But Joeri Rogelj, a professor of climate science and policy at Imperial College London, said that although the Brazilian roadmaps represented a step forward, they were limited. “The roadmaps are valuable but fall short of clearly indicating the pace of change needed by including no timeline or key benchmarks,” he said.

“Because of this omission, they still leave much to be imagined about what is needed to achieve the Paris agreement’s ultimate goal: limiting global warming to well below 2C, and preferably to 1.5C, to avoid dangerous climate harm.”

André Corrêa do Lago, Brazil’s president of the Cop30 summit, said the roadmaps were intended to help move the annual UN Cops away from their previous focus on negotiating global agreements to tackle the climate crisis, and on to implementing the targets that had already been agreed. Cop31 takes place in Antalya, Turkey, this month.

“They are documents of implementation,” Corrêa do Lago told the Guardian in an interview. “There is a different group of people that negotiate and that implement. The problem we’ve had at many Cops is to think that the same people who negotiate might also need to coordinate the implementation – this is not the case.”

He said the roadmaps should be taken up by ministries of finance, transportation, energy and infrastructure to work on how they could be implemented in each country.

Brazil hosted the Cop30 summit last November in the coastal city of Belém, in the Amazon rainforest, where a bitter dispute broke out over whether the final set of resolutions should include pledges on fossil fuels. As a compromise, the presidency agreed to produce two roadmaps, one on “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems in a just orderly and equitable manner” and the other on halting and reversing deforestation.

These roadmaps are separate from similarly named plans from governments that met after Cop30 at a conference in Santa Marta, Colombia, in April, to discuss the transition away from fossil fuels. At that conference, nearly 60 countries formed a new “coalition of the willing”, separate to Cops, to set out their own national roadmaps to phase out fossil fuels. Several countries have since produced national plans, including Germany, France and the Netherlands.

The roadmaps produced by governments under the Santa Marta initiative are much more specific and prescriptive than Brazil’s, as each of those is written by the national government responsible for implementing the policies recommended.

The Brazilian roadmaps only set out steps that are possible. They are not binding, they contain no deadlines or milestones for actions, and they leave it to countries themselves to “diagnose” their degree of dependence on fossil fuels and select steps to reduce it.

“Every country will have a different rhythm [for moving away from fossil fuels] – it’s very important to concentrate on the diagnoses of countries’ circumstances,” Corrêa do Lago said. “Based on these diagnoses, they can choose their levers [of policy] and implement the transition.”

Among the steps Brazil suggests is for oil and gas producers to urgently cut the flaring and venting of gas from extraction sites. “We’ve asked them really nicely and haven’t ever refused them water to drink … But, for the first time ever, we closed the gate at 12 [midday], because we literally had about two buckets of water left,” Bailey said in mid-October.

Police told her that the scale of the illegal mining was too big for them to do anything about, she said at the time. About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. But within a few days a smaller group had returned, although Bailey said they were now taking Claw’s water only for drinking.

South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.

Zama zamas have stepped into those voids left in the earth. While there is no comprehensive data on them, experts agree that a majority are from neighbouring countries, principally Lesotho, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

The skyline south of Johannesburg is punctuated by the pale yellow ridges of mine dumps. Increasingly, the ground is also caving open.

For politicians seeking votes in local elections on 4 November, the culprit is clear: illegal miners. In May, Kenny Kunene, the Johannesburg council member responsible for transport, took journalists on a tour of sinkholes that he claimed were the fault of zama zamas, including a section of Wemmer Pan Road that had completely collapsed.

The road was once the main artery linking the city’s central business district to the south-eastern suburbs. In one section it has completely disappeared, now raw earth pitted with the holes where illegal miners have been digging.

However, when it comes to sinkholes, experts say zama zamas are being scapegoated.

Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, who has worked extensively with illegal miners, noted that the porous dolomitic rock to Johannesburg’s south and west becomes unstable when water is removed.

“The biggest culprit of sinkholes in South Africa is formal industrial mining, which dewaters at a scale of hundreds of millions of gallons of water removed from underground every day,” she said.

“There are areas where there is a lot of residual gold, which are being very intensely mined by zama zamas, and some of that definitely causes structural collapse, there’s no doubt about it. But the idea that this was invented or caused for the first time by zama zamas is completely fallacious.”

Zama zamas are also frequently blamed for violence in mining areas, including a September mass shooting in Wedela township, west of Johannesburg. However, David van Wyk, the lead researcher at Bench Marks Foundation, said culpability laid with the often-feuding gangs that controlled access to the shafts.

“The guys working in the mine are actually super-exploited cheap labour … The guys with the guns are the guys who take the gold,” he said. “They don’t go underground.”

In Khutsong, about 50 miles (80km) west of Johannesburg, illegal miners, local and foreign, kept the township afloat, said Vusi Cele, the youth secretary for the local branch of Mining Affected Communities United in Action, a campaign group that advocates for “artisanal miners” to be granted mining licences. “Without those rents, it’s a ghost town,” he said.

While Cele, 33, currently hustles above ground selling mobile phone covers and screen protectors, he said he too would mine if he could get a licence. “On the surface there’s peanuts,” he said. “Underground there’s money.”
