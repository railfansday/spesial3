#81. Trump’s Oil Grab in Venezuela Is Testing the Country’s National Identity

Long-held beliefs in Venezuela about sovereign control over oil are fraying as the U.S. lays claim to the country’s oil reserves.

Sunrise over the Cardón Refinery in Punta Cardón, Venezuela. Oil has shaped Venezuela to an extent rare outside the Middle East, becoming a defining feature of the country’s social fabric.Credit...Adriana Loureiro Fernandez for The New York Times

Simon Romero is a former Caracas bureau chief who has covered energy politics across Latin America. Isayen Herrera reports on Venezuela for The New York Times.

Like generations before him, Leonardo Silva grew up steeped in beliefs about the extraordinarily abundant resource that made Venezuela special. All the oil under their feet was theirs, they felt. Virtually free gasoline was a birthright. To be born atop this bounty was like winning the lottery.

Mr. Silva, 38, said his generation viewed oil as a cornerstone of the country’s national identity, when a booming economy allowed Venezuela to occupy a privileged tier in the global order. His children, he said, have a far less rosy view.

“To them oil isn’t important, it’s secondary,” said Mr. Silva, who works at a foundation in Caracas helping people with disabilities. “They don’t have that culture of us being a rich country. They just know that if you don’t work, you don’t eat.”

Oil has shaped Venezuela to an extent that is rare outside the Middle East for more than a century, becoming a defining feature of the social fabric like baseball, beauty pageants or the veneration of the liberation hero Simón Bolívar.

But now the U.S. government is taking control of a huge portion of Venezuela’s oil. And, pressed by economic need, long-held nationalist convictions about oil, starting with the need for sovereign control, are giving way to a more complex web of perspectives.

The ways in which Venezuelans are re-evaluating oil, and by extension their reliance on it, are the result of immense upheaval over the last decade. Oil production plummeted because of mismanagement, political purges, corruption and sanctions.

#80. My mother told me I would die if I sought treatment for Ebola - I am proof you can survive

When Dr Raymond Kasereka Kamwanga told his family he suspected he had Ebola and was going to a treatment centre in the city of Butembo in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, his family were horrified.

They regarded the specialised unit as a "death house" as not many patients do recover - especially those admitted in the late stages of the illness. His 50-year-old mother was in tears, pleading with him not to go there.

"Why are you choosing to die at the Ebola treatment centre?" Masika Ndungo had asked him.

But as a doctor at Butembo's Katwa Hospital, he knew that if stayed at home, he was unlikely to survive.

This was in July, two months after the World Health Organization (WHO) had declared the Ebola outbreak in DR Congo a public health emergency of international concern. Since then 5,400 people have died and 11,000 have been infected, making it the second-most deadly outbreak ever recorded.

Butembo is in North Kivu province, the epicentre of the crisis, with the highest number of reported cases, according to the Congolese health ministry.

Dr Kamwanga is one of 3,031 people who have survived - and is evangelical about sharing his story in an effort to help stop the spread of infections devastating his community.

After staring death in the face and overcoming ostracisation, he has even started writing a book about his experience.

"As someone who has recovered, I am living proof that the disease exists, but also that it is possible to recover from it," he tells the BBC outside Katwa Hospital where he still works.

The 30-year-old medic believes he contracted the virus through contact with a patient in a setting where protective measures are not always adequate.

Raymond Kasereka Kamwanga uses his book to help convince sceptics that Ebola is real

The first symptoms appeared gradually: a bitter taste in his mouth, headaches and a general feeling of malaise. He contacted the Ebola response team.

After an initial consultation and some tests, he returned home, but his condition did not improve.

Butembo does not have the equipment to quickly test for Ebola - samples need to be sent to the city of Beni, 54km (33 miles) north.

This delays diagnosis as the condition of roads in this war-torn and underdeveloped part of DR Congo means it can take between 48 and 72 hours to travel even this relatively short distance.

After three days at home, Kamwanga felt extremely weak and experienced muscle pain. On the fourth day, he began vomiting and had diarrhoea.

This is when he gathered his family members - including his mother - to tell them he had to go to the Ebola treatment centre.

"She had started crying, along with my sisters. I told them everything would be alright, that I was a doctor and that I was confident. But they weren't reassured," he says.

Around 18:00, he set off on foot to the nearby centre but refused to use the main entrance.

He called his colleagues and asked them to put on their protective gear before they approached him and isolated him. The virus spreads from one person to another by contact with infected bodily fluids.

Dr Kamwanga's mother says his experience has helped dispel some of the misinformation she had heard about Ebola

The current outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo species of the virus for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment.

"The treatment given to Ebola patients is symptomatic - meaning that doctors treat the symptoms the patient presents," says Kamwanga, explaining that in his case it was severe vomiting and diarrhoea.

In some patients these symptoms can lead to organ failure and then some develop internal and external bleeding.

Kamwanga lost a lot of fluid and struggled to stay hydrated - he was terrified.

"I was afraid for myself. Every day, patients were dying right before my eyes. But I stayed positive," he explains.

Over 13 days lying in his hospital bed, he tried to keep his spirits up by watching movies and TV shows on his phone, reading and scrolling through TikTok.

The day he learned he had recovered, Kamwanga felt immense relief. He thanked the healthcare workers who had cared for him.

However, even a certificate showing he was Ebola-free did not initially dispel the unease those around him felt after he was discharged.

"We welcomed him home warmly. But because of our fear, we didn't get close to him any more," Ndungo, his mother, tells the BBC.

For 21 days - the incubation period of the virus - the doctor lived separately in another part of the family home, which was crowded at the time with at least 18 people living there, including several children and people displaced by recent fighting in the region.

He would only see them from afar when he went to pick up his meal, while other family members kept their distance.

Neighbour Kavugho Sifa shared these concerns.

"We were really afraid, but we prayed to God to protect him at the treatment centre. With God's help and the doctors' efforts, we saw him return in good health," she tells the BBC.

They all agree the experience changed their perception of Ebola.

Some do not believe the disease even exists - Ndungo says that witnessing Ebola first-hand helped to dispel some of that misinformation.

After his recovery, Kamwanga threw himself into community outreach - sharing his story as a way of combatting rumours and mistrust.

"I started writing my book - The Price of Saving Lives - one week after recovering and leaving the hospital," he says. "However, it is not finished yet. What I currently use during my awareness-raising activities is the draft, and it is already making a significant impact."

During these sessions, he also distributes photos taken while he was sick to help convince residents that Ebola is real.

But in Butembo, this mission remains difficult. Kamwanga explains that some people view awareness campaigns as a way for humanitarian organisations to profit from donor funding.

Such mistrust, which is also levelled at healthcare professionals, is often fuelled by poverty and trauma related to years of armed conflict, he says.

"Ebola has come alongside the war," he says, explaining that some even interpret it as a way for those in authority to threaten a vulnerable population "to finish off those whom the war failed to exterminate".

He also believes that the number of deaths observed during epidemics - compared to recoveries, which are sometimes less visible - reinforce fear and suspicion.

But putting himself on the front line also comes with risks - and Kamwanga says he receives death threats on his phone.

A university that Viktor Orbán tried to chase out of Hungary is open to expanding its presence in Budapest, in a mark of confidence in the country’s democratic future.

Carsten Q Schneider, the interim president and rector of Central European University (CEU), said it was interested in increasing its programmes in Budapest, as long as this did not damage the student experience.

So far the CEU has not held formal talks with Hungary’s new government, which has promised to restore academic freedom, “but our hand is reaching out, their hand is reaching out – it is just that they have many things to do”, the academic told the Guardian.

The CEU relocated its main activities to Vienna from Budapest in 2019, after pressure from the previous government led by Orbán. But the outlook changed when Péter Magyar won a landslide victory in April pledging to root out corruption and reform the state.

The CEU, founded by Hungarian-American financier and philanthropist George Soros in 1991 to entrench democratic change in the region, was forced to cease awarding degrees in Budapest in response to a law widely seen as targeting the institution.

The EU’s highest court struck down Hungary’s “Lex CEU” in 2020, finding that it violated the EU charter of fundamental rights, but the verdict came too late for the university. The CEU retains 312 staff in Budapest, mostly administrative, but the majority, 476, and all its teaching, are in Vienna.

Schneider said upgrading the status of the CEU, which is privately funded, in Budapest would be “a symbol for academic freedom [and] of resilience” but depended on goodwill and funding independent of the Hungarian government. He said a decision on its future in Hungary would be taken by the CEU’s 13-member board, whose members include Soros and his son, Alexander Soros.

“We don’t want special treatment,” Schneider said. ‘“We are a great asset, so let’s work out something.”

But the CEU, which he said was “thriving tremendously” in Vienna, does not plan a wholesale return to Budapest. Any change would have to ensure the quality of the student experience, for instance, assuring that students would be able to combine courses, say in economics and history, without having to travel the 200km-plus distance between the two cities.

Focused on social sciences, law and humanities, the CEU has more than 1,400 students from 103 countries, mostly doing master’s and PhD programmes.

According to Schneider, for the first time in the CEU’s 25-year history, students from the US now make up the largest cohort of master’s students (10%). The CEU has seen an increase in US applicants since Donald Trump returned to the White House and launched a sweeping campaign against elite universities, including slashing federal funding and seeking to exert control over internal policies.

The CEU rector has had informal talks with US academics on how to maintain academic freedom under pressure from rightwing populists. He described his message as: “Don’t be naive for a second. These people have a plan, so it’s not by accident that they attack universities. They want to have cultural hegemony.” He added, however, that Trump and allied movements, such as Germany’s Alternative für Deutschland, have identified “real problems”, such as in the US how universities have entrenched “a reproduction of elites”

When coming under political pressure, he said universities needed “to look for allies”, among other academic institutions, students, alumni and parents. Institutions also had to stand up for disciplines under attack, such as gender studies, Schneider added.

#75. UK-EU breakthrough will allow airport-style checks at Eurotunnel to avoid border ‘chaos’

Eurotunnel owner Getlink wants to replicate ‘airport experience’ at Folkestone and Calais, without making people get out of cars

Airport-style passport and fingerprinting controls are to be introduced at Eurotunnel in an attempt to prevent “short-term and long-term chaos” in Folkestone and Calais.

In a breakthrough for travellers, the European Commission has agreed to a new system in which an “eagle eye” camera system, already used in Rome and Paris airports to supervise fingerprinting and facial imaging, will be used at the border between the UK and France.

Yann Leriche, the chief executive of Getlink, the Eurotunnel owner, said it had tried to replicate the “airport experience” but wanted to go much further.

Instead of getting out of the car and getting biometrics taken at a terminal building, holidaymakers and business travellers will now be greeted with special drive-through terminals in Folkestone and Calais.

Both sides are now equipped with 120 kiosks under the case of an eagle-eye camera that will be monitored by police, similar to e-gates at airports where police are not physically present at each gate.

In the coming weeks, Leriche will meet UK and French authorities presenting a 40-page proposal to allow Eurotunnel to become a “laboratory” for an “intelligent border” using emerging tech and lessons learned from the US and Singapore.

The entry and exit system (EES) requires all third-country citizens including British passport holders to submit fingerprints and facial images as they enter and exit the Schengen area, but has been delayed for years.

EES was supposed to be active across the EU by 9 September, but nine of the 27 EU member states, including France, have yet to be deploy it fully because of continued technical issues.

It has always been a logistical nightmare for Eurotunnel and a potential cause of miles-long tailbacks at the approach to the terminal.

Originally, Getlink had planned to use a tablet to hand to the passengers in their cars to allow them to self-fingerprint and capture a facial image. It also looked at a system involving passengers parking up and going into the terminal to get fingerprinting.

But both models would have required the physical presence of police officers for first-time fingerprinting.

Leriche said: “We told the authorities: it is going to be a mess. Many people are going to the wrong place and you can’t have police jumping from one place to the next to solve the issues. It would also means customers would have a bad experience, particularly at peak time.

“This is not something people in airports experience, but nobody approached this before, thinking we could have an airport experience at the border here. But we said: why not?”

#12. Choking fires, deadly heat and devastating floods: 2026 in extreme weather

People around the world have contended with unprecedented weather events and their fatal effects caused by the climate catastrophe

After the world’s hottest summer since records began in 1850, the death toll from extreme weather in 2026 is already in the high tens of thousands. That is a conservative estimate and is certain to rise much higher.

As Cop31 continues and scientists warn that climate breakdown may be accelerating, the Guardian looks back at a year of droughts, fires and floods as a reminder that, unless the world takes urgent action to cut emissions, this disastrous year will be one of the best of the rest of our lives.

Extreme heat fuels wildfires in Australia As temperatures jump nearly 20C (68F) above average, Victoria and other southern states register new heat records, including 50C in Port Augusta and the tiny opal mining town of Andamooka. Melbourne hospitals report a 25% increase in heat-related illnesses, while authorities in many areas struggle with wildfires. Scientists say the supercharged climate has made the heatwave five times more likely. Heatwaves already cause more deaths in Australia than all other natural hazards combined.

Mudslides kill 72 in Brazil after record rain The city of Juiz de Fora in Minas Gerais records its wettest ever month, triggering landslides that sweep away hundreds of homes in poor communities on unstable hillsides. Floods also disrupt Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo. More than 10,000 people are displaced.

Devastating floods across much of the world In Ethiopia, at least 131 people are killed in floods and mudslides that bury entire villages. Eighty die in Kenya and 18 in Tanzania. The Arabian peninsula is battered by fierce winds and heavy downpours that flood the streets of the desert kingdom. Hailstones the size of tennis balls fall in Oman and lightning storms leave cities in the UAE inundated with water. Many areas report record rainfall. At least 30 die in Afghanistan as heavy rains trigger landslides. Hong Kong is soaked in twice the usual monthly precipitation. Flood waters in southern Russia submerge homes and leave more than 300,000 without electricity. Huge tracts of farmland are left underwater in Argentina and Chile.

World’s energy imbalance accelerates rapidly The World Meteorological Organization reveals an alarming buildup of energy in Earth’s system. More than 90% is stored in the oceans, where warming has more than doubled over the past two decades compared with the average over the previous 45 years. This makes weather more extreme and threatens health and food supplies. António Guterres, the UN secretary general, says: “The global climate is in a state of emergency. Planet Earth is being pushed beyond its limits. Every key climate indicator is flashing red.” Guterres urges the world to cut fossil fuel emissions as rapidly as possible to minimise future risks. However, CO2 in the atmosphere continues to rise.

Super typhoon tears through Micronesia, Guam and Saipan At peak intensity on 12 April, Sinlaku registers a wind speed of 295km/h (183mph) – roughly the same as a Shinkansen bullet train and the joint highest recorded by a storm in the western Pacific basin so early in the year. It is also the deadliest in decades, killing 12, leaving 43,000 without power, forcing mass evacuation and causing $1.5bn (£1.1bn) in damages. This will remain one of the most powerful storms of 2026, superseded only by Super Typhoon Champi that will strike Taiwan and Japan in October.

Hundreds of millions endure deadly heat in south Asia At least 47 heat-related deaths are reported in southern Asia as temperatures soar above 45C in several Indian and Bangladeshi cities, and hit a peak of 52C in Pakistan’s southern province of Sindh. Ocean heat enters uncharted territory Sea surface temperature graphs begin to jag upwards and away from all previous highs on 21 June, starting a trend that will worsen over the rest of the year. This alarms scientists because oceans absorb more than 90% of the excess energy in Earth’s system. If the seas start to release that heat then the land and air will get warmer more quickly. This coincides with the emergence of the latest El Niño, which is confirmed on 2 June.

Arctic and Antarctic swelter in unprecedented warmth Instead of the usual winter snow and ice, scientists at the Esperanza base on the Trinity peninsula of Antarctica are astonished to see rain and dirt outside their windows after an eight-day heatwave that peaked with a record temperature of 15C on 6 June. At the other end of the planet, the Arctic experiences its warmest ever June. Beliy Island, Siberia, registers 28.3C on 25 June, the highest temperature ever recorded that far north in the Eurasian Arctic.

West African deluge takes heavy toll Relentless rains claim almost 100 lives in west Africa from May to June. Record downpours of up to 140mm (5.5in) in a single day are made five times more likely and 23% more intense by the human-caused climate crisis. Floods and mudslides kill 59 in Côte d’Ivoire, 35 in Ghana and five in Togo.

The festival is part of See My World’s year-round programme showcasing pan-African arts, culture and events.

It reflects the determination among creatives and activists in Manchester to build on the legacy of the 1945 Pan-African Congress in Manchester – which connected luminaries of postwar independence movements with a shared vision of liberation – after last year’s 80th anniversary commemorations.

See My World’s CEO and artistic director, Tunde Adekoya, said this month’s film festival was taking inspiration from across the spectrum of pan-African thought, including the works of Kenyan writers John Mbiti, an Anglican theologian who died in 2019, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, the author of the groundbreaking 1986 book Decolonising the Mind who died last year.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.

Adekoya said: “It’s a day dedicated to pan-African and global majority films from the north of England with exclusive premieres of documentaries we’ve been working on, which weave together expressions of liberation through art forms like poetry and dance.
