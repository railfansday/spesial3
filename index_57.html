#76. Suspected Chinese spy balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing

The Chinese spy balloon that transited the US earlier this year was able to capture imagery and collect some signals intelligence from US military sites, a source familiar with the matter tells CNN.

The balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing in real time, the source said, and the US government still does not know for sure whether the Chinese government could wipe the balloon’s data as it received it. That raises questions about whether there is intelligence the balloon was able to gather that the US still doesn’t know about.

Still, the intelligence community has not been overly concerned about the information the balloon was able to gather, the person said, because it is not much more sophisticated than what Chinese satellites are able to glean as they orbit over similar locations.

A US intelligence official told CNN on Monday that “although analysis of the wreckage of the High Altitude Balloon remains ongoing, thus far, its flight over the United States does not appear to have provided critical new insights to the People’s Republic of China.”

The US also knew what the balloon’s path would be and was able to protect sensitive sites and censor some signals before the balloon was able to pick them up, officials have said.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

The balloon first crossed into US airspace over Alaska in late January before passing through Canada and down into Montana, where it hovered for a few days, leading the US to believe it was trying to surveil sensitive military sites, like Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana. It was eventually shot down by the US off the East Coast on February 4, and the incident further escalated tensions between Washington and Beijing, including the postponement of a diplomatic visit by US Secretary of State Antony Blinken to China.

A senior State Department official said in February that, as the balloon floated across the US, it “was capable of conducting signals intelligence collection operations.”

Gen. Glen VanHerck, the commander of US Northern Command and NORAD, said at the time that the US “did not assess” that the balloon “presented a significant collection hazard beyond what already exists in actionable technical means from the Chinese.”

The surveillance program, which includes a number of similar balloons, is in part run out of the small Chinese province of Hainan, officials told CNN. The US does not know the precise size of the fleet of Chinese surveillance balloons, but sources tell CNN that the program has conducted at least two dozen missions over at least five continents in recent years.

Roughly half a dozen of those flights have been within US airspace, although not necessarily over US territory, according to one official familiar with the intelligence.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

#73. Russia's own figures show it has lost nearly 500,000 people in Ukraine war

Russia does not provide numbers of its fallen soldiers

At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since the start of the war in Ukraine in 2022, according to Russian government statistics analysed by the BBC.

The leaked Rosstat data reveal how the full-scale invasion of Ukraine has fundamentally changed mortality patterns in Russia, and show the high cost paid in human life in a war that Vladimir Putin had assumed would last only days but is now almost five years old.

BBC Russian has had access to more than 11 million death records from 2021 to September 2026, after a hack by Belarusian group Cyber Partisans BY and Ukraine's Silent Crow.

Russia's statistics agency admitted to a cyber-attack, but denied any protected information had been leaked.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on Wednesday that only Russia's defence ministry could officially report data on losses: "I suggest no other data be taken into account." The defence ministry has not released any official casualty figures for several years.

Together with independent Russian outlet Mediazona, BBC Russian has compared the Rosstat data with its own list of confirmed military deaths, pulled from open sources since the start of the invasion, and it has found matches for 90% of the records.

Military experts estimate that the BBC's list of 290,012 deaths, gleaned from official reports, social media, new memorials and graves, may account for 45-55% of the true death toll, which would range from 527,295 to 644,471.

The records show that 65% of 18 to 24 year-olds in Russia who lost their lives in 2025 died in the war. For those aged 25-34 the number was 59%. Only among 45 to 54 year-olds does cardiovascular disease claim more Russian lives than the conflict.

The data also reveal the extent of losses in specific areas of Ukraine. Some 20,000 Russian soldiers died fighting for control of 10 Ukrainian villages whose combined pre-war population was 12,000.

During the fight for one small village called Andriivka, south of the town of Bakhmut in Donetsk region, 1,122 Russians lost their lives. Before the war 74 people lived there.

Russia does not give public figures for losses sustained since the war began in February 2022. And two years ago Rosstat stopped publishing data of deaths from external causes, which was used to gauge the extent of military losses.

For example, only 70% of deaths we have established of soldiers from the occupied regions of Donetsk and Luhansk in eastern Ukraine who fought in the Russian army were accounted for in the Rosstat files.

Overall, the Rosstat dataset confirms the trends that we have observed with our own count.

Ukrainian losses were last put at about 50,000 by President Volodymyr Zelensky in July 2026, but another list based on open sources by the anonymous UA Losses website puts the figure at 115,952.

The Rosstat dataset contains no names but does include ages, places of residence, places of death and also circumstances of death.

We have filtered the records first by location, leaving only Ukraine as well as Russian border regions that saw incursions by the Ukrainian army.

Next, we identified 425,174 entries explicitly indicating a military-related cause of death, such as explosions or shrapnel during combat operations.

As this count could include civilians, we filtered out women as well as men outside the 18-65 age group. And if someone lived and died in the four Ukrainian regions occupied by Russia, we excluded them as a possible civilian â unless he had a record of military service attached to his entry.

Another 14,093 entries were identified by looking at employment, and 51,067 were recorded in the database as deceased on the basis of a court ruling. This often happens when a soldier goes missing in a combat zone for more than six months and their body has not been recovered.

Again, we filtered out civilians by only counting deaths in the war zones in Ukraine and Russia.

According to BBC Russian's analysis of the leaked files, in the first year of the war the average age of those killed was 34. By 2025, as prisoners and contract soldiers entered the ranks, it had risen to 40 (38 according to our own list).

Some of Russia's poorest regions in Eastern Siberia account for a disproportionately large share of the losses. Men from Tyva, Buryatia and Zabaykalsky Krai are 20-25 times more likely to die in the war than residents of the biggest cities of Moscow and St Petersburg.

In most regions, the distribution of deaths based on Rosstat data differs from that in the BBC's list by just a few tenths of a percentage point.

At least 5,017 foreigners are among those who died fighting for Russia, according to our analysis of the Rosstat files, including 1,031 Uzbeks and 720 Tajiks. We have left out Ukrainians from this count to avoid including civilians.

This is a conservative estimate as dual nationals are listed as Russians in the database, and there is no reference to North Koreans who died repelling the Ukrainian incursion into Russia in 2024.

The BBC identified 2,304 North Korean soldiers who died there by analysing satellite imagery and official photographs of a newly built memorial in Pyongyang.

The leaked Russian statistics may not be complete but taken in conjunction with BBC Russian's own work they are significant.

In the context of a war that has lasted four years and 10 months, these are high numbers for Russia, when compared with the Soviet war in Afghanistan that ended in 1989.

In that 10-year war, an estimated 15,000 Red Army troops died, and an estimated one million civilians were killed.

The woman who sent me the photo says the stakes mark the graves of my grandparents, killed as the Ukrainian-Russian front line reached our little community. Not far away, in another garden in the village, the body of my father, Serezha, is buried in a greenhouse.

I cover the Russia-Ukraine war for the BBC World Service, but found myself in the middle of that story in February last year when my mother sent me a devastating text message.

"Granny, grandad and Serezha are gone. Now there is no-one left," it said.

Because not only had I lost three beloved family members, my mother blamed Ukraine for their deaths - the country where I was born and have lived for most of my life.

Mum was born in Siberia. From the moment Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, she had supported Russia in the conflict.

Backing Moscow is not that unusual in the Donbas region - the predominantly Russian-speaking part of eastern Ukraine where my village Dachne is located.

After pro-Russian forces captured large parts of the Donbas in 2014, many of those who stayed supported Moscow over Kyiv, a stance cemented by exposure to Russian state TV.

Now the Russians occupy almost 80% of Donetsk - the part of the Donbas where my village is. Over the past nearly five years, since Russia's full-scale invasion, entire towns and cities have been razed to the ground, external.

I had already fallen out with my sister Sasha, who had been living in Moscow with her young family when the war broke out. She refused to take sides - I felt hurt and cut her off.

As for my grandparents, my investigations suggested the events surrounding their deaths may have been more complicated.

Their house, on the eastern side of Dachne, had been destroyed. Neighbours told me they had escaped to another home on the same street.

I eventually reached a woman I'll call Lina, who lived nearby and was the last person, as far as I know, who saw my grandparents alive.

She could not recall what this date was, but knew it was in the second half of January.

At this time, she said, "the Russians were fully advancing - the edges of the village were being shelled. Then the Ukrainian soldiers moved from the edge of the village into the centre. They hid in people's yards and used side streets to avoid drones."

She said two soldiers entered her property. They had not been wearing any identifying insignia but had been speaking Ukrainian, she said - a language that Lina described as "Bandera", a derogatory term for Ukrainians.

"You're going to have to leave. Why are you still here?" Lina said they had asked her.

She said one had pointed a machine gun at her as she had stood outside her house, while another had gone to see my grandparents. She told me she had feared for her life and then fled her house for the night.

The following day, she told me she had returned to my grandparents' street to check on them.

She had found the door to the house they had been staying in open, she said, despite the freezing conditions.

With drones buzzing overhead, Lina said she had not dared look inside, but had later heard from neighbours that my grandparents had been found dead. She believed it had been the Ukrainian soldiers who visited her house who had shot them.

Another neighbour, who I will call Maria, described discovering their bodies - my grandmother in bed, covered in a blanket, and my grandfather lying on the floor in a pool of now-dried blood.

Maria also believed Ukrainian soldiers had killed them - because, she said, that she too had felt threatened by Ukrainian troops at the time.

"If they had seen me they would have killed me too."

Her theory was that my grandparents had irritated the troops in some way.

"Your grandparents caught them in a bad mood because your grandfather was always asking to be evacuated. And [the soldiers] were, you know, high. They were taking drugs. So your grandparents got in their way," she told me.

Most of the other people in the village I spoke to also believe Ukrainian soldiers killed them, though their testimony is not based on direct evidence.

The new local mayor, appointed by the Russian occupiers when they finally took control of Dachne in February, said she had been present at my grandparents' burial the following month, and said half of my grandmother's skull was missing.

These accounts of how they might have died in January 2025 were deeply disturbing, though no-one had witnessed their deaths and the information I was given could not be independently verified.

The villagers I spoke to - now living in occupied territory - did say there had been some tensions between themselves and Ukrainian troops at the time, because the soldiers had viewed those who remained in Dachne as supporters of Russia.

The clinical causes of my grandparents' deaths are unclear.

Their bodies were not exhumed for nearly 20 months, when the local authorities sent them to a morgue in the occupied region, where a basic autopsy was carried out by a forensics expert.

He told me that my grandmother had died of an explosives injury to the skull - but could not specify its cause.

As for my grandfather, it said his body had shown no obvious signs of injury.

I compared two satellite images of the house in which their bodies were found - from just before and just after when they are believed to have died - and could detect no significant damage to the building in the second image.

I also spoke to a villager who said he had buried my grandparents.

He said he did not know anything about who killed them.

"I wasn't there. But why would you kill old people?" he asked.

My colleagues helped me identify the Russian military units that went on to capture Dachne. We found telephone numbers of Russian soldiers. But none of them agreed to speak to me.

A soldier from the 33rd Mechanised Brigade, who gave his name simply as Vlad, said his unit had still been in Dachne at the time my grandparents were believed to have died and that the Russians had been swiftly advancing.

Another Ukrainian soldier I spoke to also confirmed Ukraine's troops had still been in control of some parts of Dachne in late January.

We put our findings on the deaths of my grandparents to the Ukrainian military.

It said it had no verified evidence on how they died, or which implicated its servicemen during the "intense hostilities". It added that investigating allegations of wrongdoing was the role of law enforcement.

We also approached Russia's ministry of defence and Russia's embassy in London for comment - they did not respond.

"Are you ready?" Sasha asks. She rings our mother - who has also moved to Serbia - and puts the phone on speaker.

"Hi, Mum. It's Nastya," I say. First there is silence. Then I hear her crying. "Nastya, my darling, hello," she replies.

#22. Has global jihadism failed, or is it simply changing shape?

Has global jihadism failed, or is it simply changing shape?

Rice, pasta, sugar, cooking oil and beans are hardly what you'd expect to see in jihadist propaganda. Yet these were among the supplies that al-Qaeda's Somali branch, al-Shabab, said its "charity" had handed out to hundreds of struggling families in August, as it publicised the aid through its social media accounts and radio station.

The group, which controls large parts of rural southern and central Somalia, has been keen to show off its so-called humanitarian work throughout 2026.

"May God reward them," said a local woman in one of al-Shabab's videos.

It's all part of an effort to win over local communities and present itself as more than just a jihadist group.

Al-Shabab has been publicising its humanitarian work

A quarter of a century after al-Qaeda's 9/11 attacks in the US, and 12 years after Islamic State group (IS) declared its so-called caliphate in Iraq and Syria, the jihadist movement looks very different.

Rather than focusing on attacking the West or fighting for a global Islamic state, some - especially those linked to al-Qaeda - are putting more effort into winning power closer to home. And they seem increasingly willing to make compromises along the way.

Because of this, a debate is growing within the movement. While hardliners reject compromise, pragmatists see working with local groups and former enemies as a way to gain power. But do the pragmatists represent a genuine change or simply a shift in tactics to achieve the same ultimate goals: establishing a state that applies their strict interpretation of Islamic Sharia law both at home and in its dealings with the outside world?

Neither al-Qaeda nor IS has achieved its ultimate dream: taking over one or more countries and ruling them according to their strict interpretation of Islam.

Both have suffered major defeats, lost leaders and faced years of international pressure. IS, which once controlled vast areas of Iraq and Syria, lost its last territorial stronghold in 2019. But jihadism hasn't gone away.

Africa is now the main battleground for jihadism, with al-Qaeda's powerful branches, JNIM in the Sahel (Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger) and al-Shabab in Somalia, fighting long-running insurgencies. IS, its bitter rival, has active branches in Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Sahel, Mozambique and Somalia.

One major source of inspiration, and envy, whether jihadists admit it or not, is Syria's new president, Ahmed al-Sharaa. The former IS and then al-Qaeda commander was once wanted by the US but rose to power in Syria in December 2024 and was welcomed by Western leaders.

Once known by his militant nom de guerre Abu Mohammed al-Jolani, he led the jihadist group Nusra Front - initially linked to IS and then al-Qaeda - and later the group Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS).

He gradually broke ties with both IS and al-Qaeda and distanced himself from their calls for global jihad.

Instead, he focused on Syria, building a local power base in the north-western Idlib province.

What surprised everyone was his growing willingness to work with non-Islamist groups and engage with foreign powers. As HTS advanced on Damascus, its leadership sought to reassure the international community, reaching out even to Russia, a key ally of then-president Bashar al-Assad, to allay fears of an Islamist takeover.

That pragmatism, combined with favourable geopolitical conditions - including a weaker Russia and Iran - helped him take power in Syria.

But the sight of him being welcomed by world leaders, including US President Donald Trump, was even more remarkable.

Trump joked with Sharaa when he visited the White House

During Sharaa's visit to the White House in November 2025, Trump sprayed him with his own branded cologne, jokingly asked how many wives he had and praised him as a "very strong leader".

For some jihadists, Sharaa's success has been inspiring. Others, however, see his actions as a betrayal of everything they believe in.

IS has declared jihad against Syria's new Islamist government, branding Sharaa and his officials "apostates", and has carried out attacks against government forces on that basis. Other hardline clerics have taken a similar stance, but have stopped short of openly calling for jihad against Sharaa.

The divide between hardline jihadists and their more pragmatic counterparts has fuelled a bitter online debate since 2025, with rival voices trading accusations of betrayal and even publishing competing books to defend their positions.

Sharaa, centre, in 2016, was once a jihadist leader wanted by the FBI

For hardliners, going too far can mean abandoning their core beliefs. This might include holding back from imposing their strict version of Islamic law, accepting "man-made" laws or a constitution, taking part in elections, working with non-Islamist groups, or building ties with governments they consider "infidel".

Another source of inspiration for jihadists was the Taliban's return to power in Afghanistan in 2021, as it showed that an Islamist movement could eventually take control of a country after years of fighting Western-backed forces. It engaged local groups, slowly secured power across the country, used international diplomacy, and bided its time before taking the capital, Kabul, in a lightning offensive.

Two groups are especially worth watching: al-Qaeda's branches in Mali and Somalia.

In late April, al-Qaeda's Sahel branch, known as JNIM, showed considerable pragmatism by joining forces with nationalist Tuareg separatists - whom it had occasionally fought - to launch a major offensive that threatened Mali's military rulers. It even went as far as to call the Tuaregs "brothers".

But it's not just JNIM's growing military strength that's attracting attention. It's also how the group is presenting itself.

Its propaganda once prominently featured al-Qaeda's leaders, slogans and calls for global jihad. Since late 2024, however, its videos have dropped the familiar al-Qaeda branding.

And instead of talking about global Muslim causes, JNIM has increasingly focused on local problems, portraying itself as a defender of ordinary people against corrupt and abusive rulers.

It has repeatedly accused Mali's military government and its Russian allies of killing civilians and making people's lives worse.

In April, it appealed to "all sincere patriots" to help build a "new Mali", promising security, justice and prosperity alongside Islamic law.

It has even suggested it could have "balanced relations" with Russia, despite the fact they're fighting Russian forces backing Mali's government. Its wording echoed that of HTS to Russia as it advanced on Damascus.

There are parallels with Sharaa's approach in Syria: build local alliances, reassure potential enemies and avoid giving outside powers a reason to intervene.

Africa security specialist Aman Bezreh sees similarities between the two groups, but says JNIM's shift may take time.

"JNIM has indeed shown signs of pragmatism similar to HTS", she says. "However, its rebranding has progressed more slowly because the group is simultaneously focused on expanding into new territories and recruiting from diverse pools."

The debate over which path to follow is also taking place within JNIM's leadership, according to Wassim Nasr, a France 24 journalist and jihadism expert.

He says one camp favours the Taliban's approach of taking power through force with minimal concessions. The other, which he says currently has the upper hand, favours an approach closer to that of Syria's HTS - making compromises and building wider alliances to gain power more quickly.

Nasr believes JNIM may borrow from both models, but that a break with al-Qaeda is inevitable as a result of its political evolution.

Like JNIM, al-Shabab in Somalia appears willing to make deals with former enemies.

According to Abdullahi Guji, BBC Monitoring's Somalia specialist, al-Shabab reached a truce in October with a powerful Somali tribe, the Ma'awisley, whose fighters had been at the forefront of the fight against the group.

The tribe has since withdrawn its fighters from the front line, dealing a serious blow to the government's campaign against jihadists, says Guji.

Guji believes the group may soon have a chance to test whether it can seize power and establish itself as an alternative government across wider parts of the country.

African Union forces are withdrawing from Somalia amid funding cuts and the approaching end of their mandate in December. Meanwhile, the Somali government is facing political crises and rebellions, creating vulnerabilities al-Shabab could exploit, he adds.

There's another reason some jihadists may see an opportunity to expand: they believe Western governments have bigger fish to fry.

With the war in Ukraine, US-Iran conflict in the Middle East and growing rivalries with China and Russia, fighting jihadists is no longer the priority it was after 9/11.

The US withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021 and the Taliban's return to power, followed by Sharaa's rise in Syria, have reinforced that belief.

Colin Clarke, executive director of the Soufan Center, a non-profit research group that focuses on security, says some jihadist groups have learnt that avoiding confrontation with the US can help them escape Western military action and "live to fight another day".

That doesn't mean the West has stopped fighting terrorism. But if jihadists believe it is distracted or less willing to intervene, they may feel emboldened to pursue power.

Unlike its fiercely uncompromising rival IS, al-Qaeda has long shown some flexibility in pursuing its goals. But history offers a warning.

After the 2011 Arab uprisings, which largely sidelined al-Qaeda, affiliated jihadist groups in Yemen, Libya and Tunisia began using the name Ansar al-Sharia (Supporters of Sharia) to win local support without carrying the al-Qaeda brand.

In Mali in 2012, al-Qaeda leaders advised jihadists who had seized the north alongside Tuareg separatists not to rush into imposing strict Islamic rules. Instead, they urged them to work with local allies, avoid alienating communities and play down their al-Qaeda links.

The message wasn't to give up their ambitions. It was to show strategic patience until the time was right. That same thinking could be at work today.

So are these groups really changing their ambitions, or just their tactics? Even those advocating compromise aren't necessarily abandoning their dream of establishing a state ruled by their strict interpretation of Islamic law.

Clarke wonders whether this more pragmatic approach could be a "Trojan horse strategy" â a way for jihadist groups to gain power without giving up their longer-term goals.

"The big question is whether being in power actually moderates these groups, or whether they're simply changing their tactics to survive and strengthen their position," he says.

IS remains firmly committed to its global ambitions and rejects compromise. Al-Qaeda's Yemen branch, AQAP, continues to call for attacks against the US and Israel, and urges "lone wolf" attacks against Western and Jewish targets.

And it's too early to say whether groups like JNIM and al-Shabab will follow the Taliban or Syrian examples, and whether they're willing to make compromises.

Some jihadist groups may become more focused on local politics as they face the challenges of governing. Others may simply be hiding their wider ambitions until they're strong enough to pursue them.

The global jihadist dream may have failed, but jihadism is far from finished.

#12. Images of asteroid targeted to test Earth's defences reveal stadium-sized crater

The asteroid before (left) and after, showing the crater left behind by a spacecraft's impact

The first images of an asteroid that Nasa deliberately crashed into have revealed a stadium-sized crater on its surface.

The extensive damage was caused when a Nasa spacecraft hit the asteroid Dimorphos in 2022. The asteroid posed no threat, but this was a test of our planetary defences to see if it was possible to knock a space rock off course.

Four years on, a European Space Agency (ESA) spacecraft called Hera is scouring the scene to fully assess the aftermath of the collision.

"The crater is almost as large as the body itself, which is really, really spectacular," said Dr Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist, who is on the Hera science team.

"It's hard to describe in words all the excitement to actually finally see what happened. And as a crater person, I can also say it's beautiful."

The original 2022 crash test was carried out by a Nasa spacecraft called Dart which hurtled into Dimorphos at 14,000mph (22,000km/h).

It was such a big smash-up that the trail of debris, measuring about 6,200 miles long (10,000km), was spotted by telescopes on Earth and in space.

Dimorphos is about 150m (500ft) wide and was partly chosen as the target because it is one of an asteroid pair - it orbits around a larger space rock called Didymos, which is about 730m (2,400ft) wide.

The crash showed it was possible to change the path of the smaller asteroid.

Before the collision, it took Dimorphos 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit around Didymos. After the crash, the duration of its orbit had shrunk by about 33 minutes.

Watch: Moment Nasa spacecraft leaves stadium-sized crater in asteroid

A key objective of the Hera mission is to measure the mass of Dimorphos, in order to work out how effective the spacecraft's shove was.

This information would be vital if we ever spotted a space rock that was on a collision course with Earth.

"It would help us to predict what would happen if we had to hit another asteroid," said Prof Simon Green, a planetary scientist who is on Hera's science team.

"We [could] measure its properties and say: 'Well, we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth'."

Hera's arrival on the scene is helping scientists answer many more questions too.

Not only did the collision alter the shape of Dimorphos, it also changed the way it moves.

"Before the Dart impact, Dimorphos was more like our own Moon - so it was always showing the same face to Didymos, and it was behaving quite nicely," explained Raducan.

"Now it's very, very chaotic - it's tumbling. Imagine a barrel that you throw down the stairs, and the barrel is rolling and it's moving, and basically that's what Dimorphos does right now around Didymos."

Hera has also taken detailed images of Didymos, revealing its surface in detail.

"The gravity at the surface of Didymos is about 40,000 times less than the gravity on Earth," said Naomi Murdoch, who is on the Hera mission team.

"We can already see some fascinating and complex geology. We can see regions of boulders.

"The smallest details that we can make out in these images are about 3m in size. So that means that all the boulders that you can see with your eyes are at least 3m big, and some as big as 10m. So what we're seeing here is boulders the size of cars and buses sitting on a world with almost no gravity."

The mission has sent back new pictures of the smaller Dimorphos orbiting around Didymos

The Hera mission will spend the next six months studying Dimorphos and Didymos, sending back more images taken from closer up in higher resolution.

"Hera is a sort of Inspector Columbo going back to the crash scene to investigate what is the outcome of this impact," said Patrick Michel, principal investigator of ESA's Hera mission.

The spacecraft is packed with instruments and also has two shoebox-sized satellites on board, which can study the impact site up close and later land on the asteroid.

But the first images taken by Hera have also revealed a new challenge. Dimorphos and Didymos are travelling through space surrounded by hundreds of boulders ranging in size from tens of centimetres to metre-size rocks.

This will make it especially challenging to operate the spacecraft and cube satellites through this boulder field.

Asteroids pose a significant threat to our planet.

The biggest, which measures more than a kilometre across, could cause global devastation. But medium-sized asteroids around the same dimensions as Dimorphos could wipe out a city.

"We all know what happened to the dinosaurs, right? And we don't want that to happen to us," said Carolyn Ernst at Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, who has worked on both the Dart and Hera missions.

"The good news for us is that there are far fewer potential threats out there in the Solar System than there were millions of years ago. But there are objects out there."

She said the biggest asteroids have been spotted, and none are heading to Earth.

"The smallest stuff isn't too much of a threat because our atmosphere protects us," she added.

"But there's a size range in the middle where we don't know where all of them are, and it could, if impacted, cause problems for humanity. And if we can find out about that and know about that, well, we sure as heck would want to try to stop that."

Artist's impression showing Hera. It has two small satellites on board to study the asteroid up close

Scientists want to gather as much intelligence as they can about space rocks to make us as well equipped as possible to deal with whatever the Universe throws at us.

A new mission is launching in 2027 to track potentially hazardous asteroids - a space telescope called the Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor.

There are also more visits planned to asteroids, including to one called Apophis, which is about 450m (1,480ft) long.

Apophis was discovered in 2004 and it was initially thought it might hit the Earth as early as 13 April 2029.

But now scientists understand its orbit better, and say it won't hit us for at least the next 100 years. Instead of a crash in 2029, it will fly just 32,000km (20,000 miles) above our surface.

This will give scientists the rare chance to study a space rock up-close.

Two spacecraft will head to Apophis, including Nasa's Osiris-Apex (which previously grabbed a sample from another asteroid called Bennu) and Ramses, a joint mission between ESA and the Japanese Space Agency (JAXA).

Tariffs are taxes levied on imported goods. The amount is typically calculated as a percentage of a good's value and paid to the government by businesses importing products.

Some of these rates will be effectively reduced because of caps on tariffs Trump has agreed to. Individual countries also face different levies.

The proposed tariffs will affect goods from China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan, and India.

The USTR's Jamieson Greer said the latest tariffs issued would "level the playing field for American workers and chart a new, more sustainable course for the world economy".

Lawyers for former President Donald Trump told a New York judge on Monday that they oppose the request by media outlets to broadcast Trump’s arraignment on Tuesday.

“We submit that the media request should be denied because it will create a circus-like atmosphere at the arraignment, raise unique security concerns, and is inconsistent with President Trump’s presumption of innocence,” the Trump team said in a letter to New York Supreme Court Judge Juan Merchant.

Trump’s attorneys said in their letter to Acting New York Supreme Court Judge Juan Merchan that he is required under court rules to take into account potential security concerns.

“As Your Honor is well-aware, this case presents extraordinary security concerns (including Secret Service-related concerns) and we submit that any video or photography of the proceedings will only heighten these serious concerns,” they wrote.

Among the Trump lawyers signing the new letter was Todd Blanche, who, CNN reported, recently joined the former president’s legal team.

Meanwhile, the Manhattan District Attorney’s office also responded Monday to the news outlets’ request, and said that it was deferring to the judge to decide how best to manage his court room.

However, the district attorney’s office wrote that it would “be a defensible exercise of the Court’s discretion to exclude or restrict videography, photography, and radio coverage of the arraignment in the interest of avoiding potential prejudice to the defendant, maintaining an orderly proceeding, assuring the safety of the participants in the proceeding, or for other reasons within the Court’s broad authority to manage and control these proceedings.”

But the prosecutors also noted that there “does not appear to be a categorical prohibition on cameras during an arraignment” under existing New York statutes and case law.

While the district attorney’s office stopped short of opposing the request, its lawyer pointed out to Merchan that a similar request for audio-video broadcasting was made for the 2021 arraignment in the tax fraud case against Trump Organization and its CFO Allen Weisselberg.

The judge responded to that 2021 request by “allowing a limited number of still photographs to be taken prior to the commencement of proceedings,” the prosecutors noted Monday.

CNN is one of the outlets requesting camera access for Tuesday’s arraignment.
