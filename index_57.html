#137. Woman in Saudi Arabia sentenced to 100 lashes for giving birth outside marriage

Rights groups condemn reported punishment of Kenyan migrant worker as an ‘act of violence’

A Kenyan mother in Saudi Arabia has reportedly been sentenced to 100 lashes after giving birth outside marriage.

Under the Saudi sharia law system, sex outside marriage is a criminal offence. But it is women who are disproportionately targeted, say rights groups, which have condemned the punishment as an “act of violence”.

Although many unmarried women give birth in Saudi Arabia, rights groups say the Kenyan mother’s case only came to light because she had been taken to a hospital, which are required to report single women giving birth to the authorities.

“Most of them give birth at home, that’s why they aren’t reported,” said Ekaterina Porras Sivolobova, founder of Do Bold, a non-profit organisation advocating for migrant workers’ rights in the Gulf.

The Kenyan mother, Esther Wanja Muthoni, was a domestic worker recruited to work as housekeeper and nanny in Saudi Arabia. She reportedly became pregnant during a brief relationship with an Egyptian man and said that the child’s father had since left the country.

A 2024 Guardian investigation found a number of Kenyan single mothers were abandoned by their children’s fathers out of fear of being arrested for having extramarital sex. After the ruling, which was issued last month, Muthoni told the New York Times she was afraid about not being able to endure the lashes and said: “I don’t deserve this.”

The case comes as a planned visit to Saudi Arabia by Reem Alsalem, the UN special rapporteur on violence against women and girls, was postponed last week. Alsalem said she had intended to “raise the situation of specific cases during the visit. If the visit does not take place soon, I will do so separately through the established channels that we have as mandate holders.”

Maryam Aldossari, acting director at the UK-based rights organisation ALQST (al-qist means “justice” in Arabic), said the ruling was an act of violence and the woman should have been sent back to Kenya and not punished.

“It just shows the hypocrisy behind so-called Saudi reform. We know MBS [Mohammed bin Salman, the Saudi crown prince] has been trying to show the world that Saudi is becoming a modern country that empowers women, yet we see the court sentence a mother to 100 lashes,” said Aldossari.

In April 2020, Saudi announced a reform to end flogging as a judicial punishment. However, it only applied to ta’zir offences, where judges have discretion over punishments. Although most flogging sentences fell into this category, flogging remained a possible punishment for certain hudud (fixed punishments under Islamic law) offences, such as alcohol consumption and adultery, ALQST said in a report in 2021.

Sivolobova said it had been difficult to help in cases involving Kenyan mothers compared with those from other countries. “I haven’t been able to get help from the Kenyan authorities to support these women,” she said.

The Saudi government was approached for comment.

#134. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

An interview with the four astronauts will air on “CNN This Morning” on Tuesday, which starts at 6 am ET.

CNN’s Kristin Fisher contributed to this story.

#129. Democracy study: One-quarter of Germans want a right-wing authoritarian dictatorship

The far-right AfD's electoral successes point to a new trend in Germany: Prejudice, resentment, and extremism are on the rise. A new study suggests satisfaction with democracy is at a low ebb.

Satisfaction with democracy in Germany has hit a new low, while the past is being idealized, and people are expressing a longing for a radical break. Antisemitic sentiments and the desire for an authoritarian leader are also on the rise. These are key findings of the authoritarianism study presented Wednesday in Berlin, which has been conducted every two years since 2002.

The study, led by the Else Frenkel-Brunswik Institute for Democracy Research at the University of Leipzig, is based on the answers to a questionnaire sent in by over 900 respondents, who the researchers say are a representative cross-section of the German population.

For the first time since the study series began, approval of democracy as an idea fell just below the 90% mark, which the study said is primarily attributable to a loss of trust among respondents in the former East Germany (85.7%). The decline in approval of democracy as experienced in everyday life is even more severe: Nationwide, only one-third are satisfied with democracy, with figures having fallen sharply both in eastern (22.8%) and western (36%) states.

The growing rejection of democracy is also reflected in the desire for some kind of authoritarian nationalist government: Just under a quarter of respondents explicitly supported the idea of a "single strong party that embodies the national community as a whole." In the east, the figure stands at 30.9%, above the Germany-wide average of 22.3%.

"Our democracy is under great pressure. It has lost acceptance among the general population. It is alarming how much an authoritarian yearning is taking its place," concluded social psychologist and study author Oliver Decker.

Far-right attitudes have risen for the second consecutive year. Of particular note in the study was what the researchers called a chauvinistic national pride, which, with 19.9% approval, has reached its highest level since the study series began. The proportion of people with a consistently far-right worldview has reached 6.9%, the highest level since 2014, the study found.

Just under 15% of respondents said they explicitly believe that "everything has to fall apart so that something new can emerge," while nearly one in three respondents believes that "something radical is needed for the current state of affairs to change." Another 29.1% agree with this to some extent.

#119. 'They will kill you too': Sonia Gandhi's turbulent journey in Indian politics

Sonia Gandhi has been one of the most powerful political figures in India's history

On the morning of 31 October 1984, Sonia Gandhi was a young wife and mother, living in the prime minister's residence in Delhi. She was about to take a bath when she heard shots ring out in the garden.

Her mother-in-law, Indira Gandhi, India's prime minister, had just left for her office in an adjoining building. Moments later, she would be assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards, in retaliation for the Indian army's storming of the Golden Temple in Amritsar, Sikhism's holiest shrine, earlier that year.

The sound was like firecrackers, but "different". The children's nanny came pounding on the bathroom door, hysterical, pointing towards the garden.

Sonia ran towards the path. A white Ambassador car was moving slowly towards her. Inside, Indira lay covered in blood, her sari stained, her eyes open, her face wearing what Sonia remembered as a look of surprise.

"Mummy, Mummy," she tried to say. No words came. At the hospital, she waited for hours before a doctor's shake of the head told her what she feared.

The ordeal did not end there. Rajiv Gandhi, her husband and the elder son of Indira Gandhi, summoned from a rally in West Bengal, arrived to tell her that party leaders wanted him to become prime minister.

Sonia's response was fury and terror. "They will kill you; they will kill you, too!" she pleaded. Rajiv replied: "They will kill me anyway." That evening, as Indira's body lay at home, Rajiv was sworn in.

It is one of the most searing passages in Belonging: A Journey of Love, Sonia Gandhi's new memoir: private devastation unfolding alongside the pressures of public life.

The woman who had largely stayed away from politics was now married to India's prime minister, and would eventually find herself at the head of the Congress party, leading it through two general election victories and becoming one of the country's most consequential political figures.

The passage also foreshadows another devastating loss. In 1991, Rajiv himself was assassinated.

Indira Gandhi was asssassinated by her Sikh bodyguards in October 1984

For Sonia, the years that followed were marked by retreat.

At 19, a young woman from a small Italian town, she had met Rajiv, an Indian student, in England. Their relationship took her into a new country and family, and a close bond with Indira followed. India became home through her husband and her mother-in-law.

After Rajiv's killing, even the public appearances required by the memorial trusts she headed became an ordeal. Before speeches, she feared tears would overwhelm her.

When the Dalai Lama asked whether she felt vengeful towards her husband's killers, she answered, "No, I don't," and burst into sobs. Slowly, she writes, Rajiv became "a presence within me rather than a piercing absence".

But grief returned at family milestones. At her son Rahul's Cambridge graduation in 1995, she and her children were overcome by the thought of how proud Rajiv would have been.

Politics, meanwhile, was closing in. The Congress party, the political vehicle of her husband's family for generations, was losing cohesion and electoral ground. Senior leaders were defecting, and the calls for Sonia to step in were growing louder.

She initially refused. She writes she had seen politics through Rajiv: its achievements, but also its "ambition and greed, hypocrisy and treachery". "I'd always disliked politics," she writes. It had taken first her mother-in-law, then "the love of my life, my children's father".

Yet the party's decline began to feel personal. Walking past photographs of Indira and Rajiv, she was seized by "a mixture of pain and guilt". She feared that the forces they had fought to "protect India's pluralistic ethos" were gaining ground: a rising Hindu nationalism that, in her view, threatened the "secular and pluralistic vision" of India they had sought to defend.

Congress leaders argued that without her, the party could splinter, leaving little to resist communal politics. The family name, she believed, carried an appeal beyond religion, caste, language and region. This, she says, was "the most compelling argument".

Rajiv Gandhi took oath as India's youngest prime minister hours after his mother's death

Her doubts were also about her own credentials. How could an outsider occupy a position held by two formidable figures? At home, Rahul and Priyanka feared losing their mother as they had lost their grandmother and father. Before a New Year holiday, they extracted a promise: "Don't you do anything crazy while we're away."

The "answer", she says, came through the Bhagavad Gita. Though not religious in adult life, Sonia arranged private lessons with a Sanskrit scholar in January 1997. They read the text verse by verse. She writes that its central message â to perform one's dharma, or duty, without attachment to the fruits of one's actions â eventually resolved her inner conflict.

"I had the answer to my own doubts and questions. I had to step out and go where my duty called. And I did."

The memoir's most dramatic political scene, however, comes seven years later, after Congress's 2004 election victory. Sonia had led the party's campaign.

Sonia goes with Manmohan Singh to meet India's president at the presidential palace in Delhi and formally stake the Congress-led alliance's claim to form a government. Everyone assumes she will become prime minister. She says nothing.

At home, she tells a senior aide she intends to refuse. Party workers gather at the gates; senior colleagues demand a meeting.

"You fought the election, you led us," says one. "People believed in you. How can you let them down like this? Our workers will revolt. You think they'll accept another leader?"

Alliance leaders arrive to persuade her. They surround her, she recalls, and one insists: "We will not take no for an answer." Overwhelmed, she murmurs "Theek hai" - all right. They take it as consent and begin congratulating her.

Her children are appalled, she writes. Rahul storms in: "You said yes?" Her daughter Priyanka urges her not to be forced into a decision.

Sonia writes she spends a long night arguing with herself, friends and family. The risk is not merely personal. She believes the main opposition Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) would continue to exploit her foreign birth and that accepting office would allow the controversy to "create havoc" and hurt the country. By morning she is clear. "I've made up my mind. I'm going to stick to my stand," she tells Rahul.

Sonia Gandhi (centre), with daughter Priyanka and son Rahul, at the funeral of Rajiv Gandhi in 1991

She chooses Singh, a trained economist and a party member, instead, describing him as a man "above reproach", with a "deep and consummate understanding of governance".

The book is at its most revealing when it turns from family memory to the India she believes has been lost.

Sonia says that when she entered public life there was "a certain decency and decorum in parliamentary politics".

Political attacks and harsh words were not new, but she sees a sharper appetite among those in power "to belittle and torment those who differ from them, to intimidate those they perceive as opponents". It is, she says, part of a wider international drift towards "a heartlessness that accompanies authoritarian regimes".

Her portrait of prime minister Narendra Modi of the Hindu nationalist BJP is unusually direct.

The two have had "practically no real contact", she writes, beyond formal greetings at parliamentary gatherings and occasional exchanges - "Theek hain, aap?" ("Are you all right?") - when she returned from hospital. "He tends to look away," she says, "avoiding eye contact."

Sonia Gandhi with Manmohan Singh, whom she chose as prime minister after declining the post herself when Congress returned to power in 2004

Modi's idea of India, she writes, was shaped by the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) and "affirmed a fundamentalism in which his faith and culture superseded all others". His is an India of "hierarchy, order, and control"; hers is "an India against hegemony", standing for "inclusiveness, secularism, and social justice".

There is a striking concession amid the polemic - she says she understood Modi's "discomfort with the ruling elite of our times".

Sonia says she felt uneasy in Delhi's privileged drawing rooms, where political conversation seemed "very far removed from the reality of India".

Modi's origins, she writes, "may not be as humble as his PR team" suggests, but he "had certainly worked his way to power within the RSS from an underprivileged world". It is a rare acknowledgement of the social distance between the Congress establishment and the electorate Modi came to represent.

The memoir also offers some glimpses of Sonia's encounters with world leaders.

She remembers Vladimir Putin as an "engaging personality" who took her on a boat ride in St Petersburg, pointing out monuments along the way. She says she was impressed by his grasp of geopolitics and his "determination to restore Russia to its former powerful status" - "long before the Ukraine war, a different era".

Sonia Gandhi writes that she and PM Modi (foreground) have had "practically no real contact"

A more amusing encounter involved France's Nicolas Sarkozy, who arrived in India in 2008 without his then partner Carla Bruni because they were unmarried.

At a state banquet, the Indian vice-president's wife cheerfully grilled him about when he planned to marry her, whether he missed her and whether a date had been fixed. Sarkozy obligingly answered her questions while messaging Bruni on his phone.

Belonging is not a neutral history. The Bofors gun scandal, which badly damaged Rajiv Gandhi's government and dogged the family for decades, gets a relatively brief treatment.

The 502-page memoir is less forthcoming about the less flattering aspects of Congress's own record. Sonia has much to say about political corruption, hypocrisy and the decline of public standards under her opponents, but largely sidesteps the culture of patronage, cronyism and corruption that dogged Congress during its years in power. There is little reckoning, either, with the party's own share of responsibility for the erosion of public trust.

She is at pains to defend the political inheritance of her husband and mother-in-law, even as the party they built has suffered a prolonged decline. The story she tells is as much about the costs of carrying that inheritance as it is about a woman who says she never wanted the life she ended up leading.

That gives the memoir's final pages an unmistakable melancholy. Sonia, 79, is now a member of the Rajya Sabha, the upper house of parliament. Rahul, 55, is a four-term MP and leader of the opposition; Priyanka, 53, is also a member of parliament.

Sonia says the challenge was to remain faithful to "a just and humane society", through pain and loss, victory and defeat, and the manoeuvring that politics demands. But she ends not with triumph, nor even with the assurance that history will vindicate her.

"At this moment it seems to me that, although I tried my best, I didn't succeed in beating back the forces I'd set out to fight."

#116. Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi bring progress on prisoners but not on peace

US envoys to visit Moscow and Dublin on Thursday for talks with Vladimir Putin and Volodymyr Zelenskyy respectively

Russia and Ukraine have agreed to the “largest prisoner exchange” of the war at their first US-brokered talks since February but made little progress towards a broader peace agreement.

Both sides sent senior delegations to the two-day talks, led by Donald Trump’s envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, in an effort to find a solution to end the nearly-five-year war.

David Arakhamia, a senior member of Ukraine’s delegation, said the Russian team had arrived with “very, very strict directives”, making it difficult to narrow differences. But he said there had been “really big progress” on security guarantees for Ukraine after the war. “I think we have good momentum to bring about a solution before the new year,” Arakhamia said.

Kushner, Trump’s son-in-law, also struck an upbeat tone, saying he could see a path to an agreement. “These deals are very hard and complex, and this one is very emotional, very complex. It’s a very hot war,” he said. “But we see all the different ingredients to make a deal. We just have to figure out how to refine those ingredients, organise them and create the right structure and pathway.”

Kushner said the planned exchange, expected to involve more than 1,000 prisoners from each side, would “reunite families, build trust and create momentum towards peace”.

The US envoys are due to travel to Moscow on Thursday to discuss the Abu Dhabi talks with Vladimir Putin, before heading to Dublin, where they are expected to meet Volodymyr Zelenskyy and a group of European partners.

“In close coordination with Ukraine and our European partners, we have aligned on proposals we will discuss with President Putin in Moscow tomorrow, followed by consultations with President Zelenskyy,” Kushner said.

But despite the optimism, Kushner also said the two sides had yet to resolve their territorial dispute.

Russia’s territorial demands have shifted over time, but Moscow has made clear it wants control of all the Donbas, including areas still held by Kyiv, as well as the parts of two southern regions it does not yet occupy.

Vladimir Medinsky, the hardline Russian official who led Moscow’s delegation, said that “every inch of our land will be returned to Russia, one way or another,” in a sign that Moscow was unwilling to soften its territorial demands. Russia would be satisfied with the negotiations only “when there is peace on its terms”, he added.

He also said the main task was to create a new security system in Europe that would become “a new Yalta and Potsdam”.

Medinsky said Europe’s post-second world war security system was failing, with Ukraine merely the place where its wider problems had erupted. “Essentially, the whole body is sick, and Ukraine was just a cyst that burst. The whole body must be cured. A Band-Aid won’t be enough,” Medinsky said.

His focus on Europe’s wider security order is likely to heighten concern among European leaders that Washington and Moscow could reach a broader agreement on the continent’s security, potentially limiting Nato’s military presence in eastern Europe. Russia appears to be seeking to revive demands it presented to Nato in 2021, including a halt to further enlargement that would rule out Ukraine’s membership, restrictions on Nato forces and weapons in European countries that joined after 1997, and limits on missile deployments.

That ultimatum was dismissed as a non-starter by the west and, weeks later, Russia invaded Ukraine.

Concerned that Europe’s security future could be discussed over their heads, European governments sent envoys to Abu Dhabi to try to influence the talks, although they are not believed to have taken part directly.

Medinsky dismissed their presence, saying that if they had come “as tourists”, they could talk in the hotel lobby, and that Russia would consider proposals only if they had “something specific” to offer.

European leaders have increasingly discussed the need for a direct channel to Putin, but no clear candidate has emerged. The leaders of Norway and Finland have recently spoken to the Russian president, while Germany’s chancellor, Friedrich Merz, said on Wednesday that he was open to doing so.

Kushner, meanwhile, said Europe should choose a single representative to speak on its behalf. “If Europe could identify one person to speak on their behalf, that would be a bigger achievement than getting the Russians to include them in the negotiations,” he said.

He stressed that one of the aims of his trip to Moscow was to “see if we can get Europeans added” to future talks. “The issues that were covered in these discussions were really postwar security,” he said. “How do we make sure that once this war ends, it doesn’t start again?”

Russian officials have long opposed European participation, arguing that countries supplying weapons to Kyiv cannot act as impartial mediators. Medinsky said Russia would speak to European countries only “when their conscience awakens” and they stop supplying Ukraine with weapons.

As diplomatic efforts continued, both sides kept up their attacks. Russia launched drones at Kyiv and other regions during the day, while Ukraine struck the Ust-Luga port on Russia’s Baltic coast overnight. The port, in the Leningrad region, is a major route for Russian energy exports and was still burning on Wednesday.

#115. Beijing promised to 'fight back' over Taiwan leader's US visit. But this time it has more to lose

An anticipated meeting between Taiwan’s President Tsai Ing-wen and US House Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California this week has sparked concerns of a repeat of the pressure campaign China launched last year when then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei.

At that time, Beijing encircled the island democracy with unprecedented military drills – firing multiple missiles into its surrounding waters and sending dozens of warplanes speeding across a sensitive median line dividing the Taiwan Strait.

It also cut off contact with the United States over a number of issues from military matters to combating climate change, in retaliation for what it viewed as a violation of its sovereignty.

This time, Beijing has already threatened to “resolutely fight back” if a Tsai-McCarthy meeting goes ahead.

It also slammed Washington for allowing Tsai to stopover in the US while en route to and from official visits in Central America, warning it could lead to “serious” confrontation between the two powers.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

But a meeting between Tsai and the leader of the Republican majority in the US House of Representatives, who ranks second in line to the Presidency, would mark another symbolic moment for Taiwan and the US, which only maintain unofficial ties.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

That increased visibility – and enhanced cooperation with the US – has followed China’s mounting pressure on the island, which sits fewer than 110 miles (177 kilometers) from the mainland coast.

China’s Communist Party claims the self-governing island democracy as its own despite never having controlled it, and has vowed to take the island, by force if necessary.

China thinks it's diplomatically isolating Taiwan. It isn't

The party has undertaken a sweeping expansion of its military capabilities over the past decade under leader Xi Jinping – and ramped up its pervasive economic, diplomatic and military pressure on Taiwan.

That’s driven concerns, among some in Washington, that Beijing is preparing for an invasion, though China’s official language still suggests that scenario is not its preferred option for achieving the claimed goal of “reunification.”

It is those pressures – and how to support Taiwan against unilateral actions by Beijing – that are likely to be on the table when Tsai, McCarthy and a bipartisan group of US lawmakers sit down on Wednesday.

Congress has been a pillar of increasing American support for Taiwan in recent years. Lawmakers regularly visit the island and drive bipartisan legislation enhancing support and cooperation.

While the US switched its diplomatic relations to Beijing decades ago, it maintains unofficial ties with Taiwan and is bound by law to provide the democratic island with the means to defend itself.

Under Washington’s longstanding “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, but has never officially recognized Beijing’s claim to the island of 23 million.

Though McCarthy does not have Pelosi’s decades-long record of advocacy regarding China, the California Republican is now a leading voice pushing for closer scrutiny of Beijing, and meeting Tsai could help him to burnish that image.

Last month, McCarthy told reporters that meeting Tsai in the US would not impact whether he travels to Taiwan in the future – something he had earlier said he wanted to do.

A meeting in California, on US soil, is widely seen as less likely to provoke Beijing than a McCarthy visit to Taiwan.

Pelosi’s trip – the first from a lawmaker of that rank to the island in 25 years – generated a fever pitch of nationalist and anti-US rhetoric in mainland China.

This time, so far, domestic conversation in China’s heavily controlled media sphere has been significantly muted.

But the stakes remain high – including for Beijing itself – over how it responds, analysts say.

As Taiwan prepares for a presidential election in January, a fierce response could push voters away from Taiwan’s main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT), widely seen as more friendly toward Beijing.

It could also jar with another high profile trip happening now: a tour of mainland China from former Taiwan president and senior KMT member Ma Ying-jeou, the first visit from a current or former Taiwan leader since the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949.

Ma’s tour is a “once in a half a century opportunity to send a conciliatory message between the two sides, Beijing shouldn’t want to tank that,” said Sung, the political scientist.

What you need to know about China-Taiwan tensions

China is also acutely aware that its actions toward Taiwan are under a significantly brighter global spotlight following the invasion of Ukraine by Russian President Vladimir Putin, a close diplomatic partner of Xi. Putin’s rhetoric over Ukraine has echoes of how Xi speaks of Taiwan.

Beijing has recently sought to position itself as an agent of peace in that conflict – especially as it aims to repair frayed ties with Europe.

This week, as Tsai is expected to meet with McCarthy, French President Emmanuel Macron and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen will head to China – an important opportunity that Xi may not want to overshadow with military posturing.

An aggressive response also risks stoking confrontation with the US, not yet six months after Xi and US President Joe Biden called to enhance communication during a face-to-face meeting in Bali.

“(A less overtly aggressive response) would imply that Beijing does not wish to escalate tension with the US to a level that can risk getting out of hand,” said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London.

“A reset in US-China ties is not on the agenda, but an easing of tension is not beyond the realm of possibility.”

#113. Fears of rising antisemitism in Europe

Holocaust survivor Regina Sluszny commemorates the victims of Auschwitz as Jewish communities across Europe face growing hostility. What does the promise of "Never Again" mean for Jewish life in Europe today?

Regina Sluszny makes her way in her wheelchair through the iron gate marking the entrance to the former Auschwitz concentration camp. "I feel the cold, both physically and emotionally," says the 87-year-old Jewish woman, her gaze fixed on the grey horizon.

How many times has she been here? Six, seven, perhaps eight, she estimates. And each time, the same thoughts run through her mind: "I think about my husband's family. He lost seven loved ones here. They were sent straight to the gas chambers and murdered."

Regina Sluszny traveled to Poland with a delegation from the European Jewish Association (EJA), a European advocacy group representing Jewish interests that regularly organizes conferences on Jewish life as well as visits to former concentration camps.

This year, the organization invited political representatives and members of Jewish communities to Krakow and Auschwitz on November 9 and 10. The trip was deliberately scheduled to coincide with the anniversary of the November Pogroms of 1938, widely regarded as a watershed moment on the path to the Holocaust.

"Honoring the past, securing the future" is the event's motto. Like many others in attendance, Holocaust survivor Regina Sluszny fears that the horrors of the Shoah are slowly fading from Europe's collective memory.

Among this year's international religious and political representatives, the guest of honor was Reza Pahlavi, the exiled son of Iran's last shah and a prominent opponent of the Islamic Republic. The European Jewish Association (EJA) honored him for his efforts to combat antisemitism.

Accepting the award, Pahlavi recalled the historic ties between the Persian and Jewish peoples and called on democratic nations around the world to unite against oppression and ideological extremism.

But talking to attendees at the symposium in Krakow, it quickly becomes clear that fear of rising antisemitism is much closer to home. Many describe growing hostility towards Jews and rising fear in Jewish communities across Europe.

In France, authorities recorded a total of 1,320 antisemitic incidents in 2025. That was fewer than in 2024, but around three times as many as in 2022.

In Germany, the number of antisemitic crimes recorded by police rose from 2,641 in 2022 to 6,236 in 2024 and 6,548 in 2025. Authorities and monitoring organizations across Europe have reported a significant increase in antisemitic incidents since October 7, 2023.

For Ellen van Praagh, chair of the Interprovincial Rabbinate in the Netherlands, October 7, 2023, marked a turning point. She also lost a family member during the Hamas-led attack which saw around 1,200 people in Israel killed and over 250 taken hostage and abducted to the Gaza Strip.

"My family has lived in the Netherlands since the 16th century. And now I'm being told to leave," says van Praagh. She says the atmosphere in the Netherlands has changed dramatically since the attack. She no longer feels safe there as a Jew. "We Jews are being held responsible for Israel's actions."

Israel launched a major military offensive in the Gaza Strip after the October-7 attack. More than 75,000 Palestinians have been killed there since the start of the war, according to conservative estimates.

Like many others at the symposium, van Praagh says she is increasingly losing faith in politicians. "Never Again" has become an empty phrase, she argues, with little relevance to the everyday lives of Jews in Europe.

Jewish communities now have to protect themselves behind high walls. Constant security measures restrict their daily lives, she explains. "That is not what we want. We want to live in freedom."

Rabbi Menachem Margolin, chairman of the European Jewish Association, is also calling on European political leaders to take greater responsibility. He says he is grateful for their support, without which Jewish life in Europe would hardly be possible.

However, laws designed to protect Jewish life are often not enforced in practice. Many officials hesitate to call antisemitism by its name as soon as doing so becomes politically inconvenient. "But Auschwitz did not begin with gas chambers. It began with words," he cautioned.

The Nazis murdered around 1.1 million people there, including approximately one million Jews. Most were killed here at Birkenau.

The delegation is gathered to commemorate the November Pogroms of November 9 and 10, 1938, when Nazis set synagogues on fire, ransacked Jewish businesses and homes and murdered Jews. Around 30,000 Jewish men were deported to concentration camps.

Survivors like Regina are increasingly rare. Most Holocaust survivors have passed away. Regina survived because, in 1942, a family friend and his wife took her in when she was just two years old while her parents fled the Nazis with her two brothers and went into hiding.

For more than 40 years, she has been sharing her story with schoolchildren, doing her part to ensure that the Holocaust is never forgotten. For her, "Never Again" is a promise that must be kept. Otherwise, it becomes a lie.

#112. Why does Australia want Brits to swap Sydney for the outback?

Paige Tomlinson from Barnsley loves the "chilled" lifestyle of Cairns in northern Australia

In a Mexican restaurant in north-east Australia, the tacos are served by staff from France, Italy, Ireland and Argentina.

Cairns has long attracted a young and transient international crowd, who enjoy the tropical lifestyle while earning money for travel.

But one nationality is in shorter supply than it used to be.

"You get far fewer British backpackers looking for jobs here these days," says Mexicairns owner Peter McLeay.

It's a trend McLeay has noticed since mid-2023, when a free trade deal between the UK and Australia kicked in.

That agreement - as well as tariff-free trade in Tim Tams and Penguins - included special treatment for Brits on working holiday visas: exemption from having to find jobs in regional or remote places if they wanted to remain beyond a year.

That, along with an increase in the age limit from 30 to 35 saw backpacker visas issued to Brits surging from 47,200 in 2023-24 to just over 77,000 in 2025-26, according to the latest Australian government data.

Without the incentive to go regional, many are choosing to base themselves in the big cities, or, according to Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, "Bondi or Coogee" - two of Sydney's most famous beachside suburbs.

Now though, with Brits making up almost a quarter of all 323,000 backpacker visas issued last year, his government wants the free trade deal to be revisited.

Many British backpackers end up in Bondi, PM Anthony Albanese says, instead of exploring the rest of Australia

"Many Brits don't treat it as a working holiday anymore. It's more a three-year career break," says Dan McConnell, general manager of the Mad Monkey backpacker hostel chain.

He says Brits used to be the largest clientele at its Cairns accommodation but "now French outnumber British three to one".

"We see the Brits get to Sydney, decide it's the coolest place in the world, and stay there," he adds.

It's a privilege not enjoyed by working holidaymakers from other countries who must do at least 88 days of "specified regional work" in industries that need a seasonal or temporary workforce - including agriculture, fisheries, tourism and hospitality - if they want to qualify for a second year extension, and six months to stay a third year.

Barely a month ago, Albanese ruled out forcing British backpackers to do the same, accepting it as part of the trade deal.

Cop31 could provide the first indications Beijing is ready to put that rhetoric into action, something it has historically been reluctant to do.

Green energy is a vital component of the export engine that has kept China’s sluggish post-pandemic economy on track. The success of its exports stands in contrast to its failure to significantly boost domestic general consumption, a structural weakness that has persistently vexed leaders.

In 2025, clean-tech exports surpassed $220bn, and they should grow again this year; the world’s largest emitter is now the world’s leading supplier of clean energy technology. China accounts for about 85% of solar and 80% of lithium-ion battery supply chain production capacity, and in 2025, nearly 75% of electric cars were manufactured there.

Smart moves now by China would position it as a linchpin in a future electrostate world order, persuade rivals to cooperate, and solidify its influence in the global south.

Meanwhile other countries, including the US under Donald Trump, have fallen badly behind. Kerry said they would lose out economically – as well as putting the climate in further danger – unless they, too, embraced low-carbon power and rapid electrification. “The only country that has hugely accelerated is China. The rest of the world is stumbling along while Mother Nature is sending us more powerful signals every week, every day,” he said.

“[China] realised suddenly they had way too much dependency on oil and gas from other places, and they decided they needed to be independent. Other countries, since the war in Iran, and since the strait of Hormuz has been interrupted, have made a similar decision, and they’re moving independently. There’s been a massive uptick in deployment of renewables. Thank you, Donald Trump.”

For autocratic states without free and fair elections, seeing the economic benefits reaped by China would be the spur to adopt low-carbon growth, he added.

“They’ll see where the market is going, and they will recognise where the cheaper alternatives are. Even they can read a spreadsheet, and understand where costs are exorbitant [in fossil fuels], and where you have a better deal,” Kerry said. “Right now, renewables together with storage, together with some transitional gas, is cheaper and more effective, than just going with fossil fuels.”

He added: “The marketplace has made its decision and globally is moving to electrons, and electrostate status. People are going to electricity and they’re going to try to do that as clean as humanly possible. That’s the choice that I see being implemented in many different places. It is unstoppable.”

China has already said it wants Cop31 to discuss the free flow of green technology against a backdrop of “intensifying” geopolitical conflicts – widely seen as a reference to US and EU tariffs on items such as Chinese electric vehicles and solar products.

#30. Two skulls likely belonging to enslaved people returned to Brazil after Harvard used them in eugenics studies

Remains believed to have belonged to Africans or Afro-Brazilians repatriated after being flown back by diplomats

Two skulls, including that of a man believed to have taken part in Brazil’s greatest slave revolt but whose remains were taken to the United States and used in racist eugenics studies at Harvard University, were repatriated on Wednesday.

The remains had been outside Brazil since the 19th century. One was taken about 190 years ago from Salvador, the capital of the state of Bahia; the other, about 160 years ago, from Rio de Janeiro – and were welcomed back in moving, subdued ceremonies in both cities.

The skulls are believed to have belonged to Africans or Afro-Brazilians in the country that received more enslaved Africans than any other during the transatlantic slave trade.

Each was brought back in a padded box inside a reinforced suitcase, carried on flights by Brazilian diplomats after a four-year campaign to recover the remains from Harvard’s Peabody Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology in the US.

Brazilian researchers from local universities will now try to learn more about who the two individuals were.

The skulls will not be displayed in museums or have their images released, and the Brazilian government has pledged that they will be buried with funeral rites as soon as the identification process is complete.

“It’s very important to restore the dignity of these people, which was taken from them in life and in death,” said Carolina Dias dos Reis, a government official who travelled to Boston to sign the agreement with Harvard.

The existence of the skulls was revealed in 2022, and Brazilian researchers sought their repatriation from Harvard, but said they faced a drawn-out process that only moved forward after the Brazilian government became involved in late 2024.

In recent months, concerns grew that Donald Trump’s sometimes hostile behaviour towards his Brazilian counterpart, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, could derail the repatriation. But everything worked out.

“We need to recover our past as a country, however difficult and violent it was, so that we can understand what we are living through in the present: the consequences and scars of structural racism,” added Reis.

Slavery was abolished in 1888, but nearly a century and a half later, Afro-Brazilians make up the majority of the population while occupying the bottom of every socioeconomic indicator, dominating the statistics on hunger, poverty, gender-based violence and victims of homicides committed by criminals or the police.

During the repatriation ceremony in Rio de Janeiro, Ivanir dos Santos, a history professor and a cleric of the Afro-Brazilian religion Candomblé, poured a glass of water on to the ground as researchers received the suitcase.

“It was a ritual act because you cannot deal with the remains of our ancestors without paying reverence to them. This is not a research object … for us, that person is still alive in memory and as our ancestor,” he said.

It is not precisely known who the skulls belonged to. The one taken to Salvador is believed to have belonged to a man who took part in the most significant urban slave revolt in Brazilian history, in 1835, when about 600 African Muslims attempted to seize control of what was then the country’s second most important city. They were ultimately defeated by police, who killed 70 rebels.

The man died at a hospital, and a US diplomat, Gideon T Snow, is believed to have sent the skull to Boston.

The Bahia Islamic Cultural Centre, one of the most active campaigners for its repatriation, has named the man Arakunrin, meaning brother in Yoruba.

Even less is known about the other person: initially, only that they were “an individual … exhumed from the ‘streets of Rio de Janeiro’” in the 1860s. Brazilian government officials, however, said on Wednesday that Harvard researchers had told them that some features – including that the skull was smaller than that of an adult man but larger than that of a child – suggested it may have belonged to a woman.

Bruno Veras, a historian who was part of the group that closely followed the repatriation, cross-referenced diaries, letters and maps of the city and believes the skull may have been taken from a cemetery for newly arrived Africans who either did not survive the long Atlantic crossing or died soon after disembarking.

The person believed to have taken it to the US was Walter Hunnewell, a student from a wealthy Boston family. He was part of an expedition led by the Harvard professor Louis Agassiz, who was in Brazil trying to document physical types to support his racist theories.

“To steal a skull and take it to another country was violent even in the context of racist research,” said Veras.

Researchers in forensic anthropology will now try to confirm the sex of the individuals and other information through measurements of the skulls – the one in Salvador is complete, while the one in Rio is missing its jaw – and microscopic analyses. If necessary, they will seek authorisation to carry out genetic or dating tests.

“This history of slavery cannot be undone,” said Irina Feisthauer Silveira, a diplomat who carried one of the skulls. “But acknowledging the mistakes of the past with transparency is a first step towards building a more equal, just and less violent future.”

The sentence, comprising 100 years for murder by starvation and 100 years by direct homicide, came two weeks after the court in Kenya’s coastal city of Mombasa found the cult leader guilty of the crimes.

Prosecutors said Paul Mackenzie and his Good News International church ran ​a Christian doomsday sect whose members were ordered to starve themselves and their children to death to go to heaven before the world ended.

His accomplices threatened and beat victims to ensure they followed his orders. The emaciated bodies were found scattered in shallow graves and dumped in latrines. Some had been buried alive.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after police received a tipoff, and weeks later authorities began uncovering bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest near the coast in south-eastern Kenya.

In total, more than 400 bodies have been recovered. Many were severely decomposed and only 26 could be identified.

Mackenzie denied murder charges, saying he did not force the victims to starve, but the court rejected that argument.

The Shakahola tragedy has gripped Kenya since the discovery of mass graves in the forest more than three years ago.

It raised questions about how Mackenzie was able to continue operating despite being on the radar of the authorities since 2017, when police searched his premises.

The festival is part of See My World’s year-round programme showcasing pan-African arts, culture and events.

It reflects the determination among creatives and activists in Manchester to build on the legacy of the 1945 Pan-African Congress in Manchester – which connected luminaries of postwar independence movements with a shared vision of liberation – after last year’s 80th anniversary commemorations.

See My World’s CEO and artistic director, Tunde Adekoya, said this month’s film festival was taking inspiration from across the spectrum of pan-African thought, including the works of Kenyan writers John Mbiti, an Anglican theologian who died in 2019, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, the author of the groundbreaking 1986 book Decolonising the Mind who died last year.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.

Adekoya said: “It’s a day dedicated to pan-African and global majority films from the north of England with exclusive premieres of documentaries we’ve been working on, which weave together expressions of liberation through art forms like poetry and dance.

#1. Lewis Hamilton visits Egypt’s Rafah crossing and Gaza aid facilities

Seven-time F1 world champion tours border area and logistics warehouses operated by Egyptian Red Crescent

Lewis Hamilton has visited Egypt’s Rafah border crossing with the Gaza Strip to meet organisations sending humanitarian aid to the territory.

The seven-time Formula One world champion has spoken out about conflict and humanitarian crises in the region, and has called for peace. He has frequently posted on social media about children killed in Israeli airstrikes in Gaza and called for a ceasefire.

“I think we need to make sure we’re pushing to free Congo, free Sudan, free Syria, free Palestine. I think there’s a lot of peace that I’m hoping we get to at some stage,” Hamilton said in October.

A ceasefire agreement reached last year has halted major combat operations in Gaza, but the amount of aid reaching the territory continues to fall far short of the agreed-upon minimum of 600 trucks a day.

Israeli forces have continued to advance, expanding their control from 53% of the territory to at least 60%, an independent commission of UN experts said last month.

Airstrikes also continue, and Israeli fire along the advancing front has killed at least 196 Palestinians, including 18 women, 32 boys and 11 girls, the commission said.

Hamilton toured Egypt’s Rafah border crossing with Gaza before visiting logistics warehouses operated by the Egyptian Red Crescent, which facilitates the entry of aid to Gaza.

At the crossing, Hamilton greeted aid workers with “Assalamu alaikum” a widely used Arabic greeting meaning “peace be upon you.” He shook hands and briefly spoke to workers and officials.

Khaled Megawer, the governor of North Sinai, where Rafah is located, said the visit was intended to help Hamilton learn about Egypt’s efforts to send aid to Gaza.

The visit comes after Hamilton made his 400th race start on Sunday at the São Paulo Grand Prix at Interlagos.

The first black F1 driver, he made his debut with McLaren in 2007. He is only the second F1 driver to start 400 races after his former teammate Fernando Alonso, who achieved the milestone in 2024.
