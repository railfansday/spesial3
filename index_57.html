#98. Beijing promised to 'fight back' over Taiwan leader's US visit. But this time it has more to lose

An anticipated meeting between Taiwan’s President Tsai Ing-wen and US House Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California this week has sparked concerns of a repeat of the pressure campaign China launched last year when then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei.

At that time, Beijing encircled the island democracy with unprecedented military drills – firing multiple missiles into its surrounding waters and sending dozens of warplanes speeding across a sensitive median line dividing the Taiwan Strait.

It also cut off contact with the United States over a number of issues from military matters to combating climate change, in retaliation for what it viewed as a violation of its sovereignty.

This time, Beijing has already threatened to “resolutely fight back” if a Tsai-McCarthy meeting goes ahead.

It also slammed Washington for allowing Tsai to stopover in the US while en route to and from official visits in Central America, warning it could lead to “serious” confrontation between the two powers.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

But a meeting between Tsai and the leader of the Republican majority in the US House of Representatives, who ranks second in line to the Presidency, would mark another symbolic moment for Taiwan and the US, which only maintain unofficial ties.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

That increased visibility – and enhanced cooperation with the US – has followed China’s mounting pressure on the island, which sits fewer than 110 miles (177 kilometers) from the mainland coast.

China’s Communist Party claims the self-governing island democracy as its own despite never having controlled it, and has vowed to take the island, by force if necessary.

China thinks it's diplomatically isolating Taiwan. It isn't

The party has undertaken a sweeping expansion of its military capabilities over the past decade under leader Xi Jinping – and ramped up its pervasive economic, diplomatic and military pressure on Taiwan.

That’s driven concerns, among some in Washington, that Beijing is preparing for an invasion, though China’s official language still suggests that scenario is not its preferred option for achieving the claimed goal of “reunification.”

It is those pressures – and how to support Taiwan against unilateral actions by Beijing – that are likely to be on the table when Tsai, McCarthy and a bipartisan group of US lawmakers sit down on Wednesday.

Congress has been a pillar of increasing American support for Taiwan in recent years. Lawmakers regularly visit the island and drive bipartisan legislation enhancing support and cooperation.

While the US switched its diplomatic relations to Beijing decades ago, it maintains unofficial ties with Taiwan and is bound by law to provide the democratic island with the means to defend itself.

Under Washington’s longstanding “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, but has never officially recognized Beijing’s claim to the island of 23 million.

Though McCarthy does not have Pelosi’s decades-long record of advocacy regarding China, the California Republican is now a leading voice pushing for closer scrutiny of Beijing, and meeting Tsai could help him to burnish that image.

Last month, McCarthy told reporters that meeting Tsai in the US would not impact whether he travels to Taiwan in the future – something he had earlier said he wanted to do.

A meeting in California, on US soil, is widely seen as less likely to provoke Beijing than a McCarthy visit to Taiwan.

Pelosi’s trip – the first from a lawmaker of that rank to the island in 25 years – generated a fever pitch of nationalist and anti-US rhetoric in mainland China.

This time, so far, domestic conversation in China’s heavily controlled media sphere has been significantly muted.

But the stakes remain high – including for Beijing itself – over how it responds, analysts say.

As Taiwan prepares for a presidential election in January, a fierce response could push voters away from Taiwan’s main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT), widely seen as more friendly toward Beijing.

It could also jar with another high profile trip happening now: a tour of mainland China from former Taiwan president and senior KMT member Ma Ying-jeou, the first visit from a current or former Taiwan leader since the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949.

Ma’s tour is a “once in a half a century opportunity to send a conciliatory message between the two sides, Beijing shouldn’t want to tank that,” said Sung, the political scientist.

What you need to know about China-Taiwan tensions

China is also acutely aware that its actions toward Taiwan are under a significantly brighter global spotlight following the invasion of Ukraine by Russian President Vladimir Putin, a close diplomatic partner of Xi. Putin’s rhetoric over Ukraine has echoes of how Xi speaks of Taiwan.

Beijing has recently sought to position itself as an agent of peace in that conflict – especially as it aims to repair frayed ties with Europe.

This week, as Tsai is expected to meet with McCarthy, French President Emmanuel Macron and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen will head to China – an important opportunity that Xi may not want to overshadow with military posturing.

An aggressive response also risks stoking confrontation with the US, not yet six months after Xi and US President Joe Biden called to enhance communication during a face-to-face meeting in Bali.

“(A less overtly aggressive response) would imply that Beijing does not wish to escalate tension with the US to a level that can risk getting out of hand,” said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London.

“A reset in US-China ties is not on the agenda, but an easing of tension is not beyond the realm of possibility.”

#95. ‘Tourists hate themselves’ – which is why Tasmania is telling them to stay away

Island state declares it ‘is not really for tourists’ – but is the marketing strategy just a cheeky piece of reverse psychology?

Get our breaking news email, free app or daily news podcast

Research shows “tourists hate themselves” – which is exactly why the marketing flacks at Tourism Tasmania are telling them to stay away from the island state.

Its new campaign declares Tasmania is “not really for tourists”, enlisting former SBS newsreader Lee Lin Chin to help sell the tongue-in-cheek campaign, based on the idea that Australians prefer identifying as a “traveller” rather than a “tourist”.

Its centrepiece is a short video of a man being pointed to the back of an endless queue for a “world famous” pastry. He eventually abandons the line to find solitude in the Tasmanian outdoors.

Chin, who lives mostly offline without a mobile phone, also fronts videos exploring the state’s walking trails on her visit.

Tourism Tasmania’s CEO, Sarah Kingston Clark, said the campaign was deliberately wry rather than anti-visitor, to reflect the “tensions” of modern travel.

Those tensions play out in the research, suggested Prof Can-Seng Ooi, a professor of cultural and heritage tourism at the University of Tasmania.

“Studies have shown that tourists hate themselves,” he said. “They want to avoid each other.”

Ooi identified stressed people yearning for “quietness” as the campaign’s target audience, especially those living in urban cities on the mainland.

“It taps into the type of tourist people want to be,” Ooi said. He added that people prefer to view themselves as “travellers” because of the negative connotation of “tourist”.

Ooi spent 20 years living in Scandinavia, researching how tourism, culture and heritage contributes to the development of regional destinations.

“The message is very Scandinavian, even the imagery: the sauna, the vinyl records, the books and so on,” Ooi said. “Instead of the cold north, we are talking about the cold south … it’s a quiet place, time slows down.”

Char-lee McLennan, an associate professor from QUT’s school of management, said the campaign used “an element of reverse psychology” that could benefit regional economies by steering visitors beyond established hotspots.

Prof David Waller, a marketing expert at the University of Technology Sydney, said Tasmania’s “Come down for air” theme was less “anti-tourist” and “more anti-overcrowded, line-joining, tourist destinations”.

“Lee Lin Chin is adding humour to the message,” he said. “Lee Lin Chin is an icon – intelligent, avant garde and [she has a] dry sense of humour.”

But McLennan said the campaign’s success depends on whether the real experience of visiting Tasmania lives up to its promise as well as delivering meaningful economic benefits to regional communities.

As Cop31 opens – this year the climate conference is in Turkey – there is plenty of speculation about whether China will finally step into the leadership role that it has long avoided. In its latest “five-year plan” adopted in March 2026, China for the first time pledged to “actively participate in and lead global climate governance”. Cop31 could provide the first indications Beijing is ready to put that rhetoric into action, something it has historically been reluctant to do.

Green energy is a vital component of the export engine that has kept China’s sluggish post-pandemic economy on track. The success of its exports stands in contrast to its failure to significantly boost domestic general consumption, a structural weakness that has persistently vexed leaders.

In 2025, clean-tech exports surpassed $220bn, and they should grow again this year; the world’s largest emitter is now the world’s leading supplier of clean energy technology. China accounts for about 85% of solar and 80% of lithium-ion battery supply chain production capacity, and in 2025, nearly 75% of electric cars were manufactured there.

Smart moves now by China would position it as a linchpin in a future electrostate world order, persuade rivals to cooperate, and solidify its influence in the global south.

Meanwhile other countries, including the US under Donald Trump, have fallen badly behind. Kerry said they would lose out economically – as well as putting the climate in further danger – unless they, too, embraced low-carbon power and rapid electrification. “The only country that has hugely accelerated is China. The rest of the world is stumbling along while Mother Nature is sending us more powerful signals every week, every day,” he said.

“[China] realised suddenly they had way too much dependency on oil and gas from other places, and they decided they needed to be independent. Other countries, since the war in Iran, and since the strait of Hormuz has been interrupted, have made a similar decision, and they’re moving independently. There’s been a massive uptick in deployment of renewables. Thank you, Donald Trump.”

For autocratic states without free and fair elections, seeing the economic benefits reaped by China would be the spur to adopt low-carbon growth, he added.

“They’ll see where the market is going, and they will recognise where the cheaper alternatives are. Even they can read a spreadsheet, and understand where costs are exorbitant [in fossil fuels], and where you have a better deal,” Kerry said. “Right now, renewables together with storage, together with some transitional gas, is cheaper and more effective, than just going with fossil fuels.”

He added: “The marketplace has made its decision and globally is moving to electrons, and electrostate status. People are going to electricity and they’re going to try to do that as clean as humanly possible. That’s the choice that I see being implemented in many different places. It is unstoppable.”

#47. Thursday briefing: What we do (and don’t) know about Alice Huang and the Nvidia affair

In today’s newsletter: US intelligence reportedly believes the former tech executive was recruited by Beijing, raising questions about the security of the AI industry and Trump’s close ties to Nvidia chief Jensen Huang

Good morning. Espionage is supposed to be a secretive business, and spies are supposed to be virtually invisible. That hasn’t been the case for Alice Huang, the mysterious Shanghai executive who formed a romantic relationship with Jensen Huang, the founder of Nvidia, one of America’s most powerful tech companies.

Many questions have arisen since it emerged that US intelligence believes that Huang (no relation of Jensen’s) is an asset recruited by the Chinese intelligence services to spy on behalf of Beijing. This morning’s newsletter explains what we know about the story, and what it tells us about the effectiveness of Chinese espionage. That’s after the headlines.

UK | Ministers are considering changing the law to force the entertainment industry to better protect women and children in the wake of the revelations over David Walliams and the conviction of Russell Brand.

Climate | The UK government has appointed the world’s first special representative for climate security, in the strongest sign yet of fears that climate breakdown will bring chaos to the country.

US midterms | Democrat Scott Colom has become the first Black senator in Mississippi to be elected since Reconstruction, in a stunning upset after more than a week of vote counting in one of the country’s most reliably red states.

Europe | Millions of people across Spain have joined the country’s first general strike in 14 years, setting up picket lines to protest against soaring housing prices before an election in which the far right could be catapulted into power.

Technology | Police officers risk arresting innocent people, especially from ethnic minorities, if they treat inaccurate facial recognition data as evidence, an official report has found.

Last month, the New York Times reported that Jensen Huang had formed a “close relationship” with Alice Huang, the former executive of a Singapore-based computing company, Megaspeed. Megaspeed is a customer of Nvidia; it buys the extremely valuable chips they produce, which are essential for powering AI, and rents computing space to Chinese companies. The US government had already been investigating whether Megaspeed could be flouting US restrictions and smuggling chips into China – allegations that both Nvidia and Megaspeed deny.

US intelligence officials, who had been conducting surveillance on Alice Huang, eventually concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset who had been passing on information to Beijing. The intelligence behind this assessment is classified, so we don’t know exactly what they found – or which intelligence body was conducting the surveillance. Nvidia said Jensen Huang’s interactions with Alice Huang were “not unusual or unexpected”, but on Sunday, Huang admitted in a letter to his employees that he had been having an affair. “I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children,” he said, adding that he was “deeply sorry”.

Nvidia’s valuation is inextricably linked to Huang. By yesterday, $430bn (5.5%) had been wiped off its share price, and in the early hours of this morning, that figure ballooned to about $600bn following reports that Nvidia’s board was deciding whether to oust Huang (you can follow the latest on that here). The company has appointed outside counsel to review the pair’s relationship and says it has no reason to believe that secrets or national security were compromised.

The most interesting aspects of this story are the questions we can’t (yet) answer. Did Nvidia’s board know that US intelligence suspected Alice Huang was a spy? What evidence led US intelligence to conclude she was spying for China? What information could she have passed to Beijing? And …

Reporters and sleuths on Chinese social media have been trying to get to the bottom of who the Megaspeed chief really is. On Tuesday, the Financial Times published an interesting report (£) about Alice Huang’s backstory. She was born in the rural area of Hunan, worked as a journalist at China’s state broadcaster, then at HSBC and the investment bank Citic Securities. In 2015, she joined an investment management company Hexi.

There, she met financier Kang Wei. The two became romantically involved, and together they reinvented themselves as AI entrepreneurs. In 2023, Huang and her business partners acquired Megaspeed, a Singaporean subsidiary of a Chinese gaming company, which she and Wei aimed to turn into a datacentre business. According to the FT’s report, the two pitched investors on their ability to secure access to Nvidia chips.

Nobody knows exactly when Jensen Huang met Alice Huang, when their relationship became romantic, or what information she might have passed to Beijing while it was going on. The earliest picture of them together is at an after-party following a tech trade show in Taipei, taken in 2024. As Aisha Down and Dan Milmo pointed out earlier this week, Jensen Huang is close to Donald Trump, so it’s possible that Alice Huang could have gained glimpses into US AI policy as it was being formed, into Trump’s thinking on AI, or into Nvidia’s company strategy. But without seeing the evidence, all this is speculation.

What does this reveal about Chinese espionage?

The US has been historically cautious about allowing AI chips to end up in Chinese hands. In 2022, Joe Biden’s administration introduced the Chips and Science Act, which restricted the sale of US chips in China, banning the export of most Nvidia chips. “The Chinese have a very strong pedigree for industrial espionage and copying other nations’ technologies,” Steve Tsang, the director of the SOAS China Institute, tells me. “They’ve been doing this for decades. And now it’s no longer engines they’re interested in, but AI.”

Both the US and China have an interest in gathering intelligence about each other’s AI firms. What’s more remarkable is the possibility that the Chinese spy agency, the MSS, could have recruited Alice Huang as a “honeytrap” to ensnare Jensen Huang – a method fraught with risk, which seems more reminiscent of 1970s spy fiction than contemporary counterintelligence (China, for its part, has rejected claims that Alice Huang was a spy).

“The facts clearly aren’t established, but it seems more likely that if Alice Huang was working for Beijing, the MSS would have recruited her after they became aware of her relationship with Huang, not before,” Nigel Inkster, the former director of operations and intelligence for the British Secret Intelligence Service, tells me.

China has built a large intelligence gathering operation: the MSS is now bigger than the CIA, and various national security laws mean that Chinese nationals can be required to provide assistance to the Chinese state. “You have a lot of people who can be co-opted into intelligence gathering, all of whom can be tasked with different requirements,” Inkster says. “Business delegations travelling abroad can be tasked with gathering information. So can visiting academics.”

“The big question, to my mind, is whether the Chinese intelligence agencies have actually been willing to deliver unwelcome information to the party leadership,” Inkster adds.

Jensen Huang is the most influential figure in the US AI industry outside major labs such as Anthropic, and has cultivated a close relationship with Donald Trump. He’s successfully lobbied the Trump administration to loosen regulations on AI and roll back restrictions on the sales of Nvidia’s chips to China (last year, Trump controversially approved the sale of Nvidia’s H200 chips directly to China in exchange for the federal government taking a 25% cut). Huang was present at the White House twice last month: once for a state dinner with Xi Jinping, and again for a meeting of tech and AI executives.

Huang’s close relationship with Trump could help explain why the president has so far played down concerns about Chinese espionage, appearing remarkably ambivalent about the recent allegations. The president was reportedly briefed last year about US intelligence conducting surveillance on Alice Huang, and in spring this year, his secretary of state, Marco Rubio, supervised the drafting of a classified memo warning that Jensen Huang was in a relationship with a Chinese spy. In May, Trump issued a last-minute invitation to Jensen Huang to board Air Force One in Alaska and join a delegation of executives bound to China. A US government official told reporters at the New York Times that when Rubio learned that Huang would be joining the trip in Alaska, he briefed Trump on the memo.

But on Monday, Trump, who is due to meet Xi Jinping later this month, said he had not known at the time of the trip about Huang’s relationship. He said the FBI had been investigating a matter relating to Huang, and defended the Nvidia CEO, describing him as an “honourable guy” who “built a great American company”.

Gibran has long faced controversy over whether his high school qualifications met legal requirements to run for office.

Indonesian law requires vice-presidents to have completed senior secondary school or its equivalent, but the Supreme Court found no evidence to prove Gibran had graduated.

"No evidence was found to show Gibran had a foreign diploma or certificate that could serve as the basis for equivalency with a senior high school or equivalent qualification," it said.

It also considered a Constitutional Court ruling from earlier in October which came to the same conclusion.

"You look outside and sometimes you see nothing. You have a big city around but you see nothing because there's no light," Daryna says.

Kyiv has been worst hit - its metro trains and trolleybuses at times brought to a standstill. Nationwide, hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians have gone without power in recent weeks.

We saw why on a visit to one of Ukraine's largest power stations, barely standing after a recent ballistic missile strike - the most powerful and devastating attack of Russia's campaign against Ukrainian energy targets.

Damaged parts from the power plant lie scattered on the ground

The enormous plant - which we've been asked not to name for security reasons - has been reduced to a pile of twisted metal and broken pipes, its turbines buried in rubble, taking the site completely off line.

Volodymyr Kovalenko was on duty in the control room keeping the plant working when the missiles hit at 3am.

"It was a very powerful attack. We'd never experienced anything like it. We'd had strikes before â part of the roof had collapsed, it was pretty grim. But this time it was particularly severe."

Kovalenko, who followed in his parents' footsteps and has worked at the site for 25 years, says the damage is heartbreaking, though he's confident it can be repaired.

But the work is being hampered by air raids every 15-30 minutes â seven times during our four-hour visit. Each warning of drones and further missiles forces teams to down tools and dive for cover, though during our visit all were shot down or flew on towards other targets.

If there isn't time to reach the site's main shelter, the workers hide in the foundations of its towering chimneys.

"It's very difficult," says Vadym Bobyr, who is leading the recovery effort. "You've mentally prepared yourself to do a particular job, and thenâ¦ the air raid siren goes off, and you have to get out as quickly as possible."

The 39-year-old, who has worked here half his life, is dwarfed by the mangled ruins and daunted by the challenge of rebuilding the plant.

"It's emotionally difficult, because we've already repaired all this, got it running again, and now it's happened all over again. It hurts, but we keep working and getting things done."

As we speak there's another alert - an incoming Banderol cruise missile.

The workers dart through the maze of passages and pipework but there isn't time to reach a shelter - one of the plant's thickest walls will have to do for cover. The missile was eventually intercepted 5km (3 miles) away.

The mangled wreckage of the Ukrainian power plant is still smouldering

Energy workers are at enormous risk. Since August there have been almost daily attacks on power facilities, claiming several lives every week. And it's getting more dangerous.

On the edge of the site, with the latest threat over, a team returns to reinstating the power lines feeding the national grid. But they won't be carrying electricity from the shattered plant before the end of this winter, at least.

President Volodymyr Zelensky says Ukraine still has enough generating capacity to meet electricity demand - the problem is getting it to homes and businesses when so many power lines and substations have been hit. Russia is using "waiter drones" that cling to pylons and cables and go undetected until Russia decides to detonate them.

Maxim Timchenko, CEO of DTEK, Ukraine's largest private energy company, said Russia was already putting the degraded electricity grid under even greater pressure than last year, and that overstretched air defences were unable to protect all of Ukraine's critical infrastructure.

"What is different is the scale of attacks and the different types of weapons used in these attacks. This is what is the difference - they've doubled in power and in scale".

He said rebuilding the damaged power station would take at least until next summer, because workers under sustained attack were limited to only one or two productive hours per day.

"We are fighting for survival of not only the whole country but ordinary people, especially during winter," Timchenko says.

Volodymyr Kovalenko has worked at the power station for 25 years

Kovalenko echoes the sense of mission about trying to keep the lights on. "Energy is the foundation of everything - people's lives, the state. Nothing can function without it. I've dedicated my entire life to this".

Many are choosing to leave the capital, or Ukraine altogether, to avoid months of bitter cold. Daryna, Kyryll and Olena know the winter has barely begun.

"It's the worst feeling," Kyryll says. "When I get home from school, I'm tired. I lay in my bed. Bam! Air alarm. Bam! No lights. Bam! A lot of homework. Bam!"

When the children are in bed Daryna confides her thoughts of moving to somewhere safer, or warmer. She's torn by the desire to stay at home - close to friends and relatives - and the worry of how unbearable life may become.

The strain is damaging her mental health, she tells me, and she fears the impact on Kyryll and Olena, who suffers nightmares. Still, Daryna remains defiant despite the darkness.

"I don't want to live without light. But actually people in Ukraine say, OK, without light, but without Russians. Yes, we're scared, we're scared a lot. But better for us to be without power, but [also] without Russians."

#1. Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza cleared of using AI to write prize-winning story

After a month-long investigation it was concluded that Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which won the £10,000 Caine prize, was ‘human-written’

Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza has been cleared of using AI to write a prize-winning short story, after the prestigious Caine prize for African writers concluded that his entry was “human-written” after a month-long investigation.

Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which charts the declining fortunes of a family after the father invests in fish tanks, was announced as the winner of the £10,000 prize on 6 October.

Five days later, the Atlantic published an article titled: “Yet another literary prize winner seems to be AI,” in which author and journalist Vauhini Vara said AI-detection software Pangram, which itself uses AI, had deemed the story to be almost completely artificially generated.

Caine prize chair Ellah Wakatama said in a statement: “From our detailed investigation, we feel the evidence is clear: The Blue Fighter was human-written. We now look forward to awarding and celebrating Simbarashe Steyn Kundizeza … before the end of the year.”

She said: “When concerns were raised, we have a duty as an organisation to look at them carefully and fairly,” adding that the prize had taken a “human-centred” approach to its investigation.

“Our review included interviews with the author, the 2026 judging panel and The Shallow Tales Review, where the story was first published,” Wakatama said. “It also included independent and expert-led metadata analysis of drafts written by the author.”

Kundizeza, who had strongly denied using generative AI to write the story, said he was “relieved” that the allegations had been dismissed after the investigation. “It was not necessary in the first place, I am happy to put all this behind me and continue with my writing journey,” he said.

Vara’s article prompted outrage online, with many claiming that Black writers, and Africans in particular, were being unfairly singled out by allegations they were using AI.
