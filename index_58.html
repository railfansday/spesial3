#33. French police officers sentenced for beating of music producer Michel Zecler

The three officers were seen on video beating up music producer Michel Zecler in his studio

Three French police officers have been convicted of beating up a black music producer six years ago in Paris in what the court upheld as an aggravated circumstance of racism.

The three were also found guilty of signing a falsified police report stating that the producer - Michel Zecler, 47 - had violently resisted their attempt to arrest him.

Their lies were exposed because the whole scene at Zecler's recording studio was caught on CCTV, revealing the producer had suffered some 30 punches, kicks and baton strikes while offering little resistance at all.

Aurelien Lapersonne, 29, Philippe Tondeux, 50, and Pierre Plantier, 37, all received five year sentences, with different amounts suspended.

Lapersonne had three years of his sentence suspended and Tondeux three and a half years suspended. Plantier had four years suspended, and will serve his one year with an ankle monitor.

All were told they could never serve in the police again.

The case became a cause cÃ©lÃ¨bre for critics of the police, who say it exposed systematic racism among officers. With police again facing criticism for alleged use of excessive force in stopping recent protests, the verdict was hotly awaited by Zecler's supporters.

Much depended in the two-week trial on Zecler's own testimony that the officers called him a racial slur during the beating.

This was denied by the three policemen, who in court admitted the beating and the faked report but denied there was a racial motivation. But the court preferred the evidence of the producer, because - unlike the police - everything else he said was verifiably true.

Because the racist element was accepted by the court, the sentences were toughened.

The case was heard by a panel of five judges.

The incident took place during the Covid lockdown in 2020. Zecler was accused by the officers for not wearing a mask when doing so was obligatory as he approached his studio on foot.

They later claimed his behaviour aroused their suspicions, and they thought he might be carrying drugs. No evidence of this emerged.

After Zecler entered the lobby, the police followed - without a warrant - and tried to bring him outside. Zecler refused to go out, and the beating ensued. Zecler cried for help and some people appeared from the basement and pushed the police outside.

The officers then smashed a glass pane and sent a tear-gas grenade into the building, forcing those inside to come out - after which Zecler was again hit. He was then arrested.

During the trial Zecler said had it not been for the CCTV video, he would have been in court not as a victim but as a potential criminal.

In their last words before the court, all three police officers apologised for what they had done. Tondeux said he was sorry "for the image of the police that I have given".

#31. Florida pastor found guilty over 2021 assassination of Haitian president

Christian Emmanuel Sanon faces life in prison after conviction on charges arising from killing of Jovenel Moïse

A Florida pastor with dual Haitian-American citizenship was convicted on Wednesday of US charges arising from the 2021 assassination of the Haitian president Jovenel Moïse.

Christian Emmanuel Sanon was found guilty on charges including conspiring to kill Moïse after a nearly eight-week trial in federal court in Miami. He had faced a total of eight charges including conspiracy to kidnap or kill a person outside the United States.

He faces up to life in prison at sentencing.

Federal prosecutors said south Florida was a hub for planning and financing the plot to oust Moïse and install a president of the conspirators’ choosing. The government said Sanon was the conspirators’ initial choice for Moïse’s replacement. Prosecutors also said Sanon helped raise money for the conspiracy and arranged travel to Haiti and lodging for himself and others in that country.

Sanon acknowledged in trial testimony that he was in communication at one point with people trying to replace Moïse, but pointed out that the group had moved on to another candidate long before the assassination. He denied any knowledge of plans to kill Moïse.

Sanon was born in Haiti and moved to the US as a teenager. He testified that he worked with churches in Haitian immigrant communities in Florida and the midwest.

Haiti has not had a president since authorities said that a squad of mostly retired Colombian soldiers killed Moïse and wounded his wife in a night-time raid at their home on 7 July 2021. The poorest country in the Americas, Haiti has a history of dictatorship, political upheaval and foreign intervention that has long hindered democratic rule.

The election of a new president is scheduled for next year.

Prosecutors said the scheme to kill Moïse included paying about two dozen foreign mercenaries with money obtained from US government programs that were intended to help businesses during the Covid pandemic, according to court records. US prosecutors have charged more than 40 people with crimes related to the assassination and a dozen have been convicted, including five who are serving life sentences.

The plot to oust Moïse began taking final shape in April 2021, when Sanon met in south Florida with a former Haitian senator and several US businesspeople, according to an indictment. The group agreed that Sanon, once in power, would award contracts to two Florida-based companies – Counter Terrorist Unit Federal Academy and Counter Terrorist Unit Security, collectively known as CTU – for infrastructure projects, security forces and military equipment, according to investigators.

CTU initially hired about 18 Colombian nationals with military training to provide security for Sanon, according to prosecutors. Conspirators also spent months obtaining weapons and body armor and attempting to build ties with Haitian gangs, officials said.

By June 2021, the conspirators realized Sanon had neither the constitutional qualifications nor sufficient popular support to become president. They shifted their support to Wendelle Coq Thélot, a former Haitian supreme court judge who died in January 2025 while a fugitive.

Sanon was previously scheduled to be tried with four other men who were convicted earlier this year, but his case was separated from theirs and delayed for health reasons.

#27. Race to be next UN secretary general exposes divisions among world powers

Debate about dominance of permanent five members could reignite if major powers block Costa Rican frontrunner

The race to become the next UN secretary general could reach a decisive moment on Friday when a UN security council vote is likely to reveal whether the Costa Rican frontrunner for the post would be blocked by the big powers. If so, the setback will reignite a debate about the dominance of the permanent five (P5) members at the United Nations.

Rebeca Grynspan, an economist and UN insider who is from a family of Polish-Jewish wartime refugees to Costa Rica, has been in the lead in all but one of the eight straw polls of the 15 UN security council members since the first secret ballot was held on 30 July. Each member goes down the list of candidates, casting an “encourage”, “discourage” or “no opinion” vote for each candidate.

Despite countries not being required to reveal how they voted, it is thought Grynspan’s candidature has been resisted by Russia and the US, two of the five permanent security council members that can ultimately wield a veto against a candidate. Knowledge among the security council delegations of how countries are voting is down to private intelligence, leaks and a process of elimination.

The intensive lobbying over the job has spread across the globe, but Friday is especially important since the poll is slated to differentiate the voting of the P5 from the 10 elected members (E10) of the security council. To be recommended as secretary general to the UN general assembly, a candidate needs nine votes and no vetoes. In Friday’s colour-coded secret ballot, the P5 will cast red ballots and the E10 white.

One diplomat said: “The electoral system means it is important how many votes you win, but equally how many of certain votes you don’t lose.”

The protracted 140-day deadlock confirms the polarisation that has hampered the incumbent secretary general, António Guterres, but also belies claims the role is now an irrelevance.

Friday’s secret ballot may also reveal whether the United Arab Emirates’ campaign for its diplomat, Lana Nusseibeh, is about to blow up in its face due to the hostility to the UAE’s brand of active, commercially driven diplomacy, not just in Sudan, but also Somalia, Western Sahara, Libya and Yemen.

A late entrant, in the two straw polls in which she has contested so far, Nusseibeh secured only seven “encourage” votes, six “discourage” and two “no opinions”. This is despite prestigious big-power backing and her acknowledged personal diplomatic skills.

Nusseibeh has US and Russian backing. The French foreign ministry may have misgivings about the UAE, but Emmanuel Macron, the French president, travelled to the UAE in the midst of French riots to see the UAE president, Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, and secure 100,000 metric tons of diesel a month.

In the UK, the foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, has kept his counsel, but he shares a Polish-Jewish refugee heritage with Grynspan and cannot be an admirer of the UAE’s human rights record or its evasion of EU sanctions on Russia. The prime minister, Andy Burnham, has UAE connections from his time as mayor of Manchester, and Nusseibeh is a fellow alumnus of the University of Cambridge. At the UN she worked closely with the UK in an informal group called “Friends of the Future of the UN”.

Yet 68 British MPs, including the Labour chair of the international development select committee, Sarah Champion, last week signed a letter calling on Burnham to veto Nusseibeh, describing her as “a tainted and compromised figure”.

Some of the opposition to her from elected security council members is not wholly political. Latin American countries believe an informal rotation system means it is their turn to lead the UN. As a result Panama and Colombia, currently on the security council, will oppose her. China has also been supportive of this rotation principle.

But others deeply object to what they consider to be the UAE’s dollar diplomacy and preparedness to use its extraordinary wealth to exacerbate and exploit pre-existing divisions in a country to gain a commercial foothold. Somalia, for instance, has a seat on the Security Council, and its president, Hassan Sheikh Mohamud, this week went on TV to accuse the UAE of buying ports, establishing military bases and supporting armed forces outside Somalia’s national security framework without federal approval.

It is also possible that Finland and Latvia, the two elected members with the greatest stake in Ukraine, are unimpressed that the UAE gave Vladimir Putin red carpet treatment when he visited Abu Dhabi in December 2023, nine months after the international criminal court had issued an arrest warrant for child trafficking. Greece, less engaged in Ukraine, may be more flexible.

Since the last poll, Nusseibeh has been to Bahrain, Britain and Pakistan, a clue as to where extra votes may be harvested. She also had a “thoughtful exchange” with Zeng Jixin, the Chinese ambassador to the UAE.

The two hurdles are whether someone so deep inside the UAE inner circle would have the courage in office to show true independence, and whether her active denials of UAE involvement in arming the RSF in Sudan leave her irretrievably tainted. From a Palestinian intellectual family, she insists she would make peace in Sudan her number one priority.

In the last ballot on 5 November, Grynspan won 12 votes in favour and three negative. But on 16 October – the only colour-coded ballot so far – which distinguishes between permanent and elected members – she received two discourage votes from permanent members, the equivalent of a veto. Nusseibeh had not joined the race at that point.

In an administration as personalised as the US, it has been hard for Grynspan to gain influence, with many sensing that Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, is the key influencer.

Grynspan has been reluctant to make the fact she is Jewish a feature of her candidacy, but the election of a Jewish person to run the UN might be seen as something the US could embrace as transformative. Her age – she is 70 – could be considered a drawback among those calling for a younger generation to renew the institution.

Although there are other Latin American candidates, Grynspan is seen as a more viable candidate than her rival, the Argentine Rafael Grossi, the head of the UN weapons inspectorate. Russia has opposed Grossi, objecting to his interventions over the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant in Ukraine.

One way or the other, the logjam has to be broken soon.

#23. ‘Weak and dizzy’: the secretive ICE force-feeding tactics that ended a Cuban father’s hunger strike

Exclusive: Luis Alejandro Cora Pérez says in messages to Guardian from inside Texas facility pain of force-feeding caused him to break strike

The text message that came from inside the Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detention center was stark: “I have spent five days eating regular food. If I don’t eat it, they put the tube in me to feed me.”

A Cuban asylum seeker, Luis Alejandro Cora Pérez, 32, said in messages to the Guardian this month that he was still weak and dizzy after three months without eating, but that he had broken his hunger strike because being force-fed was just too painful.

The Trump administration responded to requests for comment by denying it had force-fed Cora Pérez. But the detainee sent more details – and his is far from an isolated case.

Cora Pérez was one of two men on hunger striker at a Texas facility for whom ICE sought and received a federal judge’s permission to force-feed in recent weeks, a Guardian investigation found.

Cora Pérez and the other man were locked up inside the Port Isabel service processing center, an ICE detention facility in south Texas, just a few miles from the US-Mexico border, on the coast near where Elon Musk’s SpaceX launches rockets. Cora Pérez began a hunger strike in August to protest against his prolonged detention by federal immigration officials while his case plays out. At first he only drank water, then after three weeks, he says he began to drink one Ensure nutritional shake per day.

On 30 October, court records show ICE received permission to forcibly feed and hydrate him, and to restrain him while doing so.

The next day he says six guards at the center held him down, while medical staffers inserted a feeding tube through his nose and down his throat, and pumped nutritional liquid into his stomach – a process he called “traumatizing, both physically and psychologically”.

On 3 November, ICE then received permission from a federal judge to force-feed another hunger striker at Port Isabel, from Uzbekistan, federal court records show. The DHS says the Uzbek man has not been subjected to the practice, however.

On Wednesday night, after weeks of communications, Cora Pérez was suddenly transferred to another detention center in Texas. His relative says neither of them were alerted to any plans to transfer him, or a reason for it.

The DHS did not specify why Cora Pérez was transferred, adding that ICE makes custody determinations on a “case-by-case” basis based on “various factors”.

The revelations come as ICE’s detention practices continue to come under fire amid allegations of mistreatment, and as an increasing number of sporadic hunger strikes have been breaking out in ICE facilities across the country.

The two men’s cases in Texas, revealed here for the first time, are some of the most recent instances of government lawyers seeking a federal judge’s permission, as required by ICE policy, and receiving the go-ahead to force-feed detained hunger strikers. The Guardian’s reporting is based on court records, interviews, dozens of messages exchanged and an interview conducted with Cora Pérez via a facility phone. It further reveals how the Trump administration is increasingly obtaining such court orders to force-feed detained immigrants, a secretive practice widely condemned as torture by human rights organizations and medical ethics groups.

Detainees driven to hunger strike are typically using it as a last resort to protest against poor conditions and the Trump administration’s preference for arrest and prolonged detention while their civil immigration cases proceed through the system.

Cora Pérez said detention center staff inserted the feeding tube and left it in his body, a not uncommon practice, according to experts, and introduced nutrition – first a “clear liquid”, followed by a “milky liquid”, he recalled – through the tube twice more that day, before he cracked and said he broke his hunger strike, citing what he called “horrific torture”. He began voluntarily drinking nutritional shakes the following day, he said.

“ICE does not hold back on anything, and they do anything they want, like we have seen, even killing their own US citizens,” he wrote to the Guardian, referring to the fatal shootings of Renee Good by an ICE officer and Alex Pretti by a border patrol officer in January of this year during Trump’s pivotal immigration crackdown on Minneapolis.

Detained hunger strikers subjected to involuntary medical procedures, including force-feeding, have rarely spoken out about their cases or revealed their identities, much less while still detained. Cora Pérez’s messages offer a rare glimpse at conditions inside a detention center’s restricted medical unit where hunger strikers and suicidal detainees are held.

“They just took [the Uzbek man] in an ambulance after [him] fainting in front of the medical lobby where they take our vitals, our weight and our temperature,” Cora Pérez said in an alarmed message last Monday morning.

The Uzbek man was returned the following day to the facility, Cora Pérez said.

“I see him every day because I see him in the room in front of me, but they don’t let us talk to each other,” he added.

The Guardian independently confirmed the Uzbek’s man identity but is not publicly naming him, because efforts to contact him have been unsuccessful. The man arrived at the Mexico-US border in April 2022 and was released into the country while navigating his immigration case. He was arrested by border patrol officials in April of this year and placed in detention. The man does not appear to have any criminal record and the DHS did not point to any criminal history.

In a statement, DHS said that after they received the court’s permission to force-feed the Uzbek man, he “has voluntarily taken in fluids and there has been NO force-feeding or forced hydration”.

Security operations at the sprawling Port Isabel facility in Los Fresnos, Texas, are run by a private prison company, Akima Global Services, while healthcare is primarily handled by government workers.

“ICE respects detainees’ rights to exercise self-expression and medical autonomy by refusing nourishment and medical care,” the DHS said in a statement. “ICE Health Services Corps may provide involuntary medical treatment only to prevent imminent life-threatening harm or death. Medical staff may provide treatment only to medically stabilize the patient, bringing the detainee outside the imminent harm, incapacitation, or death.”

The US attorney’s office in the southern district of Texas, which represented ICE in its force-feeding request, did not respond to multiple requests for comment. Virginia-headquartered Akima Global Services, and its parent company Nana Regional Corporation, based in Alaska, did not respond to a request for comment.

Force-feeding cases are shrouded in secrecy. After a detainee launches a hunger strike, ICE often sequesters them in medical isolation cells for monitoring, according to ICE’s detention standards. The DHS said that ICE continues to offer hunger strikers three meals per day.

After a certain period of not eating, which varies per case, ICE will request a federal judge’s permission to force-feed the hunger striker. Many court records, and sometimes entire court dockets, are sealed and hidden from public view. In Cora Pérez and the Uzbek man’s cases, like in others, they did not have any legal representation to challenge the force-feeding requests.

The Guardian found at least five instances in which ICE requested permission to force-feed hunger strikers in Port Isabel since January 2025. The federal judge who ruled on Cora Perez and the Uzbek man, Rolando Olvera, has similarly ordered in at least three other cases, records show. Olvera’s judicial assistant said the court had no comment for this article.

ICE is known to have requested permission to force-feed at least 21 detainees across the US since January 2025, the Guardian found, but the number is probably higher since an unknown number of records and dockets remain sealed, unsearchable and not feasible to locate. Most of the cases coinciding with the second Trump administration and tracked by the Guardian are in Texas and Florida, with others in Georgia, Arizona and Washington state. Under the Biden administration, ICE force-feeding requests were also made in Louisiana and California.

Amy Zeidan, an emergency medicine physician based in Atlanta and an expert on healthcare in immigration detention, said force-feeding hunger strikers can amount to “torture”, and lead to significant psychological distress. She also said it was a violation of medical ethics.

“Essentially it’s like inserting a straw into your nose,” Zeidan said. “You have a gag reflex, so most times people will gag, and that’s really uncomfortable. And people will even vomit repeatedly if it continues to irritate their gag reflex.”

Zeidan added that the forceful insertion of a feeding tube can lead to the swelling of the nostrils and the throat, and even tear them, leading to bleeding.

“It is like torture – somebody is doing a painful procedure to you that you don’t consent to,” Zeidan added. “Autonomy is a fundamental principle of medical ethics. We can’t do something to patients if they don’t want to, even if we think it’s good for them.”

There are numerous cells and rooms inside the Port Isabel medical unit, including isolation cells and shared rooms for ill detainees, according to Cora Pérez. #8. Fears grow that US will put Nobel-winning judge under sanctions after Myanmar genocide ruling

A ruling against Myanmar could set precedent for court’s judgment on Israel’s actions in Gaza

Fears are growing that the US could impose sanctions on judges at the international court of justice, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court rules on Thursday that the Myanmar government committed genocide against the Rohingya.

The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide, and a significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove “intent” in a crime defined as acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

It is the role of independent judges to interpret the genocide convention. However, judges at the court have taken advice on how to protect their assets from potential US sanctions, as a genocide ruling could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime.

The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, an influential thinktank close to the White House, has predicted a genocide ruling will lead to the US imposing sanctions on the ICJ.

The US and Israel fear the final judgment may clarify how to establish a government’s intent to commit a genocide, and so set a precedent that makes it more likely the court will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza.

Myanmar, which has denied carrying out a genocide, claims its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ, sometimes described as the UN’s top court, is responsible for settling interstate disputes, unlike the international criminal court (ICC), which prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes.

The US has already imposed crippling sanctions on judges at the ICC after the court issued arrest warrants against the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. The US has also announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court”. Washington fears the ICC could go after US troops.

The Gambia, as a litigant, was entitled to appoint an ad hoc judge in the case against Myanmar and chose the South African lawyer Pillay, who in October was awarded the Nobel peace prize. She is required to act as a jurist and not an advocate for the Gambia.

Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, has sent a warning shot to Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges, saying Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponise the law against our troops”.

Waltz asserted last month: “The ICJ ad hoc judge Navi Pillay is tipping the scales on the ICJ’s current proceedings on Myanmar, intending to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponised against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.”

In remarks addressed to Pillay, he said: “We see your agenda and we’ll be watching the ICJ’s handling of these proceedings very closely.”

#4. One in four women globally unable to refuse sex within marriage, says UN

Almost half of women in west and central Africa cannot withhold consent, with girls worst affected, UNFPA finds

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In west and central Africa, almost half of women (45%) are not able to say no in their own marriage, while a third of women (33%) in east and southern Africa and in Arab states face the same predicament. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

Younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no, compared with older women aged 45 to 49, according to analysis in the report of eight countries with comparable data (Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia). The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

He said that as a first step rape laws across the continent needed to change. According to Murunga’s 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for what, outside marriage, would be classed as rape, and young wives have few options when it comes to protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

Underpinning these laws is the mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because, by marrying, wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. In many areas, religion plays a key role in affirming these beliefs.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organisation supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

She added: “In west Africa, sex is seen as an obligation in marriage. You must do whatever your husband demands.

“Once he has paid your bride price [a traditional marriage custom in which the bride’s family are paid money, livestock or goods by the groom], he owns you and you cannot reject his demands. Whatever he wants, he gets, including in terms of intimacy, finances and raising the children.”

#2. Race to replant five South African baobabs after export blocked

The trees, intended for an unnamed buyer in Georgia, were reportedly dug up without the necessary permits

Environmentalists in South Africa are racing to replant five centuries-old baobabs after the 20-tonne trees were dug up by an exporter, who tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia.

The baobabs arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, in north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they will be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. The trees had been stranded at a warehouse in Durban since July, when they were intercepted by authorities who said they had been moved without the necessary permissions.

The department of forestry, fisheries and the environment confirmed the trees had been destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted to be exported to Shekvetili Dendrological Park, owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned their export as biopiracy, but later allowed to go ahead.

Otherwise, such exports are very rare, say experts. David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors the trade in wild animals and plants, said: “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish.”

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation, who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. The winter months of May to October, when the trees lose their leaves and are dormant, is a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” she said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges associated with moving such large trees, which are transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five baobabs weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, which means a permit is required to cut or disturb them.

“A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself,” said Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya. “These trees are biodiversity anchors.”

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies, to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in its cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on its fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, added Ippolito. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

Baobabs have shallow roots, which means they do not need to be planted very deeply, and these regrow fairly quickly, said Venter. Once replanted, the trees will be stabilised with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

The trees are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” said Venter.

A criminal case has been opened against the owner of a landscaping company allegedly responsible for the illegal harvesting and transportation of the trees.
