#48. Survivors of Uganda's deadly landslide seek justice at the UN

Bududa has suffered repeated deadly landslides over the years

Survivors of a 2019 landslide in eastern Uganda that killed more than 50 people have taken their fight for relocation and compensation to the United Nations.

The 48 complainants accuse the government of violating Uganda's constitution by failing to put effective disaster management measures in Bududa, a mountainous district prone to landslides.

Their case comes as heavy rains raise the risk of further landslides. They say the government relocation programme ignores their safety, land rights and livelihoods.

The UN Human Rights Committee is considering what is believed to be the first case seeking climate adaptation measures, one that could set a global precedent.

At least 33 of the 53 people who died in the 2019 landslide were children. Years later, survivors say every spell of heavy rain brings back fears of another tragedy.

William Tsama, one of the claimants, lost four members of his family.

"Victims in such situations, ignored by governments, can take them to court to compel them to help their citizens."

Uganda introduced a resettlement plan for landslide-prone areas in 2011, but progress has been limited, with relatively few people relocated.

Last month, it launched a new resettlement programme offering affected households 18m Ugandan shillings ($4,300; Â£3,300) to help them move to safer areas.

But some survivors have questioned whether the amount is enough to cover the cost of relocating and rebuilding their lives.

Under the programme, families can choose between receiving two acres of land, valued at 8m shillings, plus 10m shillings towards building a house, or taking the equivalent land allowance in cash, alongside the same housing grant.

The scheme is expected to benefit 1,410 households identified as living in high-risk areas.

The government has now given residents of designated high-risk areas two months, from November to December, to leave or face forced eviction.

The threat extends beyond landslides. The complainants say changing rainfall patterns and declining soil fertility have also affected farming, leaving many families struggling to secure their livelihoods.

"I want to get a new good place for my people, so even if I die, they will be okay. I am an old man, but I am doing it for my children," Tsama said.

The survivors first launched legal action in 2020 in Uganda, and received support from Ugandan environmental organisation Greenwatch three years later.

They are seeking compensation for lost family members and livelihoods, as well as emergency measures, including accessible early-warning systems in high-risk areas.

Greenwatch Executive Director Samantha Atukunda Kakuru Mwesigwa said the case is about changing how governments prepare and respond to climate-related disasters.

"Justice for claimants is not just about winning in court. It is about forcing government policy to keep pace with a changing climate, and ensuring loss and damage is no longer an afterthought in disaster management," she said.

The threat of climate-related disasters extends across East Africa.

In Kenya, floods and landslides killed at least 181 people between March and May 2024, according to the Kenya Red Cross. In Rwanda, floods and landslides killed 135 people in May 2023.

The dangers are also being felt further afield. In October 2026, a flash flood swept through Nepal's Gorkha district after a landslide blocked a river, washing away homes and infrastructure, and killing over 1,500 people.

"Disasters like the one in Bududa are only getting worse because of climate change â and they are happening all around the world, as we've seen recently and tragically in Nepal," ClientEarth lawyer Vesselina Newman said.

She said poorer countries, which have contributed relatively little to global emissions, are bearing some of the heaviest consequences of climate change and need greater financial support.

"High income countries must provide financial assistance and ensure that sufficient money goes directly to countries like Uganda, so they can roll out life-saving adaptation measures," she said.

The complainants say relocation must involve more than moving people out of danger.

They want the government to protect their livelihoods, respect their land rights, and take account of individual needs.

The case is now before the United Nations Human Rights Committee, which will ask the Ugandan government to respond within six months.

#46. China, Russia and US agree peace declaration with no mention of Ukraine and Iran wars

Xi, Putin and Trump outline priorities that include UN reform, boosting oil supplies and ‘protection of diplomatic immunity’

China, Russia and the US say they have agreed a peace statement after an unprecedented trilateral summit, but the declaration made no mention of the wars in Ukraine and Iran caused by two of the participants.

The declaration on Thursday also outlined a set of priorities that included changing the UN, boosting oil supplies and what it said was the protection of diplomatic immunity.

The statement affirmed that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must not be fought”, but stopped short of suggesting the beginnings of a framework for denuclearisation.

The meeting between Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump has dominated the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) summit this week, with China, the host, promising it would be “groundbreaking”.

“We are really making history,” Trump told Xi and Putin at the start of the meeting. “No peace was ever forged by declining opportunities for engagement.”

Putin told a press conference later that the three men had had a “free discussion” and now had a “mutual understanding”.

Some had hopes that the talks, which lasted about one hour and 20 minutes, could be used to discuss reductions in arms between the world’s three largest nuclear powers. Beijing has previously rebuffed Trump’s advances to join such talks, pointing to the larger US and Russian stockpiles. On Thursday, though, Xi called for nuclear weapons to “be completely prohibited and thoroughly destroyed over time to make the world free of [them]”.

The language of the declaration around nuclear war is the same as that previously issued by the five permanent members of the UN security council, which includes France and the UK.

“The real test will be whether the three countries follow through on their stated commitment to discuss nuclear risk reduction and preventing nuclear proliferation,” said Patricia Kim, an expert on Chinese foreign policy at the Brookings Institution, a US thinktank. Even the suggestion that China would engage was significant, said Yun Sun, a senior fellow at the Stimson Center.

Analysts had said that the meeting’s primary significance was in its optics. “Overall, very little of substance seems to have been accomplished … This summit appears to have been all about the symbolism and prestige value,” said Prof William Hurst, from the University of Cambridge’s Centre for Geopolitics. Putin pushed back at the suggestion the summit had just been a photo-op. “It is a serious collaborative work,” he said.

In the declaration, the men “committed themselves to maintaining strategic stability and international security”.

First among their concerns was “promoting UN reform so that it may respond more effectively to global challenges”. Sun said this was a favourite topic of China’s and showed that Beijing was “flexing its diplomatic muscles”. She said: “It is able to set the agenda and the deliverables according to its priorities … and make other great powers accept them.”

The declaration also agreed that “the international criminal court [ICC] and other similar institutions must not abuse their jurisdiction”. Putin is subject to an ICC arrest warrant and so the clause added to the perception that he has done well from the summit. Since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the Russian president has been treated as an international pariah by Kyiv’s allies and he has not been to an Apec summit in years.

Hurst said: “This was no Yalta moment, but it was certainly a solid public demonstration of Russia’s (and Putin’s) continuing relevance and power. It’s yet another example of Putin playing a poor hand to maximum effect.”

Xi, his status as a global power broker now assured, began the morning by addressing Apec leaders, reiterating the themes of trade openness and innovation he has emphasised throughout the summit. However, Apec affairs appeared to take a back seat for most of the day, with business dominating the discussions. For all the camaraderie on display, relations are complex. Russia is under US sanctions over the Ukraine war, while China and the US spent much of the last year engaged in an escalating tit-for-tat trade war before Trump and Xi called a truce.

Trump and Putin earlier held a separate meeting – their first since August 2025 – where the US president called his Russian counterpart his “friend”.

European allies meeting in Berlin to show their support for Ukraine expressed indignation at the cosy optics. “Mr Putin is not our friend and ⁠will never be,” the Polish prime minister, Donald Tusk, said in a rejoinder to Trump. The Danish prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, said she found it “very incomprehensible” that a US ​president would want to sit down with Putin.

The peace declaration in Shenzhen said the three countries planned “to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products for the benefit of all nations”. That was important, said Shan Guo from Hutong Research, a Shanghai-based advisory firm, but “now let’s see if the US will lift sanctions”.

Trump and Xi then held their third bilateral of this year. Xi said the US-China relationship was “generally stable and positive”, according to state media. But he cautioned Washington over Taiwan, saying the US “must keep doing the right thing to avoid major setbacks in US-China relations”.

Another watched-for topic lacking detailed findings was that of AI. Xi said the two countries should “enhance communication and coordination to guard against security risks … and ensure that AI remains under human control at all times”.

Ultimately, the lack of breakthroughs might suit China, which has no appetite to move beyond the role of convener, said Kim. “Beijing has little interest in taking on the diplomatic risks of brokering an actual peace agreement in Ukraine or resolving the conflict with Iran. That would entangle China in conflicts it would rather avoid,” she said.

The site is estimated to hold deposits of critical minerals worth more than $60bn (Â£45bn), including niobium, used in electric vehicles, aerospace and defence technology.

Frontier Minerals says its proposal aims to keep more of the economic benefits in Kenya through "processing and refining of minerals in-country".

The deal comes as Washington seeks greater access to Africa's minerals, aiming to strengthen US supply chains and compete with China.

Frontier Minerals brings together three US companies - Bunker Hill Mining Corp, American Resources Corporation and ReElement Technologies Corporation. The consortium combines expertise in mineral resource development, critical materials supply chains and advanced refining technologies.

A central part of its proposal is to develop mineral processing and refining capacity in Kenya. Brad Barnett from Frontier Minerals said the company wanted the country to capture more value from its mineral resources.

Barnett was keen to reassure Kenyans that they would benefit: "We intend to create lasting opportunities for Kenyan workers, businesses and the wider economy through capacity-building and targeted investment, while prioritising environmental management and performance."

On Friday, Terrance Drew, the prime minister of the twin-island Caribbean nation, announced he would wind down the citizenship-by-investment (CBI) programme, after the European Union threatened to end visa-free access for St Kitts and Nevis.

Mark Brantley, the premier of Nevis, which has autonomous self-governance in some aspects, said he understood that the “country was given very little choice”, but did not “support the winding down of the program”.

The 42-year-old CBI programme, which St Kitts and Nevis pioneered, allows wealthy foreigners to obtain citizenship and ultimately a passport by investing in infrastructure and other development projects. It has brought billions of dollars into the country and in some years accounted for the largest share of government revenue.

In June, the European Commission wrote to the five Caribbean nations that currently operate CBI programmes: St Kitts and Nevis, Dominica, Antigua and Barbuda, Grenada and St Lucia. It warned that countries may lose visa-free access to its 29-country Schengen area if they did not phase out CBI.

In Friday’s statement, Drew blamed concerns over the programme on mismanagement by previous administrations, including years of underselling to undercut other countries, a controversial prison project, weak escrow protections and investment sums not being properly paid into the citizenship unit.

He said his government had led major reforms to the Caribbean’s programmes with international partners, including the US treasury, but argued that continuing the programme risked visa-free travel to parts of Europe and access to international banking. That could disrupt services allowing visitors to use overseas cards and families abroad to send money home.

Brantley, however, argued it was unfair for the EU to force St Kitts and Nevis to make a move without offering assistance to navigate the transition to other industries.

“I have not yet seen from the EU any corresponding suggestions of assistance. And we have to realise that these citizenship-by-investment programs, not only in St Kitts and Nevis, but … smaller countries of the Caribbean, have contributed significantly to our gross domestic product and also to ensuring that our people … can get adequate access to water and to education, to healthcare and the like,” he said.

Brantley described the 1 June 2028 deadline the EU gave the country to phase out the lucrative programme as “frankly ludicrous”.

In his statement, Drew said the CBI programme “did a lot of good, but it also created a danger”.

“When one source pays for half or more of everything, you are at its mercy. If it stops, everything stops. That is putting all of your eggs in one basket,” he added.

The prime minister said the government aimed to diversify its economy by strengthening tourism, yachting, and digital and creative industries, among others. He added that the sale of clean, geothermal energy was “at the heart of it” with drilling and exploration already planned.

“I will not pretend that the road is easy. Less CBI money means we must spend wisely and plan carefully. We will. Our plan is to make this change by growing our economy. Raising taxes is not part of that plan,” Drew said.

Antigua and Barbuda has also expressed willingness to end the programme, though it is trying to negotiate a longer transition period. The five Caribbean governments have taken differing positions on the EU demand. According to local media, Grenada has indicated it wants its programme to continue until 2032, while Dominica and St Lucia have responded to Brussels without publicly committing to ending their programmes.

#6. Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya minority, top UN court rules

Rohingya genocide: What was said in court and what happens now?

Myanmar has been found guilty of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority by the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

The verdict marks the first time a state has ever been found to have committed genocide by an international court.

Yuji Iwasawa, president of the UN's top court, said Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population and engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" to hide its crimes.

A military crackdown in 2017 killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar, formerly known as Burma. Myanmar denied the claims against it, saying its forces had responded to attacks by armed groups.

Rohingyas at the Cox's Bazar camp in Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now reside, watched the verdict live

The court found on Thursday that "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence" it had seen.

The verdict was reached by a majority of nine votes to six among a panel of judges from around the world. The ICJ also found in favour by majority that Myanmar had violated several other parts of the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The case brought by The Gambia centred around so-called "clearance operations" initiated in 2017 in Rakhine State, where many of the Rohingya Muslim minority resided.

After fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (Arsa) launched deadly attacks on more than 30 police posts there, Myanmar troops, backed by local Buddhist mobs, launched a wave of violence, prompting a mass exodus of Rohingya.

Refugees recounted entire villages being burned down and the mass killing of civilians, while numerous survivors and eyewitnesses provided testimony that the Myanmar military had also raped and abused Rohingya women and girls.

The ICJ determined that around 10,000 Rohingya were "violently killed" during the clearance operation, while more than 700,000 are thought to have fled to Bangladesh by foot or by sea. The court found that approximately 815,000 had been targeted in some way.

Iwasawa said Myanmar's military engaged in "indiscriminate" killings, the execution of men, "widespread" sexual violence, "extreme violence" against children and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes.

He said the military had also attacked Rohingya as they were fleeing to Bangladesh.

Myanmar, he added, had engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" by burning or disposing of bodies, burned and bulldozed sites that were cleared and failed to prosecute those who had perpetrated the acts of violence.

While the ICJ recognised the role Arsa played, it found there was "overlap" between an "anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign" - which portrayed the Rohingya as "sub-human" and "a violent threat" - and the clearance operations that made violence against the population more likely.

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In west and central Africa, almost half of women (45%) are not able to say no in their own marriage, while a third of women (33%) in east and southern Africa and in Arab states face the same predicament. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

Younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no, compared with older women aged 45 to 49, according to analysis in the report of eight countries with comparable data (Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia). The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

He said that as a first step rape laws across the continent needed to change. According to Murunga’s 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for what, outside marriage, would be classed as rape, and young wives have few options when it comes to protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

Underpinning these laws is the mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because, by marrying, wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. In many areas, religion plays a key role in affirming these beliefs.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organisation supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

#1. Race to replant five South African baobabs after export blocked

Environmentalists in South Africa are racing to replant five centuries-old baobabs after the 20-tonne trees were dug up by an exporter, who tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia.

The baobabs arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, in north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they will be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. The trees had been stranded at a warehouse in Durban since July, when they were intercepted by authorities who said they had been moved without the necessary permissions.

The department of forestry, fisheries and the environment confirmed the trees had been destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted to be exported to Shekvetili Dendrological Park, owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned their export as biopiracy, but later allowed to go ahead.

Otherwise, such exports are very rare, say experts. David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors the trade in wild animals and plants, said: “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish.”

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation, who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. The winter months of May to October, when the trees lose their leaves and are dormant, is a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” she said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges associated with moving such large trees, which are transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five baobabs weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, which means a permit is required to cut or disturb them.

“A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself,” said Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya. “These trees are biodiversity anchors.”

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies, to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in its cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on its fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, added Ippolito. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

Baobabs have shallow roots, which means they do not need to be planted very deeply, and these regrow fairly quickly, said Venter. Once replanted, the trees will be stabilised with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

The trees are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” said Venter.
