The war in Ukraine has cost an estimated $2.6 billion of damage to heritage and cultural sites in the country, the UN cultural body UNESCO said in a recently published report.

The report, which covers one year of war in Ukraine between February 24, 2022, and February 24, 2023, was a joint assessment conducted by the Ukrainian government, the World Bank, the European Commission, and the United Nations.

UNESCO was in charge of the part of the report dedicated to culture and tourism.

“As of February 24, 2023, the total damage cost from identified assets is estimated at $2.6 billion,” the report said in its assessment of damage related to culture and tourism.

Some $1.7 billion of that cost was distributed to “historic cities, buildings, and sites imbued with recognized cultural/social values,” $650 million to tourism facilities, $143 million to “movable cultural properties and collections, repositories of culture,” and $150 million to “buildings/workshops/ateliers dedicated to cultural and creative industries,” according to UNESCO.

The most impacted region in Ukraine was Kharkivska, which has suffered 30% of the total damage to its cultural assets, the report added.

UNESCO said that losses are estimated at around $15.2 billion, which includes revenue losses from tourism, art, sports, entertainment, recreation, cultural education, and creative and cultural industries.

“The war has significantly impacted the diversity and richness of culture and cultural heritage in Ukraine, causing damage to cultural infrastructure and assets, reducing livelihoods for cultural creators, bearers and practitioners, limiting access to culture, and impeding the exercise of cultural rights,” the report said.

UNESCO’s head Audrey Azoulay is currently in Ukraine, the organization told CNN on Monday.

Addressing the tribunal on the second day of hearings, Kohen said that the dispute over the archipelago of 700 islands in the south Atlantic – known in Argentina as the Malvinas – did not mean that the area was a “legal black hole” and that the UK could do as it wished with its natural resources.

“The fact that the parties dispute sovereignty does not turn the areas surrounding the Malvinas into some kind of Dominium maris Britannici,” he said. “We are not in the 17th century … We are in an era where we should put an end to the last vestiges of colonialism.”

Ellie Reeves, the attorney general for England and Wales, told the tribunal’s panel of judges on Tuesday that Argentina was seeking to further its sovereignty ambitions by applying for suspension of the drilling.

The remains had been outside Brazil since the 19th century. One was taken about 190 years ago from Salvador, the capital of the state of Bahia; the other, about 160 years ago, from Rio de Janeiro – and were welcomed back in moving, subdued ceremonies in both cities.

The skulls are believed to have belonged to Africans or Afro-Brazilians in the country that received more enslaved Africans than any other during the transatlantic slave trade.

Each was brought back in a padded box inside a reinforced suitcase, carried on flights by Brazilian diplomats after a four-year campaign to recover the remains from Harvard’s Peabody Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology in the US.

Brazilian researchers from local universities will now try to learn more about who the two individuals were.

The skulls will not be displayed in museums or have their images released, and the Brazilian government has pledged that they will be buried with funeral rites as soon as the identification process is complete.

“It’s very important to restore the dignity of these people, which was taken from them in life and in death,” said Carolina Dias dos Reis, a government official who travelled to Boston to sign the agreement with Harvard.

The existence of the skulls was revealed in 2022, and Brazilian researchers sought their repatriation from Harvard, but said they faced a drawn-out process that only moved forward after the Brazilian government became involved in late 2024.

In recent months, concerns grew that Donald Trump’s sometimes hostile behaviour towards his Brazilian counterpart, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, could derail the repatriation. But everything worked out.

“We need to recover our past as a country, however difficult and violent it was, so that we can understand what we are living through in the present: the consequences and scars of structural racism,” added Reis.

Slavery was abolished in 1888, but nearly a century and a half later, Afro-Brazilians make up the majority of the population while occupying the bottom of every socioeconomic indicator, dominating the statistics on hunger, poverty, gender-based violence and victims of homicides committed by criminals or the police.

During the repatriation ceremony in Rio de Janeiro, Ivanir dos Santos, a history professor and a cleric of the Afro-Brazilian religion Candomblé, poured a glass of water on to the ground as researchers received the suitcase.

“It was a ritual act because you cannot deal with the remains of our ancestors without paying reverence to them. This is not a research object … for us, that person is still alive in memory and as our ancestor,” he said.

It is not precisely known who the skulls belonged to. The one taken to Salvador is believed to have belonged to a man who took part in the most significant urban slave revolt in Brazilian history, in 1835, when about 600 African Muslims attempted to seize control of what was then the country’s second most important city. They were ultimately defeated by police, who killed 70 rebels.

The man died at a hospital, and a US diplomat, Gideon T Snow, is believed to have sent the skull to Boston.

If necessary, they will seek authorisation to carry out genetic or dating tests.

#2. Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza cleared of using AI to write prize-winning story

After a month-long investigation it was concluded that Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which won the £10,000 Caine prize, was ‘human-written’

The Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza has been cleared of using AI to write a prize-winning short story, after the prestigious Caine prize for African writers concluded that his entry was “human-written” after a month-long investigation.

Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which charts the declining fortunes of a family after the father invests in fish tanks, was announced as the winner of the £10,000 prize on 6 October.

Five days later, the Atlantic published an article titled “Yet another literary prize winner seems to be AI”, in which the author and journalist Vauhini Vara said the AI-detection software Pangram, which itself uses AI, had deemed the story to be almost completely artificially generated.

The Caine prize chair, Ellah Wakatama, said in a statement: “From our detailed investigation, we feel the evidence is clear: The Blue Fighter was human-written. We now look forward to awarding and celebrating Simbarashe Steyn Kundizeza … before the end of the year.”

She said: “When concerns were raised, we have a duty as an organisation to look at them carefully and fairly,” adding that the prize had taken a “human-centred” approach to its investigation.

“Our review included interviews with the author, the 2026 judging panel and The Shallow Tales Review, where the story was first published,” Wakatama said. “It also included independent and expert-led metadata analysis of drafts written by the author.”

Kundizeza, who had strongly denied using generative AI to write the story, said he was “relieved” that the allegations had been dismissed after the investigation. “It was not necessary in the first place, I am happy to put all this behind me and continue with my writing journey,” he said.

Vara’s article prompted outrage online, with many claiming that Black writers, and Africans in particular, were being unfairly singled out by allegations of the use of AI.
