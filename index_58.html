#32. Israeli military launches rare ground operation in Gaza beyond Yellow Line

Residents fled the area near Nuseirat and Bureij refugee camps following the Israeli evacuation order

Israeli ground forces have launched a rare operation in part of the central Gaza Strip outside their control, after issuing evacuation orders for residents.

Witnesses said tanks advanced east of Nuseirat refugee camp and rolled onto the territory's main north to south road under the cover of artillery strikes.

The Israeli military said the "targeted" operation aimed to dismantle a "terror tunnel" west of the so-called Yellow Line, which marks the more than 60% of Gaza's territory occupied by Israel.

The Palestinian armed group Hamas, which controls the rest of Gaza, accused Israel of a "flagrant violation" of the October 2025 ceasefire agreement.

Violence has continued in Gaza over the past 13 months, with near-daily Israeli strikes, and living conditions for the 2.1 million people there remain dire.

Early on Wednesday afternoon, Israeli military body Cogat's Arabic spokesperson announced the start of the incursion and warned Palestinians living in an area north of Nuseirat and Bureij refugee camp to stay away for their own safety.

It also said a section of the north to south Salah al-Din road that passed through the area would be closed to traffic.

Later, the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) said it had begun a joint operation with the Shin Bet security service "to dismantle a terror tunnel west of the Yellow Line that posed an immediate and direct threat to IDF troops".

"IDF troops under the Southern Command are deployed in the area in accordance with the ceasefire agreement and will continue to operate to remove any immediate threat and thwart attempts by the Hamas terrorist organisation to harm Israeli civilians or IDF troops, or to violate the agreement," it added.

Hamas warned in a statement that the advance of Israeli military vehicles beyond the Yellow Line and the evacuation of residents constituted "a dangerous development and a flagrant violation of the ceasefire agreement".

Footage published by Palestinian media outlets and on social media showed families fleeing areas near Nuseirat camp following the announcement.

In one video verified by BBC Verify, a man can be seen ducking after hearing artillery fire and then running along Salah al-Din road, about 1.5km (0.9 miles) west of the Yellow Line. "The Israeli occupation now is firing randomly near the houses and tents of people," he says.

Automatic gunfire can be heard in another video filmed just to the south-east.

A resident of Nuseirat who was forced to flee told BBC Arabic's Middle East Daily programme: "The situation is now extremely dire, with Israeli forces firing indiscriminately at areas where displaced people have sought shelter."

"We couldn't take any of our belongings with us - not even our blankets, pillows, or clothes. We had no choice but to leave everything behind."

A second resident also reported "indiscriminate gunfire and shelling" and said there had been "numerous casualties, including fatalities".

Asked about reported fatalities, the IDF sent a general statement about the operation.

One man was killed in Nuseirat, the second to the north in Gaza City, and the third in the central town of Deir al-Balah, it added.

The IDF told the BBC that it had "struck several military operatives".

At least 1,658 people have been killed in Israeli attacks in Gaza since the ceasefire began last year, according to the territory's Hamas-run health ministry, whose figures are considered reliable by the UN.

The Israeli military says six of its soldiers have been killed over the same period.

Meanwhile, more than 1.9 million people - about 90% of Gaza's population - remain displaced, with 1.4 million of them living in tents or makeshift shelters, according to the UN.

Reconstruction has yet to begin in Gaza, where an estimated 82% of all structures have been destroyed or damaged during the war, as US President Donald Trump's peace plan has largely stalled.

#30. Florida pastor found guilty over 2021 assassination of Haitian president

Christian Emmanuel Sanon faces life in prison after conviction on charges arising from killing of Jovenel Moïse

A Florida pastor with dual Haitian-American citizenship was convicted on Wednesday of US charges arising from the 2021 assassination of the Haitian president Jovenel Moïse.

Christian Emmanuel Sanon was found guilty on charges including conspiring to kill Moïse after a nearly eight-week trial in federal court in Miami. He had faced a total of eight charges including conspiracy to kidnap or kill a person outside the United States.

He faces up to life in prison at sentencing.

Federal prosecutors said south Florida was a hub for planning and financing the plot to oust Moïse and install a president of the conspirators’ choosing. The government said Sanon was the conspirators’ initial choice for Moïse’s replacement. Prosecutors also said Sanon helped raise money for the conspiracy and arranged travel to Haiti and lodging for himself and others in that country.

Sanon acknowledged in trial testimony that he was in communication at one point with people trying to replace Moïse, but pointed out that the group had moved on to another candidate long before the assassination. He denied any knowledge of plans to kill Moïse.

Sanon was born in Haiti and moved to the US as a teenager. He testified that he worked with churches in Haitian immigrant communities in Florida and the midwest.

Haiti has not had a president since authorities said that a squad of mostly retired Colombian soldiers killed Moïse and wounded his wife in a night-time raid at their home on 7 July 2021. The poorest country in the Americas, Haiti has a history of dictatorship, political upheaval and foreign intervention that has long hindered democratic rule.

The election of a new president is scheduled for next year.

Prosecutors said the scheme to kill Moïse included paying about two dozen foreign mercenaries with money obtained from US government programs that were intended to help businesses during the Covid pandemic, according to court records. US prosecutors have charged more than 40 people with crimes related to the assassination and a dozen have been convicted, including five who are serving life sentences.

The plot to oust Moïse began taking final shape in April 2021, when Sanon met in south Florida with a former Haitian senator and several US businesspeople, according to an indictment. The group agreed that Sanon, once in power, would award contracts to two Florida-based companies – Counter Terrorist Unit Federal Academy and Counter Terrorist Unit Security, collectively known as CTU – for infrastructure projects, security forces and military equipment, according to investigators.

CTU initially hired about 18 Colombian nationals with military training to provide security for Sanon, according to prosecutors. Conspirators also spent months obtaining weapons and body armor and attempting to build ties with Haitian gangs, officials said.

By June 2021, the conspirators realized Sanon had neither the constitutional qualifications nor sufficient popular support to become president. They shifted their support to Wendelle Coq Thélot, a former Haitian supreme court judge who died in January 2025 while a fugitive.

Sanon was previously scheduled to be tried with four other men who were convicted earlier this year, but his case was separated from theirs and delayed for health reasons.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has quickly become a big name in the tech industry

Anthropic's stock market debut will make it the first US firm focused exclusively on AI to go public. It will also mark a crucial test for the fast-growing industry, which faces questions over whether the huge amounts of money being poured into the technology will be profitable.

The firm said earlier this month it would offer its shares for between $70 and $78 each, which valued the company at up to $1.3tn.

But demand for Anthropic's stock debut - commonly referred to as its initial public offering, or IPO - is very high, according to one early investor in the company who spoke with the BBC on condition of anonymity.

If that turns out to be the case, Anthropic's valuation could quickly blow past its target on Thursday when trading begins.

However, in the weeks that followed, SpaceX shares fell significantly in price.

As it stands, Anthropic's listing will see the firm raise more than $68bn from its initial investors. It is also expecting another $26bn from private placements. So the total amount the company will raise is expected to be $94bn.

The amount of cash Anthropic has raised from its listing is already more than SpaceX raised in June.

OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT and Anthropic's biggest AI rival, is expected to go public next year.

Until then, Anthropic will be the only public company in the US that is entirely focused on the technology, or one that was founded and built around AI tools and products. Its scope is different from tech giants like Google and Microsoft that also develop AI but are involved in other tech services.

Yang Wang, a principle financial analyst with Counterpoint Research, said the central issue for Anthropic is whether its business "can keep pace" as hundreds of billions of dollars in financial obligations come due in the next couple of years.

"Anthropic going public gives us a whole new window into what AI demand looks like," Marta Norton, who heads strategy at investment firm Empower, told the BBC.

Anthropic was started by a group of co-founders, led by siblings Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, all of whom left their work at OpenAI to start a new AI firm.

The company has carved itself out in the ongoing AI race as more focused on building the technology with safety top of mind.

It refused to comply with a demand by the US Department of Defense that Anthropic's tools be used in an essentially unrestricted way.

Rival firm OpenAI has also voiced similar concerns about the technology's risks and delayed its plans to list on the stock market to address AI safety concerns.

#16. Five EU states announce plans for immigration ‘return hubs’ in Rwanda

Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Denmark and Greece likely to face challenges to policy criticised as ‘grave moral failing’

Five EU member states have announced plans to set up an offshore centre in Rwanda for people with no legal right to stay in Europe, in a pilot scheme that is expected to face legal challenges and has been denounced as “a grave moral failing”.

The five countries – Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark and Greece – are the first to seek to use the EU’s newly agreed returns law that allows people who have exhausted all avenues of appeal against their removal from Europe to be sent to a third country.

In a statement released on Monday the five EU countries said “concrete negotiations with partner country Rwanda will begin for implementation”, saying the scheme would make “an important contribution to further curbing illegal migration, dismantling the business model of smugglers and relieving the burden on our population”.

Officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

In theory, according to diplomatic sources, migrants would stay in a reception centre in Rwanda for a couple of months, before choosing to integrate into life in the Great Lakes country or return to their home nation.

Unlike the failed scheme pursued by the British Conservative government in Rwanda, or Italy’s agreement with Albania, it will not apply to people pursuing asylum claims.

The reception centre, which would be an open facility, was not intended to house people long term but to accommodate regular flows of people, the countries said. Diplomatic sources said the EU countries were not using British-funded facilities built in Kigali.

#11. Myanmar faces historic genocide verdict at UN court over Rohingya massacres

When the judges of the International Court of Justice read out their verdict in the seven-year-old genocide case filed by The Gambia against Myanmar on Thursday, they could make history.

In the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in 1948, after the Nazi Holocaust, dozens of individuals have been successfully prosecuted for the crime of genocide, in conflicts such as those in Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia.

But no state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide.

"It would be a judgment of landmark importance if The Gambia were to win this case," says Arsalan Suleman, the lawyer leading the case against Myanmar at the court. "It would give renewed vitality to the 1948 Genocide Convention and to the notion of international legal accountability for crimes committed by a state."

The mass expulsion of the Rohingya population of Rakhine State in August and September 2017 was without question a military operation of exceptional brutality.

I was among a very small number of journalists who witnessed the deliberate burning of Rohingya villages in Rakhine, and also the dreadful human suffering of the hundreds of thousands who fled to neighbouring Bangladesh.

Their accounts of killing, torture and mass rape by Burmese soldiers have been thoroughly documented by human rights groups and a United Nations fact-finding mission, external.

I have on many occasions interviewed ethnic Rakhine people about their animosity towards the Muslim population, and I have documented the systematic obliteration of what remained of Rohingya villages by the Myanmar state.

But the question posed at the UN's top court, the ICJ, is whether all of this amounted to genocide.

Hundreds of thousands of Rohingya fled across the Myanmar border in 2017 as attacks on them grew

The ICJ was established in 1945 to help settle disputes between states, not to try international crimes. However, it was chosen to address the attacks on Rohingyas in Myanmar because there were few other avenues of redress for the victims.

Myanmar authorities have denied accusations of ethnic cleansing or genocide, saying they were only responding to terrorist actions by Rohingya militants with so-called military "clearance operations".

A UN report in 2018, based on hundreds of interviews, said the army's tactics were "grossly disproportionate to actual security threats". It said top military figures in Myanmar must be investigated for genocide in Rakhine State and crimes against humanity in other areas. Myanmar rejected the report.

As Myanmar was not a signatory to the International Criminal Court, it was not possible to file a case there against the officials accused of atrocities in Rakhine State.

Instead in November 2019 The Gambia, backed by the Organisation of Islamic Co-operation, filed a case at the ICJ, arguing that all states are bound by the Genocide Convention, so all have an interest in upholding it.

It accused the state in Myanmar, not just the military, of genocide.

This was a novelty in international law; a state not directly affected by an act, arguing that it had legal standing to make a complaint because it was indirectly affected by any violations of such an important convention.

Myanmar objected to this argument, but the ICJ judges ruled that The Gambia was eligible to file the case.

The case drew even more attention when the Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi, who at the time headed the elected government in Myanmar, chose to lead the initial legal defence of her country at The Hague in person, for which she was widely criticised.

She had very limited influence over the actions of the Myanmar military in Rakhine State, but her refusal to condemn them dismayed many of those who until then had admired her courage and adherence to democratic principles.

Since she was deposed by a coup in 2021 and placed under arrest the defence has been led by a new team appointed by the military.

Historically, proving that genocide has been committed has been very difficult. Judges apply a high bar to this.

Typically, where there is no explicitly genocidal programme, like that of the Nazis, the judges require that the evidence presented at court must be such that the only reasonable inference from the actions of the defendants is that they had genocidal intent.

When Bosnia-Herzegovina filed a genocide case against Serbia at the ICJ in 1993, it took the court 14 years to conclude that while incidents like the 1995 massacre at Srebrenica did amount to genocide, there was not sufficient proof that the state of Serbia had genocidal intent, though it was found to have failed to prevent genocide.

Myanmar's lawyers and Aung San Suu Kyi have argued something similar.

Suu Kyi said that The Gambia's team mischaracterised a complex conflict and military operation, and that while she accepted that war crimes may have been committed against the Rohingyas, that is not proof of genocidal intent.

"What we presented to the court was the long history of discrimination targeting the Rohingya, and how that history, and the policies connected with it, provide a background to understand the government's language, rhetoric, propaganda, and policies that were part of the 2017 'clearance operations'," Suleman said.

"We discussed the 1982 citizenship law, and the tightening noose of discriminatory laws that restricted access to education, healthcare, livelihoods, and travel - all of which made life really impossible for the Rohingya and, we argued, was one of several indicators of genocidal intent."

Myanmar would then find itself tarnished by having committed "the crime of crimes", as sitting on the same bench as Nazi Germany, the Rwandan Hutu militants and the Bosnian Serbs at Srebrenica.

That might seem a setback for Min Aung Hlaing, the general who now runs Myanmar, who above all was responsible for the military's operations in 2017, and who has been trying to improve his dismal international image in recent months.

There is no right of appeal against an ICJ verdict. However, there is also no penalty that follows an ICJ verdict.

The Burmese coup leader is focused mostly on rebuilding relations with neighbouring Asian countries, who are unlikely to pay too much attention to such a verdict, and much less on rebuilding relations with Western countries who do care.

"This is like formal recognition of our suffering," says Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya who was driven out of the Rakhine town of Buthidaung in 2017 and now lives in Canada.

"For the Rohingya, this trial is a moment of acknowledgement of our suffering after years of denial. The Rohingya have been waiting for justice for decades, not just this year."

Rakhine State has been ravaged by a conflict between the military and the insurgent Arakan Army, which now controls most of the territory. It is supported by the indigenous Rakhine population who are very hostile to the idea of the Rohingyas coming back.

#3. This is a remarkable diplomatic moment for China's Xi - but it comes with pitfalls

Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping are meeting for a historic trilateral summit

Xi Jinping will believe he has achieved something no other world leader could in securing a historic summit with Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump in Shenzhen.

For decades, Washington and its western allies shaped the world order.

Yet it is China that has orchestrated the remarkable diplomatic spectacle of three of the world's most powerful protagonists sitting around the table.

And surrounding it all is the futuristic display that is Shenzhen, with driverless cars cruising past as drones fly overhead and robots - literally - dance in the streets.

Ostensibly, this is an economic summit for Asia Pacific leaders. But for Xi, it is also a demonstration of China's grip on the global economy and world politics.

His country is no longer just reacting to decisions made in Washington, London or Brussels - instead Beijing is at the heart of conversations which are reshaping the international order.

But this meeting is not without its pitfalls for Xi as there are many things he does not appear keen to discuss with Putin and Trump.

First, China is not keen for the war in Ukraine, now well into its fifth year, to be on the agenda.

This is a delicate balancing act for Xi who is aware his relationship with Putin may damage China's connections in Europe. The EU is a key market, especially now when the Chinese economy is heavily relying on exports.

However Xi also doesn't want the war to end in a way that will destabilise Russia, a key ally and neighbour.

Beijing is still trying to maintain that it is neutral in this war and has repeatedly called for negotiations and a political settlement while avoiding condemnation of Russia's full-scale invasion.

Russia's invasion of Ukraine, now into its fifth year, has claimed hundreds of thousands of lives

The US, especially under the previous Biden administration, and Europe have accused China of sending Russia components that it could use in its war in Ukraine, while also propping up the country's economy by buying oil and gas.

Xi wants to keep Putin close, but not too close because he also doesn't want Russia's problems to become China's problems.

Donald Trump has already made it clear he wants the three leaders to discuss limiting the world's supply of nuclear weapons.

The US and Russia possess the world's largest stockpile of nuclear warheads. But China is rapidly expanding its own arsenal, which is raising alarm bells in Washington.

The last major US-Russian arms control treaty, known as New START, expired in February this year, triggering fears of an arms race. The agreement had set a cap to control the number of nuclear weapons in US and Russia's arsenal.

Trump would like China to be included in any new agreement, but Beijing has pushed back.

Xi's argument will be that his arsenal is considerably smaller than what Washington and Moscow own, and, therefore, it would be unfair for Beijing to be bound by their limits. China also believes it should have the freedom to develop its own nuclear capabilities.

This subject could complicate Xi's bid for China to be recognised as a leader on the world stage - because the US could argue that the status includes being a responsible nuclear power.

Xi will want to control the optics of this meeting, from start to finish.

Chinese officials like to nail down agendas with talking points and produce carefully choreographed speeches.

But in this unprecedented summit, Xi will have to live with two very unpredictable leaders.

Both Trump and Putin have their own agendas and the US President has already threatened to go off script.

There is a risk that they both dominate the meeting and Xi ends up looking like a host, rather than the architect of a major diplomatic moment.

Of course, Xi may be ready for this. It may even play into his hands.

The Chinese leader has been trying to portray himself as a stable global leader - at this summit he will be flanked by two men embroiled in wars they cannot seem to end.

So any unscripted moment could serve to highlight his preferred role as senior statesman.

For all the attention, this meeting of three men with vastly different agendas is unlikely to yield any significant outcomes.

But the power it projects is important for Xi as he heads towards next year's Communist Party Congress, where he is expected to secure a historic fourth term as leader of the party and country.

He may hope to use this as propaganda at home, given the country is grappling with huge domestic challenges.

A country-wide property crisis has dented consumer confidence. Local governments are heavily in debt. Manufacturing has slowed and more of China's workforce has become dependent on flexible jobs with no pension or social security. One in five young people are thought to be struggling to find work. Frustration and anxiety about the future bubble up routinely.

Xi does not have to deal with elections, and the country has not seen major protests in decades. But he will be concerned about his popularity within the party elite as these problems - and the palpable frustrations they produce - grow.

At a time like this, a photo-op with Putin and Trump may offer him proof that his country's "century of humiliation" is over. China is no longer a rising power, he can argue - rather, it is increasingly shaping the rules of a new era.

#2. One in four women globally unable to refuse sex within marriage, says UN

Almost half of women in west and central Africa cannot withhold consent, with girls worst affected, UNFPA finds

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In west and central Africa, almost half of women (45%) are not able to say no in their own marriage, while a third of women (33%) in east and southern Africa and in Arab states face the same predicament. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

Younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no, compared with older women aged 45 to 49, according to analysis in the report of eight countries with comparable data (Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia). The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

He said that as a first step rape laws across the continent needed to change. According to Murunga’s 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for what, outside marriage, would be classed as rape, and young wives have few options when it comes to protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

Underpinning these laws is the mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because, by marrying, wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. In many areas, religion plays a key role in affirming these beliefs.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organisation supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

She added: “In west Africa, sex is seen as an obligation in marriage. You must do whatever your husband demands.

“Once he has paid your bride price [a traditional marriage custom in which the bride’s family are paid money, livestock or goods by the groom], he owns you and you cannot reject his demands. Whatever he wants, he gets, including in terms of intimacy, finances and raising the children.”
