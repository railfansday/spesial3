#80. Suspected Chinese spy balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing

That raises questions about whether there is intelligence the balloon was able to gather that the US still doesn’t know about.

Still, the intelligence community has not been overly concerned about the information the balloon was able to gather, the person said, because it is not much more sophisticated than what Chinese satellites are able to glean as they orbit over similar locations.

A US intelligence official told CNN on Monday that “although analysis of the wreckage of the High Altitude Balloon remains ongoing, thus far, its flight over the United States does not appear to have provided critical new insights to the People’s Republic of China.”

The US also knew what the balloon’s path would be and was able to protect sensitive sites and censor some signals before the balloon was able to pick them up, officials have said.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

The balloon first crossed into US airspace over Alaska in late January before passing through Canada and down into Montana, where it hovered for a few days, leading the US to believe it was trying to surveil sensitive military sites, like Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana. It was eventually shot down by the US off the East Coast on February 4, and the incident further escalated tensions between Washington and Beijing, including the postponement of a diplomatic visit by US Secretary of State Antony Blinken to China.

A senior State Department official said in February that, as the balloon floated across the US, it “was capable of conducting signals intelligence collection operations.”

Gen. Glen VanHerck, the commander of US Northern Command and NORAD, said at the time that the US “did not assess” that the balloon “presented a significant collection hazard beyond what already exists in actionable technical means from the Chinese.”

The surveillance program, which includes a number of similar balloons, is in part run out of the small Chinese province of Hainan, officials told CNN. The US does not know the precise size of the fleet of Chinese surveillance balloons, but sources tell CNN that the program has conducted at least two dozen missions over at least five continents in recent years.

Roughly half a dozen of those flights have been within US airspace, although not necessarily over US territory, according to one official familiar with the intelligence.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

#71. California governor commutes sentences for state's 548 death row inmates

California Governor Gavin Newsom's term in office comes to an end in January

California Governor Gavin Newsom has commuted the death sentences of all 548 people on death row in the state, changing them instead to life in prison.

"We can now let these offenders die in obscurity while we forever remember and honour the victims," he said in a letter.

The decision to spare death row inmates is largely symbolic in a state that has not executed an inmate since 2006, though experts say it could save millions of dollars in court costs each year. Only 13 inmates have been executed since voters reinstated the death penalty in 1978.

The decision comes as Newsom, the Democratic leader of the most populous US state, has been discussed as a possible 2028 contender for president.

Newsom, who is term limited, is winding down his final months in office. Fellow Democrat Xavier Becerra is projected to take over after the midterm elections on Tuesday.

Newsom explained in his letter that he is exercising his constitutional clemency authority to commute the sentences to life without the possibility of parole.

"After nearly 30 years in public office, I have come to understand the limits of what government can justly accomplish," Newsom said. "I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of our legal system and diminishes each of us".

California has historically had the largest population of death row inmates. Newsom had previously ordered a halt to the death penalty in the state in 2019.

Americans' views on death penalty after failed execution in Tennessee

Describing his decision as a difficult but "righteous action", Newsom said that although many Californians feel that those who commit brutal crimes deserve to die, he has concluded that "the state cannot morally kill them".

He said that by every measure, California's system for administering the death penalty "is a failure", and that it has not and cannot be fairly applied, resulting in the conviction of innocent people.

Life without the possibility of parole is the most severe punishment he can lawfully impose and that the state can ethically enforce, he said.

He acknowledged that his clemency action will anger and hurt some Californians and said that it will "always weigh heavily on me".

"As governor, I alone bear responsibility for this decision and its impact, just as I alone would bear responsibility for each execution," he wrote.

"For this reason, I am compelled to follow my conscience and, as authorised by our Constitution, take this action for the welfare of the State of California."

Because individual states have the power to prosecute crimes and order executions, much of the power lies with state governors.

"I detect a very similar ethical positioning in Governor Newsom's statements about the commutations. And here too, it may well be something that the voters respect," he said.

#41. Peru airport officials discover 3,000 tarantulas smuggled inside dirty socks

Three suitcases’ worth of the spiders were on their way to Germany, where they could fetch an estimated €1.5m

More than 3,000 tarantulas were seized at an airport in Lima on their way to Germany, Peruvian authorities said.

The spiders were hidden inside dirty socks contained in three suitcases carried by two Germans and a Polish citizen, the National Forestry and Wildlife Service (SERFOR) told Agence France-Presse on Thursday.

They were spotted after agents noticed some unusual wriggling inside the luggage during a routine customs inspection at Jorge Chávez international airport. “We noticed that some of them were moving. There were small ones and large ones,” said Cora Suarez, a SERFOR wildlife control technician.

One tarantula can sell for about €500 ($560) in Europe, Suarez said, meaning the wildlife trafficking scheme would be potentially worth €1.5m for just one trip.

The hidden arachnids, which came from several regions of Peru, were found in varying stages of development, from eggs and hatchlings to adults, Suarez said.

The Europeans accused of smuggling the tarantulas were detained by police specialising in environmental crimes and will face criminal proceedings, SERFOR said.

Illegal wildlife trafficking is a highly lucrative criminal activity that generates around $20bn annually worldwide, according to Interpol.

Such trafficking is among the primary causes of the loss of biodiversity on the planet.

#39. Pope begins lengthy trip to Latin America weeks after lung cancer surgery

Pope Leo is travelling to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru

Pope Leo XIV is beginning a lengthy trip to Latin America just three weeks after surgery for lung cancer - a tour that includes a much-anticipated "homecoming" to Peru where he was a priest for many years.

The Vatican says no extraordinary measures are being taken in relation to the Pope's health, describing him as a "71-year-old who has undergone an operation" not someone with a "permanent medical condition".

It was Leo himself who revealed last month that doctors had discovered a tumour during a routine scan. The growth was removed along with the lower lobe of his right lung and his medical team then confirmed the cancer had not spread.

At a briefing in Rome ahead of the trip, which takes in Uruguay and Argentina as well as Peru, a spokeswoman stressed that Pope Leo had "fantastic stamina" and was "extremely enthusiastic" about the schedule which he had "personally confirmed... with no changes".

"The Vatican's message with this trip is, 'that was a short episode and now we're back on track'," Professor Massimo Faggioli of Villanova University told the BBC, pointing out that the Pope has had a busy return to work since his surgery.

"We have seen him in these last few days making speeches, audiences and so on. He seems in great shape. That was important."

On Friday Pope Leo presided over the annual memorial Mass for cardinals and bishops who have died

The 12-day tour would be demanding for anyone, though. It incorporates multiple long-haul and local flights and covers thousands of miles from the Amazon to the Andes.

The starting point will be Uruguay, where the number of Catholics has fallen away steeply in recent years and which is getting its first Papal visit in four decades.

Leo then heads for Argentina, homeland of Pope Francis, his predecessor.

Father Fernando Laguna said his heart was filled with joy when the Pope's visit to Argentina was announced

"This visit is also a homage to Francis, who didn't make it to Argentina [as pope]," says Father Fernando Laguna, the rector of Santa Maria Addolorata, Argentina's national church in Rome.

"When this visit by Pope Leo was announced, it filled all of our hearts with joy," he told the BBC. "I always thought he would go, despite his illness. I don't think he ever thought of calling it off!"

The priest is installing a big screen inside the church in Rome to allow parishioners to follow all the key moments of the trip from afar. The building is already decked out with posters declaring Pope Leo is welcome in Argentina. "We're waiting for you!", one proclaims.

'Welcome Pope Leo' reads this poster in the Argentinian church in Rome

This leg of the Pope's journey - which includes meetings with heads of state as well as religious events - could be the most politically sensitive. President Javier Milei once called Pope Francis "evil on earth" and a "communist" for promoting a doctrine of social justice to help the poor. In turn, Argentina's bishops have criticised Milei's harsh austerity measures, making sweeping cuts to social spending.

But Milei has already welcomed Pope Leo, writing in a post on X that Argentina awaits "with immense joy". Millions are expected to turn out to greet him.

The highlight of the trip is undoubtedly saved for last, when Pope Leo reaches Peru - described by the Vatican as a return to his roots.

"He knows these streets," is how a spokeswoman put it.

Worshippers at Chiclayo cathedral in Peru were delighted when their former bishop became Pope in 2025

Born in the US, the then Robert Prevost spent almost 20 years in Peru, on and off, and even has Peruvian citizenship. That time is seen as fundamental to shaping his papacy.

His schedule includes a return to the diocese of Chiclayo where he was ordained bishop in 2014 and remained until Francis moved him to the Vatican almost a decade later.

He will also travel to Cusco, former capital of the Inca Empire, to say Mass more than 3,000m above sea level before heading to Pucallpa in the Amazon.

In all three countries, the Vatican says the Pope will have meetings with survivors of clerical sexual abuse - an issue which clouded a 2018 visit to Chile and Peru by his predecessor.

"This is how the papacy reconnects with Latin America after Francis, whose visits to the region were not always seamless," is how Professor Faggioli sees it.

He believes Pope Leo has a strong sense of the unity of the Americas as one continent. "Democracies are in trouble there, so there are so many things he can address," he suggests.

"The big question then is the United States. There's no question he wants to go. So will he travel when [President Donald] Trump is still in office? I'm sure they're thinking about this."

#37. Paraguay’s president quits after millions of people’s savings frozen in bank crisis

Santiago Peña’s rightwing administration had authorised Ueno Bank to drastically expand operations before collapse

Paraguay’s rightwing president, Santiago Peña, resigned on Thursday after a political and financial crisis collapsed the South American country’s banking system in recent days.

At the centre of the crisis is Ueno Bank, which in just a few years under Peña went from an obscure digital bank to one of the country’s largest lenders, claiming to have about 60% of Paraguay’s adult population as customers.

Last Monday, the central bank ordered Ueno into resolution over insolvency, freezing the deposits of 3 million customers, leaving hundreds of thousands of families without access to money for food and basic daily expenses. Bank executives were arrested, and people took to the streets in massive protests.

In power since August 2023 and with a term running until 2028, the 47-year-old economist was already facing impeachment proceedings that had broad support.

“Dear compatriots, today I address you at one of the most difficult moments of my life. With my heart set on Paraguay and with the conviction that our republic must always come before any person or circumstance, I have decided to submit my resignation as president of the republic,” said Peña in a video.

Thousands of Paraguayans gathered outside Congress on Thursday to protest against Peña. Lawmakers voted unanimously on Thursday to launch impeachment proceedings against Peña over the crisis, alleging misconduct and a conflict of interest in dealings with the bank to which he is tied.

Ueno Bank began as a finance company in the 1970s and, in 2021, was bought by Grupo Vázquez, one of Paraguay’s largest business and industrial conglomerates. At the end of 2023, it received authorisation from the central bank, already under Peña’s administration, to operate as a digital bank.

The bank rapidly expanded its operations, attracting customers with generous cash-back offers on groceries and sponsoring Paraguay’s national football team. On Wednesday, the players released a letter “demanding” a “forceful response” from the government and the justice system.

The bank came to manage between $800m and $850m in deposits from state and pension entities, including retirement funds, prompting criticism over conflicts of interest because of Peña’s previous shareholding ties to Ueno Holding.

Peña said that, as an economist, he had merely made an “investment”, but that he had already disposed of all his shares. When the local press pointed to possible improper favouritism in directing state and pension funds to the bank, he called it a “false media campaign” aimed at weakening his government.

On Wednesday, opposition lawmakers and even members of Peña’s Colorado party filed an impeachment motion holding the president responsible for the collapse. He had been a shareholder in the group that ran the bank until early last year and, in recent months, had reportedly been ignoring warnings from the central bank and the media about its operations.

Political pressure intensified on Thursday morning when the ex-president Horacio Cartes, his former ally, posted a message endorsing the impeachment.

“We will not stand on the side of the theft of public, private and pensioners’ funds. We must think of the people and of the values we uphold in the Colorado party. We support the impeachment of the president of the republic, Santiago Peña, in order to restore the institutional order of the republic,” he wrote.

Other lawmakers followed suit and began debating impeachment when Peña released a video announcing his resignation.

“I do so thinking of the stability of our country, social peace and the need to preserve our democratic institutions,” said the now former president.

“I also want to apologise to the people if at any point I failed them … But today my priority is only one thing: the peace of mind of Paraguayans who trusted their savings to our financial system … My greatest wish is that the return of those funds is guaranteed,” he added.

Since the scandal broke, Peña has insisted in interviews and statements that he committed no wrongdoing.

Opposition senator Rafael Filizzola called the case “the biggest fraud carried out from political power in the democratic era … This is a bank created from political power, to use public money for private businesses linked to political power, with the president of the republic at the head of it,” he told 1020 AM radio.

Peña, a former finance minister who promised to rebuild a “great Paraguay”, was a key ally of the US president, Donald Trump, in the region – one of the few Latin American presidents to attend Trump’s second inauguration. US officials, including the secretary of state, Marco Rubio, repeatedly praised him as a “strategic partner”, and Peña recently flew to Washington to discuss critical minerals and economic cooperation. Neither Rubio nor Trump has yet commented on Peña’s resignation.

It is still unclear who will replace him or when, because his resignation must, in theory, be accepted by Congress – in which case the vice-president, Pedro Alliana, would take over. Alliana is also suspected of involvement in the Ueno Bank scandal, which he has denied.

About an hour after Peña’s announcement, the lower house unanimously approved the impeachment motion, which now goes to the senate. If approved, even after his resignation, he would lose his senate seat and, with it, parliamentary immunity, leaving him to face any criminal proceedings in the ordinary courts.

#32. Low pressure in US midwest brings unseasonable heat to New York and Boston

Further south this week, severe thunderstorms affected midterms voting in Florida, Oklahoma and Colorado

Despite the rapid descent into autumn in the northern hemisphere, parts of eastern North America have experienced an exceptionally late taste of summer in recent days. Temperatures approached 23C (73F) in New York and Boston, about 7-10C above what is expected in early November. The unseasonable warmth spilled into parts of eastern Canada, with temperatures exceeding 20C, about 10-15C above normal for the time of year. The balmy conditions were caused by an area of low pressure in the US midwest, dragging up exceptionally warm air from the Gulf of Mexico along its eastern flank. This area of low pressure will gradually drift eastwards, allowing temperatures to fall closer to the average.

Further south, severe thunderstorms have resulted in numerous flood warnings and evacuation warnings being issued this week across many states, with disruptive weather affecting polling stations in Florida, Oklahoma and Colorado. The powerful thunderstorms even resulted in a tornado touching down in Keyes, Oklahoma, forcing the relocation of a polling station. The tornado caused extensive damage, although no causalities have been reported. Officials across many states have requested federal funding assistance after the extreme weather resulted in widespread damage to homes and infrastructure.

Across the Atlantic, a number of authorities in the Mediterranean regions of southern France, Corsica and Sardinia issued severe flood warnings this week, as an area of low pressure caused thunderstorms and torrential rain. As much as 50-100mm (2-4in) of rain is expected over the coming days with more localised totals in excess of 100mm possible in the southern French regions of Provence-Alpes-Côte d’Azur and Occitanie, as well as Corsica. Where torrential rain is most prolonged, up to 70-80mm of rain could fall within a 24-hour period. More rain is expected next week as low pressure continues to dominate the region.

#19. Sinkholes, scarred land and scapegoats: the scourge of illegal gold mining in Johannesburg

About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.
