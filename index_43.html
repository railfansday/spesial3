#3072. Fake job interview scams like the one that hit Binance are getting more sophisticated. Here are the new signs to watch for

Job search scams can cost applicants hundreds or thousands of dollars. But one fake job interview recently impacted over $6 billion in assets.

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, announced on Tuesday it recently experienced a security breach impacting $6.34 billion in digital assets. The hack was enabled in part by a job seeker's nightmare: bogus job interviews.

While the Binance hackers had their sights set on a much bigger target than an individual job seeker, job-related scams are on the rise. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) tallied employment-related losses amounting to roughly $363 million in 2025, up sharply from $264 million the year prior, and $70 million in 2023. The actual figure is likely higher, since many victims of such crimes do not report them.

Those orchestrating fake job scams have many potential targets today "because the job search is taking so long for so many right now," says Stefanie Fackrell, a former recruiter at Google and Nvidia. While October's jobs report showed promise as the U.S. economy added 165,000 jobs, far more than expected, many applicants have been struggling through a low-hire, low-fire market for months.

Artificial intelligence can also make scams more widespread and harder to detect.

"Cyber scams in general are becoming much more sophisticated just because they're evolving at the same speed as AI and public access to AI," says Garber. "AI is going to make things seem more and more real."

In 2025, victims reported losses of almost $13 million to AI-involved employment scams, according to the IC3.

Binance's announcement included an abbreviated play-by-play of how its hack went down. Fackrell and Garber broke down some of the warning signs in how the events unfolded, including what job seekers can learn from the incident.

What happened: A software engineer, who was not named, was contacted through "a legitimate job-hunting platform" by someone posing as a project director, Binance said in its announcement. That person arranged for the engineer to have an initial interview with a purported HR representative and a second-round interview with the supposed CEO of a fake company.

Experts' red flags: A headshot that's clearly AI-generated or otherwise looks uncanny, as well as sparse profile information or activity, and few connections should give you pause, Fackrell says. On LinkedIn, for example, you can click the three dots on a person's profile to open an "About this member" pop-up, where you can see when they joined LinkedIn and if they've verified their identity, workplace and school.

Though some small startups may arrange for an interview with a C-suite executive, it's generally unusual, particularly for a second-round interview, unless you'd be reporting to that person, she adds.

What happened: In the first round, the fake HR representative had a live video interview with the software engineer, even sharing with him a social media account with personal photos and videos to build trust, Binance said. The tactic was "more advanced and deceptive than previously seen social engineering approaches," Binance added in its announcement.

Experts' red flags: A recruiter might bring up commonalities with a candidate like the same alma mater or ex-employer to "bring that human element into the conversation," Fackrell says. But sharing personal social media in a first-round interview is strange, she adds.

In video interviews in general, some red flags might include the person moving unusually slowly or not responding to one of your questions, or their visual skipping a beat, Garber says.

What happened: The second-round interview with the purported CEO of the fake company was conducted on "a popular social chat app," Binance said, and the software engineer was instructed to download "an 'assignment' containing a malicious code repository onto his work computer."

Experts' red flags: Interviews commonly happen over Microsoft Teams, Zoom or Google Meet, not a social chat app. "I don't know anybody that's FaceTiming or WhatsApping any of their candidates," Fackrell says.

While take-home assignments, particularly for technical roles, may use third-party systems, don't download software if you're not familiar with it, Fackrell advises. These assignments should be shared via email through a legitimate system, not over the phone. Job seekers should take interviews on a personal device, not a work computer.

Job sites like LinkedIn and Indeed, and entities like the Federal Trade Commission and Better Business Bureau, also have more information about common scams.

You should not have to provide sensitive information or payment in order to get a job, according to the FTC. Legitimate recruiters will reach out from their corporate email addresses, not a personal address. Other red flags include pressure to move quickly through the hiring process or seeing multiple typos in communications.

"Do your research before clicking, responding or engaging with anybody," Fackrell says.

Take a minute to think about how you were contacted and what you're being asked to do to proceed, Garber suggests, and cross-check job postings against the company's official careers page.

"If seems too good to be true, it definitely is," says Garber.

There is an array of initiatives on ballots around the nation that could change tax laws and add to bond supply. Taxes matter to muni investors since the income received is free of federal tax, as well as state tax if the holder lives in the state where the bond is issued.

Depending on the location, ballot measures are up for approval that would either provide tax breaks, or raise taxes on the wealthy.

"It's a complete bifurcation between red and blue states," said Dan Close, head of municipals at Nuveen. "Blue states are trying to put in place graduated income tax structures, trying to capture more revenue from high-income earners. Red states are promoting affordability by trying to cut property taxes."

Red states are those that predominantly vote Republican, while blue states swing toward Democrats.

For instance, California has a proposition known as the "billionaire tax," which would impose a one-time levy equal to 5% of the wealth of certain taxpayers with at least $1 billion in assets. The state also has a measure on the ballot that would make a temporary tax hike permanent on individuals earning over $371,000, adjusted annually for inflation. The tax is currently set to expire in 2031.

"The impact to the muni bond market will be to make the exemption more valuable," Close said of the possible passage of tax increases. Investors will likely seek shelter in the muni market, which would send bond prices higher, he noted.

It is a different story in Florida, where voters are set to decide whether to increase the Sunshine State's homestead exemption for non-school district property taxes. Qualified homeowners currently have a $25,000 property tax exemption, as well as another for $25,000 that doesn't apply to school property taxes. The new proposal raises the latter exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 by 2028. New residents have a five-year waiting period before receiving the benefit.

If passed, some local governments will have to find other revenue sources or increase the overall tax rate to replace what they've lost, said Tom Kozlik, head of public policy and municipal strategy at HilltopSecurities.

"It's not necessarily going to result in downgrades for sure, but there would have to be some balancing with regard to overall revenues," he said.

Not all municipalities will be hurt equally, which means credit research is important, said Close.

"Like everything in municipal finance, it is really on a case-by-case basis that you could have some credit deterioration from [a] rollback of property taxes," he said.

Other voters on Tuesday will be asked to approve more state or local government debt. That could weigh on a market that has already seen heightened supply.

Some $473.8 billion has already been issued year to date, through September, according to the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association. That's a 7.8% increase over the same period a year ago. In 2025, total muni issuance reached $587.3 billion, 14.3% higher than 2024, Sifma said.

Cooper Howard, director of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, believes supply is the biggest issue coming out of the election.

"That amount of issuance has been one of the reasons why you have to see that strong amount of demand continue to keep pace with it for total returns to keep up," Howard said.

Fortunately, new supply doesn't necessarily come quickly into the market. "There's a window over a couple years that they can issue," he noted.

The ultimate makeup of the next Congress could also have consequences for muni investors as lawmakers sort out their priorities. Most notable is what lawmakers will do to tackle the country's affordability problem, Kozlik at Hilltop said.

"There could be some tax policy initiatives and even affordability-related initiatives that manifest themselves in the first quarter of next year," he said. "That could impact housing. It could impact higher education. It could impact public power financing."

Investors considering moves before the election may want to look at those states with tax increases on the ballot, said Close at Nuveen.

For instance, Nuveen recently began offering a separately managed account dedicated to Washington after getting an enormous number of inquiries from financial advisors. The state recently enacted a 9.9% tax on individuals with taxable annual income above $1 million, set to go into effect in 2028. But the issue is now being put to voters, who can decide whether to repeal the new law.

"At the margin, if you do think that some of these measures are going to pass, knowing that bonds usually move up with an increase in taxes, there might be some opportunities to buy ahead of the election for blue states," Close said.

Howard believes munis are attractive right now, particularly for those who are in a tax bracket of 24% or above. Fundamentals are good and the bonds largely have good credit quality, he said. "About 70% of the broad muni index is either triple-A or double-A rated."

Kozlik is also bullish, calling yields "really attractive." He suggests investors concentrate on AA-rated or higher bonds.

"The muni space is part of the portfolio that most investors want," he said. "It helps them sleep well at night."

The road to bull-market glory does not run through Walmart and Berkshire Hathaway and Eli Lilly.

The Nasdaq-100 and the S&P 500 Low Volatility ETF (SPLV) are rushing toward one another in a satisfying picture of symmetry that nonetheless reflects an almost comical level of tactical rotation executed by an investment community accustomed to playing one factor against another.

While this to-and-fro has so far protected the S&P 500 as a whole from an appreciable setback for months, it has left it stymied around levels that date back to May — before we got two earnings-reporting seasons showing 30% annual profit growth.

A week ago here, I suggested the AI theme was nearing a make-or-break moment to stage a recovery or risk surrendering its leadership status and destabilizing the broader market. Thus far, this trade has failed to respond to moderate oversold conditions or a run of cosmetically strong hyperscaler quarterly results, further loosening its grip on the benefit of the doubt.

In stressed markets undergoing soul-searching about the long-term bullish thesis, prices often need to travel further to find buyers with conviction while the market action must disappoint dip-buyers to extinguish complacency. Hard to know if we're there, but presumably we're now closer.

The Nasdaq-100 closed Monday right at its 200-day moving average, off almost 11% from its record high. After an enervating failed Monday-morning bounce attempt, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index finished down another 3.8% and settled under the late-July low that was set as the Situational Awareness hedge fund was finished liquidating.

"Markets did not get the memo that bearish seasonal pressure typically subsides in mid-October and instead ended the month with some of the most acute selling pressure that has been realized during the late-summer/early-fall period," notes JPMorgan technical strategist Jason Hunter. He's advising clients to remain on the defensive "until we see clear evidence of a bottoming process on the charts or until there are clear signs of capitulation."

This is a time-tested playbook, though many a sharp market pullback has ended without what would qualify as unqualified capitulation.

The market action is winning some plaudits for the way non-tech stocks have held up better, many groups benefiting from the "quality" rotation while abandoned consumer cyclicals get a reprieve from easing oil prices and the slide in the 10-year Treasury yield back below 5%.

This gives active managers plenty to do in playing against a highly concentrated and tech-dependent S&P 500. But I'll continue to argue that the bull market built on the AI story will almost surely need the AI story to get back on track to keep advancing. A prolonged period when the equal-weight S&P 500 outperforms the market-cap-weighted version is otherwise known – usually – as a bear market.

The AI-related stocks' weighting in the S&P 500 is off the highs but remains near 45%, according to Morgan Stanley.

As the chart shows, AI has wobbled relative to the rest of the market during this uptrend before reasserting itself.  But there's a trickier element to the current gut check: In prior phases of the buildout, the key limiting bottleneck gave investors something to buy in order to exploit it.

The initial scarcity of GPUs meant to own Nvidia above all else from 2023 through 2024. When cloud capacity, power and cooling were found to be the crucial scarce resources, hyperscaler stocks worked and the AI-adjacent industrials were drastically revalued. Then memory emerged as the chokepoint and Micron and Sandisk went stratospheric.

Now, as Nvidia's Jensen Huang has said, capital is the constraint and that not only leaves no asset to buy, but it raises the cost of capital broadly as the mega-caps go free-cash-flow-negative.

Overlapping feedback loops and scrambled correlations across the markets and the real economy have further sapped investor conviction. Treasury yields no longer move with oil but largely with AI-capex sentiment, with both in retreat lately. AI-stock setbacks also mute inflation momentum and the equity wealth effect, meaning fewer Federal Reserve rate hikes are anticipated now versus a month ago.

This all probably sounds like an elaborate trap, and it has become one for the previously crowded trades. Could this reflect the end game for the AI-exuberance market chapter, which as I keep pointing out peaked around the SpaceX IPO in June? Impossible to say for sure, but if it were, I'm not sure things would look much different to this point.

But I don't want to dismiss the possibility that a lot of the known challenges are largely reflected in prices.

I argued in August that we should assume the valuation peak for this cycle was in. We've now retrenched to a forward P/E of 18.5 on the S&P 500 from 23 a year ago and the Nasdaq-100 at 20x is at the slimmest premium to the S&P 500 valuation in nine years. I'm on board with the idea that the market is telling us companies are now "over-earning," but if nothing else there is less valuation risk now than in recent months.

Sentiment toward AI stocks is in the dumps. Funds' positioning in tech has retreated below neutral. Important trading lows this year have happened near the turn of the month on March 30 and July 29, and the Nasdaq-100's relative-strength reading is exactly as oversold as it was at those turns.

So, stay alert for possible relief bounces that could evolve into something more consequential if indeed the midterm elections pass in a way that allows traders to unclench a bit into the year's home stretch.

It's a troubling sign in an otherwise steady labor market. Workers stuck on the sidelines are a casualty of what's become a "low-hire, low-fire" job market, in which companies are hesitant to lay workers off, but aren't adding them either.

Research shows that Americans who experience long-term unemployment face depleted savings, lower earnings potential over time and declining mental health.

What's more, workers who are unemployed for extended periods risk falling into a self-perpetuating cycle, researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond wrote earlier this year.

"Time spent unemployed may itself reduce the probability of finding a job, perhaps because skills depreciate, networks thin or employers interpret long unemployment durations as a negative signal," the researchers warned.

Workers stop being able to claim most, if not all, government unemployment benefits after 26 weeks in a year. Given that, a rising share of long-term unemployed people can explain in part why initial and continuing jobless claims have remained low.
