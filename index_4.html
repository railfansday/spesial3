A few years ago, animating a ten-second scene meant storyboards, rigging and a lot of patience. Today you can type a sentence, upload a drawing or point at a reference clip and get moving footage back in a minute or two. The results are uneven, but they are good enough that designers, marketers and indie developers now use them for storyboards, product teasers, game trailers and social content.

The catch is that these tools are not interchangeable. Some excel at cinematic camera moves, some at editing footage you already have, and some at a particular visual style. This guide compares five AI animation generators, what each one is best at and where it falls short. Plans, credits and model names change quickly, so check each vendor's pricing page before you commit.

Runway is a cloud workspace for generating and editing video, images and audio in one place. Its current video model is Gen-4.5, which Runway promotes for motion quality, prompt adherence and visual fidelity. The platform also offers agent and workflow tools, mobile apps for iPhone and Android, and an API for developers.

Best for: Creators who want to generate a shot and then keep refining it in the same environment, and teams that need an API.

Limitations: There is a lot to learn, and credit costs add up when you iterate. Runway offers a free trial, but check the pricing page for current limits.

Kling AI is an AI video and image generator with a creative studio for image generation, video generation and motion control. It also includes an agent for brainstorming and scripting, and a node-based canvas for working through scripts, storyboards, images and video with multiple rounds of editing. Kling lists advertising, film, music videos and e-commerce among its use cases, and offers an API platform and automation options for producing video at scale.

Best for: Marketers and studios that want to move from script to storyboard to footage in a single workspace.

Limitations: The feature set is broad, so newcomers may need time to find the right workflow. Free credits were not described on the pages I checked, so confirm what is available before relying on it.

Luma focuses on turning still images into video with camera motion. Its workspace can create transitions between frames, extend clips, transform footage into new styles, reframe aspect ratios and add voice, music, captions and lip sync. It runs Luma's own Ray models and also gives access to several third-party models, so you can compare results without switching platforms.

Best for: Designers and illustrators who already have key art, product shots or concept images and want to bring them to life.

Limitations: Paid plans start at around $30 a month at the time of writing, and generation credits can run out quickly if you test many variations.

Pika is a creative platform covering video, images and audio. Its Video Studio can produce anything from a short clip to a multi-shot video of around 30 seconds, and its toolkit includes Extend Video, Motion Transfer, Swap Anything and Color Grade. Character Studio lets you create reusable characters, and separate studios handle music, speech and sound effects. Pika also exposes several video models, including its own Pika 2.5 alongside third-party options.

Best for: Social content creators who want effects, sound and edits in one tool.

Limitations: With so many tools and models, results vary from one to the next. Check the current pricing page for credit details.

Pollo AI is a generator that leans into one look: anime. Its animation generator creates anime-style videos from a text prompt and lets you adjust the video content, output style and size. The page offers eleven Japanese animator-inspired style options, such as Hayao Miyazaki and Makoto Shinkai, and the platform also provides a separate tool that converts existing video into anime.

Best for: Creators who want a consistent anime look for shorts, fan projects, music visuals or social posts without learning a full editing suite.

Limitations: The generator page describes text prompts as its input and does not state clip length, resolution or credit details, so test a few generations and review the terms before planning a project around it. As with any style-focused tool, the look is distinctive, which is a strength for anime projects and a limit for anything else.

Legacy applications often support critical business operations, but keeping them running can become increasingly difficult. Outdated infrastructure, growing technical debt, security vulnerabilities, and limited integration capabilities can make these systems expensive to maintain and harder to adapt to changing business needs.

Modernizing them involves more than upgrading technology. You need to understand what the existing application does, identify which components need improvement, and manage the transition without disrupting essential operations.

This 10-point legacy application modernization checklist walks you through the process, from assessing your existing systems and selecting a modernization strategy to migrating data, testing applications, and measuring results.

Legacy application modernization is the process of updating existing software applications, their architecture, infrastructure, or underlying technologies to meet current business and technical requirements.

Depending on the application's condition, modernization may involve moving it to a new platform, improving its code, redesigning its architecture, or replacing it with a new solution.

The goal is to improve performance, security, scalability, maintainability, and the application's ability to support future business requirements.

Modernization isn't necessarily the same as cloud migration. Moving an application from on-premises infrastructure to the cloud changes where it runs, but it may leave its underlying architecture and technical limitations unchanged.

An organization can modernize an application without moving it to the cloud or combine both approaches as part of a larger transformation.

Use this checklist to track the ten essential steps involved in modernizing legacy applications.

1. Define modernization goals: Identify business problems and establish measurable success criteria.

2. Assess existing applications: Review infrastructure, technical debt, code quality, and performance limitations.

3. Map application dependencies: Document critical workflows, integrations, and business rules.

4. Choose a modernization strategy: Select an approach based on business value, cost, complexity, and risk.

5. Develop a roadmap and budget: Prioritize activities, estimate costs, assign responsibilities, and establish milestones.

6. Plan architecture and security: Design the target environment and address security, compliance, and availability requirements.

7. Prepare data migration: Plan data transfer, integration changes, and validation procedures.

8. Modernize incrementally: Validate the approach with a pilot and implement changes in manageable phases.

9. Test and deploy: Verify functionality, performance, security, and data integrity before production rollout.

10. Monitor and optimize: Measure results, support users, and continuously improve the modernized application.

Let's examine what each step involves and how to determine whether you're ready to proceed.

Before selecting technologies or planning development work, identify what the modernization initiative needs to achieve.

Clear business objectives help prioritize investments, guide technical decisions, and establish whether the project delivers meaningful improvements.

Start by examining the problems affecting your application's performance, reliability, or business value.

For example, a legacy order management system might still process transactions reliably but require extensive manual work whenever the business adds a new sales channel.

In this situation, improving integration capabilities may be more valuable than replacing the entire application.

An application's age alone doesn't justify a major technology investment. Whether legacy modernization makes sense for your business depends on the problems the existing system creates, the cost of maintaining it, and whether smaller improvements could resolve those issues.

Once you've identified the problems, translate them into measurable objectives.

If slow performance is the primary concern, establish a target for response times or transaction processing speed. If maintenance expenses are rising, determine what reduction in operating costs would justify the investment.

These baseline measurements will help determine whether the changes deliver the expected results.

Completion check: You have a documented business case identifying the problems to address, expected outcomes, measurable KPIs, and stakeholders responsible for approving success.

A legacy application assessment provides a clear picture of your current technology environment.

Without this assessment, teams may underestimate project complexity, overlook critical components, or choose a modernization approach that doesn't address the application's actual limitations.

Begin by creating an inventory of your applications and their supporting technologies.

Document each application's purpose, business owner, primary users, operating environment, programming languages, frameworks, databases, and infrastructure requirements.

Also identify software versions, third-party dependencies, licensing restrictions, and components approaching or past their end-of-support dates.

If your organization operates several legacy applications, use a consistent assessment framework. This makes it easier to compare their business importance, technical condition, and modernization urgency.

Review the application's codebase to identify issues that make maintenance and development difficult.

Look for tightly coupled components, duplicated logic, outdated libraries, insufficient automated tests, and undocumented configurations.

Pay particular attention to areas where small modifications regularly cause unexpected problems elsewhere in the system.

For example, if payment processing, inventory management, and customer records share tightly connected code, modifying one function could require extensive testing across the entire application.

Your infrastructure assessment should also cover hardware limitations, database compatibility, backup capabilities, operating systems, and unsupported dependencies.

Analyze how the application performs during normal operations and periods of increased demand.

Review response times, database queries, resource utilization, transaction volumes, and recurring failures.

Existing monitoring data and incident records can reveal problems that aren't immediately visible during routine use.

Determine whether performance limitations originate from inefficient application code, infrastructure capacity, database design, or external dependencies.

This distinction matters because adding computing resources won't necessarily resolve problems caused by poor application logic.

Completion check: You have an application inventory and technical assessment report documenting major components, technical debt, operational risks, and performance limitations.

Legacy applications rarely operate independently. They exchange data with other systems, support interconnected business processes, and often contain business rules that aren't fully documented.

Understanding these dependencies is essential because changing one component can affect several other applications or workflows.

Document the processes that rely on the application, including the steps users follow, the data they need, and the expected outcomes.

Work with business users, application owners, operations teams, and developers familiar with the system.

Identify processes that cannot tolerate significant interruption, such as payment processing, order fulfillment, customer authentication, or financial reporting.

Don't overlook activities that happen less frequently. Month-end financial reports, annual calculations, and scheduled data exchanges may depend on functionality that isn't exercised during everyday operations.

Map how the application communicates with databases, APIs, third-party services, and other internal systems.

Include scheduled jobs, file transfers, message queues, authentication services, and reporting tools.

Some legacy applications contain years of accumulated business logic.

Rules for pricing, approvals, calculations, or customer eligibility may exist only in source code, database procedures, or undocumented workflows.

Identify these rules before modifying the underlying implementation.

Where possible, create automated tests that capture existing expected behavior. These tests can help verify that the modernized application preserves required functionality.

Completion check: Critical workflows, system dependencies, integrations, and business rules are documented and reviewed by the relevant business and technical owners.

The appropriate modernization strategy depends on the application's technical condition, business importance, budget, and future requirements.

Not every application needs to be rebuilt. Some systems require targeted improvements, while others may be better replaced or retired.

Different applications within the same organization may require different approaches.

Evaluate each option against your technical constraints and business requirements.

Consider implementation effort, expected benefits, ongoing maintenance costs, required expertise, and potential disruption.

For example, replatforming may be suitable for an application that operates reliably but depends on aging infrastructure. Rearchitecting may be necessary when tightly coupled components prevent independent updates or scaling.

A complete rebuild requires particular caution. Recreating undocumented functionality can be expensive, and important business rules may be lost if the existing system hasn't been thoroughly assessed.

The right strategy should solve the identified problems without introducing unnecessary cost or complexity.

Completion check: Each application has a selected modernization strategy, documented rationale, estimated effort, and an approved decision on whether to modernize, retain, replace, or retire it.

Once you've selected a modernization approach, convert it into an actionable plan.

Your roadmap should establish what will change, in what order, who will be responsible, and how progress will be evaluated.

Prioritize modernization work according to business value, technical complexity, urgency, and dependencies.

Applications with unsupported components or significant security risks may require early attention. However, starting with the most complex application isn't always the best choice.

A smaller, lower-risk application can provide an opportunity to validate tools, workflows, and team capabilities before modernizing more critical systems.

Dependencies also affect the sequence. For example, a shared authentication service may need to be updated before applications that rely on it can be migrated.

Prepare a budget covering the full modernization process rather than development alone.

Compare upfront implementation expenses with expected operating costs over time.

A modernization approach with a lower initial cost may still require significant ongoing maintenance.

Allow contingency for unexpected issues, particularly when legacy documentation is incomplete or system dependencies remain uncertain.

Divide the project into phases with defined deliverables and approval requirements.

Assign responsible owners for architecture, development, security, data migration, testing, and business acceptance.

Establish how project risks will be reported and who can approve changes to scope, budget, or deployment schedules.

Keep the roadmap adaptable. Findings from pilot implementations or migration rehearsals may require changes to the original plan.

Completion check: Your modernization roadmap includes prioritized activities, estimated costs, responsible owners, dependencies, milestones, and approval checkpoints.

The target architecture determines how the modernized application will operate, scale, integrate with other systems, and remain maintainable.

Security and compliance requirements should be incorporated into the design from the beginning.

Choose an architecture that fits the application's actual requirements.

Possible approaches include a modular monolith, service-oriented architecture, microservices, or event-driven components.

Microservices aren't automatically the best option. Although they allow independent deployment and scaling of components, they also introduce complexity in communication, monitoring, deployment, and data management.

For smaller applications or tightly integrated business processes, a well-structured modular architecture may be easier to maintain.

Document architectural decisions covering application components, data storage, interfaces, infrastructure requirements, and operational responsibilities.

Identify security weaknesses that should be resolved during modernization.

Review authentication, authorization, encryption, secrets management, logging, and access to sensitive information.

Evaluate third-party dependencies and establish processes for vulnerability scanning, patching, and security testing.

Regulatory and contractual requirements should also influence the target design.

Security controls should be applied throughout development and deployment rather than added shortly before release.

Define how the application will remain available and recover from failures.

Establish recovery time objectives (RTOs) and recovery point objectives (RPOs) based on business requirements.

Plan backup procedures, restoration testing, appropriate redundancy, and monitoring for critical components.

Validate that the target environment can support the required availability and recovery objectives.

Completion check: The target architecture has documented design decisions, security controls, compliance requirements, availability objectives, and recovery procedures.

Data migration is one of the most sensitive stages of legacy application modernization.

Legacy databases may contain inconsistent records, outdated formats, duplicated information, or relationships that newer systems handle differently.

A migration strategy needs to explain how data will be transferred, validated, and made available without compromising business operations.

Begin by reviewing the quality and structure of existing data.

Identify missing values, duplicate records, inconsistent formats, obsolete fields, and relationships that could create migration problems.

Document how existing data structures correspond to the target system.

For example, a legacy customer database might store contact details in a single field, while the new application requires separate fields for email addresses and phone numbers.

The migration process needs defined transformation rules to handle these differences.

Determine which records must be migrated, archived, retained for compliance, or excluded.

Choose a migration approach based on data volume, application availability requirements, and acceptable downtime.

Depending on the system, this may involve a scheduled migration during a maintenance window, staged transfers, or synchronization between old and new environments.

Identify integrations that will need modification.

Where applications exchange information through APIs, files, or messaging systems, verify that the modernized implementation supports the required data formats and business behavior.

If the legacy and modernized applications will operate simultaneously, define how data updates will be synchronized between them.

Test the migration process using representative datasets before transferring production data.

Validate record counts, field values, relationships, and critical business calculations.

For sensitive or business-critical information, use appropriate reconciliation methods, including integrity checks where applicable.

Document how inconsistencies will be identified and resolved.

Also establish what happens if migration fails or the application needs to return to its previous environment.

Completion check: Data mapping rules are approved, migration rehearsals have been completed, and reconciliation procedures can verify data accuracy.

Modernizing an entire application in a single major release can increase project risk, particularly when the system supports critical business operations.

An incremental approach allows teams to validate decisions, identify problems earlier, and limit the impact of failures.

Select a manageable component or workload to validate your modernization approach.

The pilot should test an important assumption, such as whether the target platform supports the application, whether a legacy integration can be replaced, or whether an architectural change improves performance.

Define success criteria before implementation.

A useful pilot should provide evidence that the approach is technically feasible and that the team can manage its operational requirements.

Use the findings to improve estimates, identify missing skills, and refine the broader implementation plan.

Divide the application into manageable migration or development units where its architecture allows.

One approach is the Strangler Fig pattern, which gradually replaces legacy functionality while the remaining parts of the original application continue operating.

For example, an organization might modernize customer notifications first, followed by reporting and then core transaction processing.

This can reduce the risks associated with replacing an entire system at once.

However, incremental modernization also requires careful coordination. Temporary integrations, duplicated components, and parallel systems can introduce complexity.

Each phase should have defined boundaries, validation requirements, and completion criteria.

Establish repeatable processes for developing, testing, and deploying application changes.

Continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD) pipelines can automate builds, execute tests, and promote approved changes through environments.

Use version control, consistent configuration management, and infrastructure as code where appropriate.

Include security and quality checks in the delivery process to reduce the risk of introducing defects during frequent releases.

Completion check: The pilot has validated the selected approach, implementation is divided into manageable phases, and repeatable build, test, and deployment processes are established.

Modernization isn't complete when the new application runs successfully in a development environment.

It must perform the required business functions, communicate correctly with connected systems, and meet operational expectations before production rollout.

Develop a testing plan based on the application's critical workflows and identified risks.

Automate repeatable tests where practical, especially for critical business processes.

Compare results against the performance baselines and acceptance criteria established earlier.

Business users should verify that the modernized application supports their actual workflows.

User acceptance testing (UAT) should include routine operations, exception handling, reporting requirements, and tasks that depend on specialized business knowledge.

Use representative migrated data during testing.

Confirm that critical records, calculations, and reports remain accurate after the transition.

Resolve issues affecting essential operations before approving production deployment.

Prepare a documented deployment plan outlining the sequence of activities, responsible teams, communication channels, and expected service interruptions.

Choose a deployment method appropriate for the application, such as a phased rollout, canary release, or scheduled cutover.

Establish measurable conditions that would stop deployment or trigger a rollback.

Rollback planning must account for data changes made after cutover. Restoring an earlier application version doesn't automatically restore database consistency, especially if new transactions have already been processed.

Rehearse deployment and recovery procedures in a suitable non-production environment.

Completion check: Required tests have passed, business owners have approved acceptance criteria, and deployment, cutover, and recovery procedures have been validated.

The initial deployment marks the beginning of ongoing operation rather than the end of modernization.

The application needs continued monitoring and maintenance to ensure that expected improvements are sustained.

Compare post-modernization results against the baseline established at the beginning of the project.

Track metrics linked to your business objectives, such as application availability, maintenance costs, response times, deployment frequency, security vulnerabilities, and user satisfaction.

Use monitoring tools, logs, traces, and alerts to identify problems that may not be visible through basic availability checks.

Review performance after deployment and at agreed intervals to determine whether the modernization initiative is meeting its objectives.

Technical improvements may introduce changes to established workflows.

Provide relevant training, updated documentation, and support resources for users and operational teams.

Ensure that administrators and developers understand the new architecture, deployment processes, monitoring tools, and incident response procedures.

Collect user feedback to identify usability issues or missing functionality.

Use production data and operational feedback to guide further improvements.

Optimization may involve updating dependencies, improving database queries, adjusting infrastructure capacity, strengthening security controls, or simplifying application components.

Once replacement functionality is stable and approved, begin decommissioning components that are no longer needed.

Before retirement, confirm that dependent applications have been migrated, necessary records are retained, and archival or compliance obligations have been met.

Removing unused infrastructure and software can reduce maintenance costs and unnecessary security exposure.

Completion check: Monitoring and support responsibilities are established, modernization KPIs are reviewed, and unused legacy components have an approved retirement plan.

A support email lands: "Order NW-47790, the tent poles snapped on the first night, I paid $389 and I want every cent back today or I'm disputing the charge."

To act on that, your code needs answers to a handful of small questions. Is it a refund request? How urgent is it? Should a person handle it? What's the order number? How much money is involved? It's the same problem whether you're sorting a support inbox or building a custom chatbot that has to work out what each customer is asking for.

The usual move is to send the whole email to a chat model with a prompt that ends "respond in JSON", then validate whatever comes back. It works, but you're paying a model built to write essays to fill in five fields, and you're parsing its guess at a schema.

System One models are built for exactly this kind of job. In this tutorial we'll build a small support inbox pipeline with two of them: Jev from TypeSafe makes the decisions, and Clairic from Cloudflare reads the values. Plain TypeScript then turns their answers into an action. No prompt engineering for output formats, and no JSON repair.

The name comes from Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow. System 1 is the fast, intuitive kind of thinking you use to read a face or a sign. System 2 is the slow, deliberate kind you use for long division.

A System One model is an AI model that understands natural language but doesn't generate text. You give it some input (the state) and a set of typed questions, and it gives back typed answers with probabilities. There's no free text to parse, because the model can only answer in the shape you asked for.

That's different from asking a chat model for structured output. SitePoint's guide to LLM structured output with JSON Schema shows how to constrain a chat model and validate its JSON with Zod. With a System One model, the type is the interface: a yes/no question comes back as a probability, a pick-one question comes back as one of your options, and a date comes back as a date.

#10647. How Product Teams Can Use AI User Research to Test Assumptions Before Sprint Planning

Sprint planning is on Thursday, and the top of the backlog rests on three things nobody has checked. Users want the feature, they will find it, and they will understand what it does when they do. Each belief is plausible, and each has already been written into a ticket.

AI user research uses language models to simulate or analyze user conversations, so a team can put a question to a modeled audience in minutes instead of recruiting people for weeks. Used well, it gives a product team a cheap first check on backlog assumptions. Used badly, it produces confident-sounding agreement. This guide covers a two-day routine that gets the benefit and avoids the trap.

An assumption is worth testing when the plan depends on it and a wrong answer would change the ticket. "Users like clean interfaces" fails that test. "Analysts re-apply the same filters every session" passes, because if it is false the saved-filters feature shrinks or disappears.

The timing matters because planning leaves little room for research. The Scrum Guide says Sprint Planning is timeboxed to a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint, and the first question the team answers is why the Sprint is valuable. Checks run before that meeting inform the answer. Checks run during it eat the time meant for deciding.

Write each risky item as a short card that names the belief, the consequence of being wrong, and the decision it feeds. A card forces the team to say what would change its mind, which most tickets never do. Here is an example:

Aim for three to five cards. A team that tries to test the whole backlog will test none of it, and the cards on the riskiest items give the best return.

AI user research comes in two forms. The first analyzes data you already hold: clustering support tickets and summarizing interview transcripts. It works on real evidence, so its main risk is a sloppy summary.

The second form interviews simulated people. AI user research of this kind builds a panel of modeled personas matched to your target profile, asks them your questions, and reports the themes. It needs no recruiting and returns results quickly, which fits a two-day window. Its output comes from a model of users and not from users, and that distinction should appear in every planning document that cites it.

A simulated audience helps most when you want to sharpen a question, and least when you need proof. Nielsen Norman Group's 2024 review of synthetic users found them useful for generating hypotheses and preparing interview guides. It warned against using them for final decisions or concept validation, and for niche populations, because AI tends to please and "every idea is often seen as a good one."

Spend Tuesday collecting answers and Wednesday reading them skeptically. On Tuesday, put each card's question to the modeled audience in neutral wording, and ask what would make them not use the feature. A question phrased as "Would saved filters help you?" invites a yes, while "Walk me through setting up your weekly report" does not.

On Wednesday, compare the answers with what you already know from support tickets and analytics. Mark each assumption as supported, contradicted, or unclear. Treat a unanimous answer with suspicion, because real audiences rarely agree on anything. Also be wary of lists in which the modeled users "seem to care about everything," which is how Nielsen Norman Group describes their weak prioritization.

Whatever stays unclear or high risk goes to real people. Jakob Nielsen's classic analysis found that five users find about 85% of usability problems, assuming comparable users, and he recommended several small rounds over one large one. An AI check is a good way to decide what those five people should be asked.

Each mark maps to a different decision. A contradicted assumption means the ticket is cut or reduced to its smallest useful version. A supported assumption that rests only on simulated evidence is not yet safe, so schedule a real-user check as the first task in the sprint, ahead of the build work that depends on it. An unclear assumption should not receive full scope.

The cost of skipping this step is real. A 2019 analysis of 615 product subscriptions by a product analytics vendor found that about 12% of features generate 80% of daily usage volume, and 80% of features are rarely or never used. That is one vendor's customer base, and the figures cover features that customers chose to track, so treat them as a rough guide. They still suggest that most of a backlog is a bet.

AI user research cannot replace talking to real users. Nielsen Norman Group found that synthetic users gave overly optimistic accounts of their own behavior, such as claiming to finish every course in a study, and that they cannot supply real past experience. It also called presenting synthetic findings as real-user findings "an unethical way to share what you've learned."

Two habits keep a team honest. Label every piece of evidence in the planning document as modeled, observed, or reported, and require that any feature built on modeled evidence has a real-user check scheduled. The review also warned that teams starting with synthetic research may get comfortable and skip the real kind, so put the real check on the calendar before the first AI result arrives.

Can AI user research replace interviews with real users? No. It is a cheap first pass that helps you write better questions and spot weak assumptions. Anything that drives a significant build still needs real people.

How many assumptions should a team test before planning? Three to five. Pick the ones where a wrong answer would cut or reshape a ticket.

How do I stop simulated users from just agreeing with me? Use neutral, open questions, ask what would make them avoid the feature, and treat unanimous answers as a warning sign.

What should I do with results I cannot verify? Mark them as modeled, give them less weight than observed evidence, and schedule a real-user check before committing full scope.

Sources: Scrum Guide, Sprint Planning; Jakob Nielsen, "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users" (March 18, 2000); Nielsen Norman Group, "Synthetic Users: If, When, and How to Use AI-Generated 'Research'" (June 21, 2024); 2019 Feature Adoption Report (615 product subscriptions, vendor-reported).

A generative AI tool can draft a client email, summarise a forty-message thread or refactor a function in seconds. That speed is real, and it explains how fast these tools have become part of the ordinary working day, used to summarise threads, draft replies and reformat data in a single paste. The same ease is the problem. It moves sensitive material into systems that were never scoped to hold it, quietly and at scale.

Here is the uncomfortable part for anyone running a lean team. The risk in that workflow is almost never the model being wrong. It is that nobody decided what should go into the tool in the first place, and nobody is accountable for where the data ends up. AI made the drafting instant. It did nothing on its own to make delegation safe.

This article is about the control that closes that gap. It is not a product and it is not a policy document. It is a person, placed deliberately in the workflow, making the one call that no model can make for you.

Samsung learned this in public. In 2023, engineers in its semiconductor division were given access to ChatGPT to help debug source code. Within weeks, staff had pasted proprietary code and internal meeting notes into the tool, and Samsung banned generative AI across the company soon after. The story ran everywhere, from Bloomberg to The Register. Nothing malfunctioned. People simply moved sensitive material into a system that was never scoped to hold it, and the material was gone.

Two things make this failure mode easy to hit, and both are structural rather than careless.

First, most of these tools run in the browser. The data loss prevention software a security team relies on was built to watch files, endpoints and network egress, and it often cannot see a paste action into a web app at all. Worse, much of this activity happens through personal accounts that sit outside the controls the organisation does have. The leak does not trip an alarm because nothing was downloaded and nothing was emailed. Text was typed into a box.

Second, consumer-grade accounts can retain what you give them. OpenAI's own documentation states that content from consumer ChatGPT may be used to improve its models unless the user turns that setting off. Paste a client list or a contract clause from the wrong account, and that material may now sit inside a training pipeline you do not control and cannot claw back.

For a developer or a founder, the lesson is not "do not use AI". It is that the point of failure sits one layer above the model, in how the work is handed off and who decided what was safe to hand over.

If any of the data moving through that workflow identifies a person, a client, a candidate, an employee or an investor, then GDPR treats the tools and people touching it as processors acting on your behalf. That sounds abstract until you translate it into design constraints, which is what a technical team can actually work with.

Under Article 28 of the GDPR, anyone who processes personal data for you must do so under a binding contract that sets out what is processed, for what purpose and for how long, and everyone handling that data must be under a confidentiality commitment. An AI vendor you have never papered is a processor you have not accounted for. A free account used quietly by one engineer is a sub-processor nobody signed off. The obligation does not disappear because the tool was convenient.

Location is the second constraint, and it is the one most teams miss. A person, contractor or tool processing your data outside the EU means personal data is being transferred outside the EU, which requires a specific safeguard such as the Standard Contractual Clauses on top of the Article 28 contract. Keep the procesing inside the EU and that entire category of transfer risk falls away before you have to manage it. For UK teams the position is the same: the Information Commissioner's Office sets out the identical core terms for a processor contract, documented instructions, confidentiality, security measures, rules on sub-processors, and deletion or return of data at the end of the engagement.

There is now a second regulatory layer as well. The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024, adds obligations on how AI systems are deployed and governed, on top of the data-protection duties GDPR already imposes. The detail is still settling, but the direction is clear: unmanaged, undocumented AI use is getting harder to defend, not easier. None of this is exotic. It is the same governance you would apply to any third party with access to your systems. AI simply introduced a new processor that most workflows never wrote down.

"Human in the loop" usually gets discussed as a quality measure: a person checks the output so errors do not ship. That is true, and it undersells the point badly. The human is also the only part of the workflow that can make a judgment about what should never enter the tool at all.

A model cannot decide that a board deck stays out of a consumer chatbot. A browser extension cannot know that one folder holds client data governed by a contract that forbids sub-processing without written consent. Those are judgment calls, and judgment is precisely the thing you cannot automate. The person doing the delegating is your policy enforcement point, sitting at the exact moment the decision is made. They decide what gets pasted, what gets redacted first, what runs only inside an enterprise account with training switched off, and what a human handles end to end because the risk is simply not worth the minutes saved.

This reframes the division of labour that actually holds up. The AI owns the mechanical layer: summarising a long thread, turning rough notes into a first draft, extracting action items, reformatting data. The human owns the judgment layer: what matters, how it is framed, and, above all, where the data is allowed to go. Remove the human and you have not automated the work. You have removed the only control that was reading the situation and deciding what was safe.

It is worth being specific about what that judgment looks like in practice, because it is a skill and not a job title. Someone exercising it knows which inputs are off limits without having to ask, keeps sensitive material inside controlled systems by default, and treats the question "should this go into the tool at all" as part of the work rather than an afterthought. Whether that person is an engineer, an operations lead or an executive assistant, the control is the same: someone with enough context to know what is sensitive, and enough standing to say no. That habit is what separates a workflow that is genuinely governed from one that merely has a policy document nobody reads.

The practical version, for a founder or a small technical team that wants the speed without the exposure:

Most teams can put the first four in place in about a week. The last two are harder, because they are not configuration changes. They are about whether the workflow still holds when the people in it change, and that is the part most teams discover they got wrong only after someone has already left.

The speed of AI is not in question. What AI cannot supply is the judgment about what should enter it, and the accountability for where the output goes. Treat the human in the loop as a security control, design the workflow around that control, and you keep the nine-second draft without the quiet data leak. Remove the human, and the only thing you have really automated is the leak.

Motion works in onboarding because it speaks to how human perception actually operates, not because it looks modern. A few principles do most of the heavy lifting.

It directs attention. The eye is drawn to movement before it reads a single word. A gently pulsing button or an element that slides into view tells a new user where to go next without a paragraph of instructions. On a crowded first-run screen, that guidance is the difference between a confident first action and a confused exit.

It preserves context. When a panel expands, a card flips, or a new view slides in from the right, a short transition shows users where things came from and where they went. It confirms actions. Every click, toggle, and form submission is a small question: did that work? A quick visual response — a checkmark drawing itself, a row settling into place — answers it immediately. Without that feedback, users repeat actions, doubt the system, or assume something broke.

It shapes perceived performance. A thoughtful loading animation or skeleton screen makes a wait feel shorter and the product feel responsive, even when the underlying request takes exactly as long. Perception of speed is part of the experience, and motion is how you manage it.

These are the patterns that reliably help new users reach their first win.

#10566. Quantum computer breakthrough tracks qubit fluctuations in real time

Researchers at the Niels Bohr Institute have significantly increased how quickly changes in delicate quantum states can be detected inside a qubit. By combining commercially available hardware with new adaptive measurement techniques, the team can now observe rapid shifts in qubit behavior that were previously impossible to see.

Qubits are the fundamental units of quantum computers, which scientists hope will one day outperform today's most powerful machines. But qubits are extremely sensitive. The materials used to build them often contain tiny defects that scientists still do not fully understand. These microscopic imperfections can shift position hundreds of times per second. As they move, they alter how quickly a qubit loses energy and with it valuable quantum information.

Until recently, standard testing methods took up to a minute to measure qubit performance. That was far too slow to capture these rapid fluctuations. Instead, researchers could only determine an average energy loss rate, masking the true and often unstable behavior of the qubit.

It is somewhat like asking a strong workhorse to pull a plow while obstacles constantly appear in its path faster than anyone can react. The animal may be capable, but unpredictable disruptions make the job much harder.

A research team from the Niels Bohr Institute's Center for Quantum Devices and the Novo Nordisk Foundation Quantum Computing Programme, led by postdoctoral researcher Dr. Fabrizio Berritta, developed a real time adaptive measurement system that tracks changes in the qubit energy loss (relaxation) rate as they occur. The project involved collaboration with scientists from the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Leiden University, and Chalmers University.

The new approach relies on a fast classical controller that updates its estimate of a qubit's relaxation rate within milliseconds. This matches the natural speed of the fluctuations themselves, rather than lagging seconds or minutes behind as older methods did.

To achieve this, the team used a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA), a type of classical processor designed for extremely rapid operations. By running the experiment directly on the FPGA, they could quickly generate a "best guess" of how fast the qubit was losing energy using only a few measurements. This eliminated the need for slower data transfers to a conventional computer.

Programming FPGAs for such specialized tasks can be challenging. Even so, the researchers succeeded in updating the controller's internal Bayesian model after every single qubit measurement. That allowed the system to continually refine its understanding of the qubit's condition in real time.

As a result, the controller now keeps pace with the qubit's changing environment. Measurements and adjustments happen on nearly the same timescale as the fluctuations themselves, making the system roughly one hundred times faster than previously demonstrated.

The work also revealed something new. Scientists did not previously know just how quickly fluctuations occur in superconducting qubits. These experiments have now provided that insight.

FPGAs have long been used in other scientific and engineering fields. In this case, the researchers used a commercially available FPGA based controller from Quantum Machines called the OPX1000. The system can be programmed in a language similar to Python, which many physicists already use, making it more accessible to research groups worldwide.

The integration of this controller with advanced quantum hardware was made possible through close collaboration between the Niels Bohr Institute research group led by Associate Professor Morten Kjaergaard and Chalmers University, where the quantum processing unit was designed and fabricated. Quantum technologies promise powerful new capabilities, though practical large scale quantum computers are still under development. Progress often comes incrementally, but occasionally major steps forward occur.

As artificial intelligence systems began scoring extremely high on long used academic benchmarks, researchers noticed a growing issue. Their goal was to build an exam that is broad, difficult, and grounded in expert human knowledge in ways that current AI systems still struggle to handle.

The result is "Humanity's Last Exam" (HLE), a 2,500 question assessment covering mathematics, humanities, natural sciences, ancient languages, and a wide range of highly specialized academic fields. Details of the project appear in a paper published in Nature, and additional information about the exam is available at lastexam.ai.

Among the many contributors is Dr. Tung Nguyen, instructional associate professor in the Department of Computer Science and Engineering at Texas A&M. Nguyen helped write and refine many of the exam questions.

"When AI systems start performing extremely well on human benchmarks, it's tempting to think they're approaching human-level understanding," Nguyen said. "But HLE reminds us that intelligence isn't just about pattern recognition -- it's about depth, context and specialized expertise."

The purpose of the exam was not to trick or defeat human test takers. Instead, the goal was to carefully identify areas where AI systems still fall short.

Specialists from around the world wrote and reviewed the questions included in Humanity's Last Exam. Each problem was carefully designed so it has one clear, verifiable answer. The questions were also crafted to prevent quick solutions through simple internet searches.

The topics come from advanced academic challenges. Some tasks involve translating ancient Palmyrene inscriptions, while others require identifying tiny anatomical structures in birds or analyzing detailed features of Biblical Hebrew pronunciation.

Researchers tested every question against leading AI systems. If any model was able to answer a question correctly, that question was removed from the final exam. This process ensured the test remained just beyond what current AI systems can reliably solve.

Nguyen explained that the issue of AI surpassing older tests is more than a technical concern. He contributed 73 of the 2,500 publicly available questions in HLE, the second highest number among contributors, and wrote the most questions related to mathematics and computer science.

"Without accurate assessment tools, policymakers, developers and users risk misinterpreting what AI systems can actually do," he said. "Benchmarks provide the foundation for measuring progress and identifying risks."

According to the research team, high scores on tests originally designed for humans do not necessarily indicate genuine intelligence. Those benchmarks mainly measure how well AI can complete specific tasks created for human learners, rather than capturing deeper understanding.

Despite the dramatic name, Humanity's Last Exam is not meant to suggest that humans are becoming obsolete. Instead, it highlights the large amount of knowledge and expertise that still remains uniquely human.

"This isn't a race against AI," Nguyen said. "It's a method for understanding where these systems are strong and where they struggle. That understanding helps us build safer, more reliable technologies. And, importantly, it reminds us why human expertise still matters."

Humanity's Last Exam is designed to serve as a durable and transparent benchmark for future AI systems. To support that goal, the researchers have released some questions publicly while keeping the majority hidden so that AI models cannot simply memorize the answers.

"For now, Humanity's Last Exam stands as one of the clearest assessments of the gap between AI and human intelligence," Nguyen said, "and despite rapid technological advances, it remains wide."

Nguyen emphasized that the scale of the project demonstrates the value of collaboration across disciplines and countries.

"What made this project extraordinary was the scale," he said. "Experts from nearly every discipline contributed. Researchers are exploring how DNA can be engineered into working machines using creative design approaches. These include building rigid DNA joints, incorporating flexible components, and using folding techniques inspired by origami. By applying principles from larger-scale robotics such as rigid, compliant and origami robots, scientists are adapting familiar mechanical concepts to the nanoscale. This allows DNA-based systems to carry out controlled and repeatable tasks despite their extremely small size.

Guiding the motion of DNA robots in a constantly shifting molecular environment is a major challenge. To address this, scientists have developed control systems that help these machines behave in predictable ways. One important method involves DNA strand displacement, a biochemical process that enables precise programming of movement using specific DNA sequences labeled as "fuel" and "structure."

In addition to biochemical control, external physical signals such as electric fields, magnetic fields, and light can direct how these robots move. Together, these approaches provide a toolkit for fine-tuning the behavior of DNA machines with a high degree of accuracy.

The potential uses for DNA robots extend well beyond laboratory experiments. In medicine, they could function as "nano-surgeons," locating diseased cells and delivering targeted treatments with precision. Researchers are also exploring whether these machines could capture viruses like SARS-CoV-2, with future systems potentially operating as fully autonomous drug delivery platforms.

DNA robots may also play a role in advanced manufacturing. Acting as programmable templates, they could position nanoparticles with sub-nanometer accuracy. This capability could lead to breakthroughs in molecular computing and highly efficient optical devices that outperform current technologies.

Despite rapid progress, several obstacles remain. Moving from large-scale systems to molecular machines introduces challenges such as Brownian motion, which makes precise control more difficult. Many current DNA robot designs are still relatively simple and operate in isolation, limiting their usefulness in complex real-world environments.

There are also gaps in foundational knowledge. Researchers still lack detailed databases describing the mechanical properties of DNA structures, and simulation tools for predicting behavior at this scale are not yet fully developed.

To overcome these barriers, scientists emphasize the need for collaboration across disciplines. Proposed solutions include creating standardized DNA "parts libraries," using artificial intelligence to improve design and simulation, and advancing bio-manufacturing methods. Progress in these areas will be essential for scaling DNA robots and integrating them into practical applications in healthcare, manufacturing, and beyond.

"The robots of tomorrow won't just be made of metal and plastic," says the research team. "They will be biological, programmable, and intelligent. They will be the tools that allow us to finally master the molecular world."

#10538. AI breakthrough cuts energy use by 100x while boosting accuracy

Artificial intelligence is consuming enormous amounts of electricity in the United States. According to the International Energy Agency, AI systems and data centers used about 415 terawatt hours of power in 2024. That accounts for more than 10% of the country's total electricity production, and demand is projected to double by 2030.

This rapid growth has raised concerns about sustainability. In response, researchers at a School of Engineering have created a proof-of-concept AI system designed to be far more efficient. Their approach could reduce energy use by up to 100 times while also improving performance on tasks.

A Hybrid Approach Called Neuro-Symbolic AI

The research comes from the laboratory of Matthias Scheutz, Karol Family Applied Technology Professor. His team is developing neuro-symbolic AI, which combines traditional neural networks with symbolic reasoning. This method mirrors how people approach problems by breaking them into steps and categories.

The work will be presented at the International Conference of Robotics and Automation in Vienna in May and will appear in the conference proceedings.

Teaching Robots to See, Understand, and Act

Unlike familiar large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and Gemini, the team focuses on AI systems used in robotics. These systems are known as visual-language-action (VLA) models. They extend LLM capabilities by incorporating vision and physical movement.

VLA models take in visual data from cameras and instructions from language, then translate that information into real-world actions. For example, they can control a robot's wheels, arms, or fingers to complete a task.

Why Traditional AI Struggles With Simple Tasks

Conventional VLA systems rely heavily on data and trial-and-error learning. If a robot is asked to stack blocks into a tower, it must first analyze the scene, identify each block, and determine how to place them correctly.

This process often leads to mistakes. Shadows may confuse the system about a block's shape, or the robot may place pieces incorrectly, causing the structure to collapse.

These errors are similar to the problems seen in LLMs. Just as robots can misplace blocks, chatbots can generate false or misleading outputs. Examples include fabricating legal cases or producing images with unrealistic details such as extra fingers.

How Symbolic Reasoning Improves Accuracy and Efficiency

Symbolic reasoning offers a different strategy. Instead of relying only on patterns from data, it uses rules and abstract concepts such as shape and balance. This allows the system to plan more effectively and avoid unnecessary trial and error.

"Like an LLM, VLA models act on statistical results from large training sets of similar scenarios, but that can lead to errors," said Scheutz. "A neuro-symbolic VLA can apply rules that limit the amount of trial and error during learning and get to a solution much faster. Not only does it complete the task much faster, but the time spent on training the system is significantly reduced."

The researchers tested their system using the Tower of Hanoi puzzle, a classic problem that requires careful planning.

The neuro-symbolic VLA achieved a 95% success rate, compared with just 34% for standard systems. When given a more complex version of the puzzle that it had not encountered before, the hybrid system still succeeded 78% of the time. Traditional models failed every attempt.

Training time also dropped sharply. The new system learned the task in only 34 minutes, while conventional models required more than a day and a half.

Massive Energy Savings in Training and Use

Energy consumption was reduced dramatically as well. Training the neuro-symbolic model required only 1% of the energy used by a standard VLA system. During operation, it used just 5% of the energy needed by conventional approaches.

Scheutz compared this inefficiency to everyday AI tools. "These systems are just trying to predict the next word or action in a sequence, but that can be imperfect, and they can come up with inaccurate results or hallucinations. Their energy expense is often disproportionate to the task. For example, when you search on Google, the AI summary at the top of the page consumes up to 100 times more energy than the generation of the website listings."

The Growing Strain of AI on Power Infrastructure

As AI adoption accelerates across industries, demand for computing power continues to climb. Companies are building increasingly large data centers, some of which require hundreds of megawatts of electricity. That level of consumption can exceed the needs of entire small cities.

This trend has sparked a race to expand infrastructure, raising concerns about long-term energy limits.

The researchers suggest that current approaches based on LLMs and VLAs may not be sustainable in the long run. While these systems are powerful, they consume large amounts of energy and can still produce unreliable results.

In contrast, neuro-symbolic AI offers a different direction. By combining learning with structured reasoning, it may provide a more efficient and dependable foundation for future AI systems.

#10532. This new chip survives 1300°F (700°C) and could change AI forever

Modern electronics power everything from smartphones to satellites, but they all share a major limitation. Heat. Once temperatures climb above roughly 200 degrees Celsius, most devices begin to break down. For decades, this thermal barrier has been one of the toughest challenges in engineering.

Researchers at the University of Southern California now believe they have found a way past that limit.

In a study published on March 26, 2026 in Science, a team led by Joshua Yang, Arthur B. Freeman Chair Professor at the Ming Hsieh Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the USC Viterbi School of Engineering and the USC School of Advanced Computing, unveiled a new type of memory device that continues to operate at 700 degrees Celsius (~1300 degrees Fahrenheit). That temperature exceeds molten lava and goes far beyond anything previously achieved for this class of technology. The device showed no sign of failure. In fact, 700 degrees was simply the maximum their equipment could test.

"You may call it a revolution," Yang said. "It is the best high-temperature memory ever demonstrated."

The new device is known as a memristor, a nanoscale component that can both store data and perform computations. It is constructed like a microscopic layered structure, with two electrodes on either side and a thin ceramic layer in between.

Jian Zhao, the study's first author, built the device using tungsten for the top electrode, hafnium oxide ceramic in the middle, and graphene for the bottom layer. Tungsten has the highest melting point of any element, while graphene, a single-atom-thick sheet of carbon, is known for its exceptional strength and heat resistance.

This combination produced remarkable performance. The device retained data for more than 50 hours at 700 degrees without needing to be refreshed. It also endured over one billion switching cycles at that temperature and operated at just 1.5 volts with speeds measured in tens of nanoseconds.

The discovery was not part of the team's original plan. They were initially attempting to create a different graphene-based device, which did not work as intended. Along the way, they encountered something surprising.

"To be honest, it was by accident, as most discoveries are," Yang said. "If you can predict it, it's usually not surprising, and probably not significant enough."

Further investigation revealed why the device performed so well. In conventional electronics, heat causes metal atoms in the top electrode to slowly migrate through the ceramic layer. Eventually, they reach the bottom electrode, creating a permanent connection that short-circuits the device and leaves it stuck in the on state.

Graphene prevents this failure. Its interaction with tungsten is, as Yang described it, similar to oil and water. Tungsten atoms that approach the graphene surface cannot attach to it. Without a stable point to settle, they drift away instead of forming a conductive bridge. This prevents short circuits and preserves the device's function even under extreme heat.

The researchers confirmed this mechanism using advanced electron microscopy, spectroscopy, and quantum-level simulations. By understanding what happens at the atomic interface, they have turned an unexpected result into a principle that could guide future designs. Other materials with similar surface properties could be identified, which may help scale the technology for industrial production.

Electronics capable of operating above 500 degrees Celsius have long been a goal for space exploration. Venus, for example, has a surface temperature around that level, and every lander sent there has failed in part due to extreme heat. Current silicon-based chips cannot survive such conditions.

"We are now above 700 degrees, and we suspect it will go higher," Yang said.

The potential applications go far beyond space missions. Geothermal energy systems require electronics that can function deep underground, where surrounding rock can glow red-hot. Nuclear and fusion systems also expose equipment to intense heat. Even in everyday settings, durability improves significantly. A device rated for 700 degrees would be extremely robust at the roughly 125-degree temperatures often reached inside automotive electronics.

Why It Matters for Artificial Intelligence

In addition to storing data, the device offers a major advantage for artificial intelligence. Many AI systems rely heavily on matrix multiplication, a mathematical operation used in tasks like image recognition and language processing. Traditional computers perform these calculations step by step, consuming large amounts of energy.

Memristors approach the problem differently. By using Ohm's Law, where voltage times conductance equals current, the device performs calculations directly as electricity flows through it. The result is obtained instantly as the measured current.

"Over 92 percent of the computing in AI systems like ChatGPT is nothing but matrix multiplication," Yang said. "This type of device can perform that in the most efficient way, orders of magnitude faster and at lower energy."

Yang and three co-authors of the study (Qiangfei Xia, Miao Hu, and Ning Ge) have already co-founded a company called TetraMem to commercialize memristor-based AI chips at room temperature. Their lab is already using working chips from TetraMem for machine learning tasks. The high-temperature version described in this research could extend those capabilities to environments where traditional electronics cannot operate, allowing devices such as spacecraft or industrial sensors to process data directly on site.

From Lab Prototype to Real-World Technology

Despite the promising results, Yang emphasizes that practical applications are still some distance away. Memory is only one part of a complete computing system. High-temperature logic circuits will also need to be developed and integrated. In addition, the current devices were built manually at very small scales in a laboratory setting, so manufacturing at scale will take time.

"This is the first step," Yang said. "It's still a long way to go. But logically, you can see: now it makes it possible. The missing component has been made."

From a manufacturing perspective, two of the materials used in the device, tungsten and hafnium oxide, are already widely used in semiconductor production. Graphene is newer but is actively being developed by major companies such as TSMC and Samsung, and it has already been produced at wafer scale in research environments.

The work was conducted through the CONCRETE Center, short for Center of Neuromorphic Computing under Extreme Environments, a multi-university Center of Excellence led by USC and supported by the Air Force Office of Scientific Research and the Air Force Research Laboratory. Key experimental work was carried out in collaboration with Dr. Sabyasachi Ganguli's team at the AFRL Materials Lab in Dayton, Ohio, while theoretical analysis involved USC researchers and collaborators at Kumamoto University in Japan.

For Yang, publication in Science reflects more than a single achievement.

"Space exploration has never been so real, so close, and at such a large scale," he said. "This paper represents a critical leap into a much larger, more exciting frontier."

#10527. This new chip could slash data center energy waste

As data centers consume more energy to support growing digital demands, engineers at the University of California San Diego have introduced a new chip design that could make powering graphics processing units (GPUs) more efficient. The innovation focuses on a key function in electronics: converting high voltages into the lower levels required by computing hardware. In laboratory testing, a prototype chip successfully performed this type of voltage conversion with high efficiency under conditions similar to those found in modern data centers.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, suggest the potential for smaller and more energy-efficient systems in advanced computing environments.

Rethinking DC-DC Converters for Modern Electronics

At the center of the new design is an improved version of a widely used component known as a DC-DC step-down converter. These converters are found in nearly all electronic devices and serve as a critical link between power sources and sensitive circuits. Their job is to take a high incoming voltage and reduce it to the exact level needed for safe operation.

In data centers, electricity is often distributed at 48 volts, while GPU processors typically require much lower voltages, usually between 1 and 5 volts. Efficiently managing this large voltage drop has become increasingly challenging as computing systems grow more powerful and compact.

Limits of Traditional Power Conversion Technology

Conventional step-down converters often struggle when dealing with large differences between input and output voltage. As that gap increases, efficiency drops and it becomes harder to supply enough current. Most existing designs rely on magnetic components such as inductors. While these components have been refined over many years, they are approaching their practical limits and are becoming harder to improve further.

"We've gotten so good at designing inductive converters that there's not really much room left to improve them to meet future needs," said study senior author Patrick Mercier, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering.

Exploring Piezoelectric Resonators as an Alternative

To move beyond these limitations, Mercier and his team, including first author Jae-Young Ko, an electrical and computer engineering Ph.D. student at UC San Diego, investigated a different approach using piezoelectric resonators. These small devices store and transfer energy through mechanical vibrations rather than magnetic fields.

Converters based on piezoelectric components could offer several advantages. They have the potential to be smaller, more energy dense, more efficient, and easier to manufacture at scale. "They have a lot of room to grow and have the potential to deliver better performance than anything that's come before them," Mercier said.

However, earlier versions of piezoelectric converters have had difficulty maintaining efficiency and delivering sufficient power when handling large voltage differences.

Hybrid Design Achieves High Efficiency and Power Output

To overcome these issues, the researchers created a hybrid converter that combines a piezoelectric resonator with small, commercially available capacitors arranged in a carefully designed configuration. This setup enables the system to handle larger voltage conversions more effectively.

The team incorporated this design into a prototype chip and tested its performance. The device successfully converted 48 volts down to 4.8 volts -- a level commonly required in data centers -- with a peak efficiency of 96.2 percent. It also delivered roughly four times more output current than previous piezoelectric-based designs.

This hybrid approach offers several benefits. It creates multiple pathways for energy to move through the system, reduces wasted power, and lessens the strain on the resonator. Together, these improvements enhance both efficiency and power delivery while only slightly increasing the chip's size.

Challenges and Next Steps for Real-World Use

Although the technology shows strong promise, it is still in the early stages of development. The researchers view it as an important step toward overcoming the constraints of current power conversion systems. Future efforts will focus on refining materials, improving circuit designs, and developing better packaging methods.

One challenge is that piezoelectric resonators physically vibrate, which means they cannot be attached to circuit boards using standard soldering techniques. New integration strategies will be needed to incorporate them into electronic systems, Mercier explained.

"Piezoelectric-based converters aren't quite ready to replace existing power converter technologies yet," Mercier added. "But they offer a trajectory for improvement. We need to continue to improve on multiple areas -- materials, circuits and packaging -- to make this technology ready for data center applications."

#10522. “Giant superatoms” could finally solve quantum computing’s biggest problem

Researchers at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden have introduced a new theoretical design for quantum systems based on what they call "giant superatoms." This concept offers a fresh way to protect, control, and share quantum information, potentially bringing scientists closer to building large-scale quantum computers.

Quantum computers are expected to transform fields like drug discovery and encryption by solving problems that are far beyond the reach of conventional machines. However, progress has been limited by a major challenge known as decoherence. This occurs when quantum bits, or qubits, lose their information due to interactions with their surroundings. Even small amounts of electromagnetic noise can disrupt the fragile quantum states needed for computation.

"Quantum systems are extraordinarily powerful but also extremely fragile. The key to making them useful is learning how to control their interaction with the surrounding environment," says Lei Du, postdoctoral researcher in applied quantum technology at Chalmers.

Lei Du is the lead author of a study that outlines this new type of quantum system. The design is built around giant superatoms, which combine several important features. These systems reduce decoherence, remain stable, and consist of multiple interconnected "atoms" that function together as a single unit.

Giant superatoms bring together two previously separate ideas in quantum physics: giant atoms and superatoms. While each has been studied on its own, this is the first time they have been merged into a single system. These structures behave like atoms but are not found in nature. Instead, they are engineered by scientists (see fact box below).

The idea of giant atoms was first introduced by researchers at Chalmers over a decade ago and is now widely used in the field. A giant atom is typically designed as a qubit (which is the smallest unit of quantum information). Unlike ordinary atoms, it connects to light or sound waves at multiple, physically separated points. This allows it to interact with its environment in several places at once, helping it preserve quantum information.

"Waves that leave one connection point can travel through the environment and return to affect the atom at another point -- similar to hearing an echo of your own voice before you've finished speaking. Although giant atoms have improved understanding of quantum behavior, they have had limitations when it comes to entanglement. Entanglement allows multiple qubits to share a single quantum state and act as one coordinated system, which is essential for powerful quantum computers.

This work creates new possibilities for building quantum systems that are both scalable and reliable. The researchers plan to move from theory toward actually constructing these systems. #10518. This simple change stops robot swarms from getting stuck

Imagine a swarm of robots rushing to complete an urgent job, such as cleaning up an oil spill or assembling complex machinery. At first, adding more robots speeds things up. But after a certain point, the space becomes crowded, robots start interfering with one another, and overall progress slows.

This raises a simple but important question: in a limited area, how many robots can you deploy before efficiency starts to drop? Researchers at Harvard believe they have found a clear answer.

A new study from the lab of L. Mahadevan, the Lola England de Valpine Professor of Applied Mathematics, Organismic and Evolutionary Biology, and Physics, shows that adding a controlled amount of randomness to how robots move can reduce congestion and improve performance in crowded environments.

The work combines mathematical modeling, computer simulations, and real-world experiments. It demonstrates how basic local movement rules can lead to organized, efficient outcomes on a larger scale. The findings could influence how robotic fleets are designed and may even apply to human crowd management and traffic flow. The research was published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences and led by applied mathematics Ph.D. student Lucy Liu, with guidance from SEAS Senior Research Fellow Justin Werfel.

Why Randomness Helps Predict Complex Behavior

Studying dense crowds is difficult because individuals can take countless possible paths and interact in unpredictable ways, Liu explained. To simplify the problem, the researchers treated each robot as a basic unit with a small, adjustable amount of variation in its movement.

"This might be counterintuitive, because how could randomness make things easier to work with?" said Liu. "But in this case, when you have a lot of randomness, it becomes possible to take averages -- average distances, average times, average behaviors. This makes it a lot easier to make predictions."

To explore this idea, the team created computer simulations of robot groups, referred to as agents. Each agent started at a random location and was assigned a random destination. Once it reached its target, it immediately received a new one, mimicking continuous task assignment in real-world systems.

Each agent moved toward its goal with a tunable amount of variation, described as "noise." With no noise, agents moved in straight lines. With high noise, their paths became erratic and inefficient. However, this wandering also helped them navigate around one another.

The simulations revealed a clear pattern. When agents moved in perfectly straight paths, they quickly formed dense clusters and traffic jams that halted progress. When movement became too random, congestion disappeared but efficiency dropped due to excessive wandering.

Between these extremes, the researchers identified a sweet spot. In this range, agents occasionally bumped into one another and formed short-lived clusters, but still managed to slip past and keep moving. This balance allowed the system to maintain a steady flow.

Using these insights, the team developed formulas to estimate "goal attainment rate," or how many destinations are reached over time. These equations made it possible to determine the ideal combination of crowd density and movement randomness to maximize performance.

To confirm their findings, Liu collaborated with physicist Federico Toschi at Eindhoven University of Technology in the Netherlands. Together, they set up experiments with small wheeled robots in a lab equipped with an overhead camera.

Each robot carried a QR code so its position could be tracked and updated with new destinations. Although the physical robots moved more slowly and less precisely than the simulated agents, they displayed the same overall patterns.

The experiments supported a key idea: highly complex coordination does not require advanced intelligence or centralized control. Instead, simple local rules can produce effective group behavior, at least within certain density limits.

"Understanding how active matter, whether it is a swarm of ants, a herd of animals, or a group of robots, become functional and execute tasks in crowded environments using the principles of self-organization, is relevant to many questions in behavioral ecology," Mahadevan said. "Our study suggests strategies that might well be much broader than the instantiation we have focused on."

Liu noted that she has long been interested in designing safer and more efficient crowded spaces. This research points toward a future where the movement of large groups, whether robots, vehicles, or people, could be predicted and optimized using mathematical tools.

The results suggest that introducing controlled variability into movement patterns may improve flow in many real-world systems, from factory floors to city streets.

#10512. Quantum AI just got shockingly good at predicting chaos

A new study led by researchers at UCL (University College London) shows that combining quantum computing with artificial intelligence can significantly improve predictions of complex physical systems over long periods. The hybrid approach outperforms leading models that rely only on conventional computers.

The results, published in Science Advances, could enhance simulations of how liquids and gases behave, known as fluid dynamics. These types of models are essential in fields such as climate science, transportation, medicine, and energy production.

The improved accuracy appears to come from how quantum computers process information. Unlike traditional computers that use bits set to either 1 or 0, quantum computers use qubits, which can exist as 1, 0, or anything in between. In addition, each qubit can influence others, allowing a relatively small number of qubits to represent an enormous number of possible states.

Professor Peter Coveney, senior author from UCL Chemistry and the Advanced Research Computing Centre, explained the challenge: "To make predictions about complex systems, we can either run a full simulation, which might take weeks -- often too long to be useful -- or we can use an AI model which is quicker but more unreliable over longer time scales.

"Our quantum-informed AI model means we could provide more accurate predictions quickly. Making predictions about fluid flow and turbulence is a fundamental science challenge but it also has many applications. Our method can be used in climate forecasting, in modeling blood flow and the interaction of molecules, or to better design wind farms so they generate more energy."

Although quantum computers are widely expected to surpass classical machines in power, their real-world use has so far been limited. This new approach integrates quantum computing into a specific stage of the AI training process.

Typically, AI models learn from large datasets generated by simulations or observations. In this case, the data is first processed by a quantum computer, which identifies key statistical patterns that remain stable over time. These patterns, known as invariant statistical properties, are then used to guide the training of an AI model running on a conventional supercomputer.

The quantum-informed AI system delivered about 20 percent greater accuracy compared to standard AI models that did not use quantum-derived patterns. It also maintained stable predictions over longer periods, even when modeling chaotic systems.

Another major advantage was efficiency. The method required hundreds of times less memory, making it far more practical for large-scale simulations.

This performance boost comes from two defining features of quantum computing. Entanglement allows qubits to influence each other regardless of distance, while superposition enables a qubit to exist in multiple states at once until measured. Together, these properties allow quantum systems to process vast amounts of information in a compact form.

Demonstrating Practical Quantum Advantage

First author Maida Wang of the UCL Centre for Computational Science said: "Our new method appears to demonstrate 'quantum advantage' in a practical way -- that is, the quantum computer outperforms what is possible through classical computing alone. These findings could inspire the development of novel classical approaches that achieve even higher accuracy, though they would likely lack the remarkable data compression and parameter efficiency offered by our method. The next steps are to scale up the method using larger datasets and to apply it to real-world situations which typically involve even more complexity. In addition, a provable theoretical framework will be proposed."

Co-first author Xiao Xue, from Advanced Research Computing at UCL, added: "In this work, we demonstrate for the first time that quantum computing can be meaningfully integrated with classical machine learning methods to tackle complex dynamical systems, including fluid mechanics. It is exciting to see this kind of 'quantum-informed' approach moving towards practical use."

The researchers suggest that quantum computers are particularly well suited to modeling these systems because they can compactly represent their underlying physics. Many complex systems behave in ways that resemble quantum effects, where changes in one region can influence distant parts of the system, similar to entanglement.

Overcoming Limits of Current Quantum Hardware

Current quantum computers face challenges such as noise, errors, and interference, which often require large numbers of measurements. The new method avoids these issues by using the quantum computer only once during the workflow, rather than repeatedly exchanging data between quantum and classical systems.

The study used a 20-qubit IQM quantum computer connected to powerful classical computing resources at the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Germany.

To function, quantum computers must operate at extremely low temperatures, around minus 273C (close to absolute zero, colder than anything in space).

The research was funded by UCL and the UK's Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC), with additional support from IQM Quantum Computers and the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Munich.

Behind almost every Twitch tool you have ever seen, the stat trackers, the moderation bots, the stream overlays, the alert systems, sits one interface: the Twitch Helix API. Twitch is unusually generous with public data, and Helix is the door to it. If you have ever wondered how third-party sites know who is live, what a channel's viewership looks like, or details a Twitch profile page does not even display, this is the machinery.

This article is a practical overview of Helix for developers who have not used it yet: what it offers, how its authentication works, what people build with it, and where the sharp edges are.

Helix is Twitch's current REST API, served from api.twitch.tv/helix. It replaced the old "Kraken" API, which was fully retired in 2022, so any tutorial still showing Kraken endpoints is a sign to keep scrolling. Helix covers users, streams, channels, games and categories, clips, chat, moderation actions, subscriptions, and events, with JSON in and JSON out.

Two things distinguish it from many platform APIs. First, a large share of it works without any user's permission, because the data is public by Twitch's own definition. Second, it is paired with EventSub, a push system that notifies your application when things happen (a stream goes live, a user follows, a message arrives) instead of forcing you to poll. Most serious Twitch tools use both: Helix for lookups, EventSub for live events.

Authentication is where most newcomers stall, mainly because Twitch offers several OAuth flows and tutorials rarely say which one you actually need. The short version:

Every request needs a Client ID, which you get by registering an application in the Twitch developer console (two-factor authentication on your account is required first). Requests carry the Client ID and a bearer token together; forgetting the Client ID header is the classic mystery 401.

App access tokens come from the client credentials flow, a single server-to-server request with your ID and secret. They have no user attached and that is fine, because public reads, user profiles, live streams, top games, clips, need no user context. For most read-only tools, this is the only flow you ever touch.

Tokens expire, so real applications cache them with their reported lifetime and refresh on a 401 rather than assuming a token lives forever.

A tour of the endpoints that power most tools:

Users. Give the users endpoint a username and it returns the account's public record: display name, profile image, description, Affiliate or Partner status, and the account's exact creation timestamp. That last field is interesting because Twitch does not display it anywhere on a profile, yet it is one of the platform's most useful trust signals, moderators use account age to spot throwaway accounts, and streamers use it to filter giveaway entries. The creation date alone powers a small subcategory of tools: free account age checkers, such as Twitch account age checker, are essentially this one endpoint with a clean page around it, taking any public username and returning the creation date, age, avatar, and broadcaster status. It is a good illustration of how far a single well-chosen Helix field can go.

Streams. The stream's endpoint reports who is live right now, with titles, categories, thumbnail URLs, and viewer counts. This is the backbone of every "is my favorite streamer live" notifier and every directory-style site, and polling it over time is how stat trackers chart viewership history.

Games and categories. Top games, category search, and metadata. Combined with streams data, this is how analytics sites rank categories and how tools answer "how crowded is this category right now."

Clips and videos. Clip metadata, view counts, and creation tools. Highlight compilers and clip-sharing bots live here.

Helix enforces a points-based rate limit per Client ID, and every response tells you where you stand through Ratelimit-Limit, Ratelimit-Remaining, and Ratelimit-Reset headers. Well-behaved tools read those headers and back off before hitting zero; badly behaved ones retry 429s in a loop and wonder why things get worse. For lookup-style tools the practical fix is caching, data like account creation dates never changes, so there is no reason to ask twice.

A few more edges worth knowing before they bite:

Usernames change, IDs do not. Store user IDs, not logins, or your saved data will eventually point at the wrong person.

Missing users are not errors. Looking up a nonexistent username returns a successful response with an empty data array, so handle the empty case explicitly.

Batching exists. The users endpoint takes up to 100 logins in one call, which matters the moment your tool checks lists instead of individuals.

Secrets stay server-side. The client secret identifies your application; calling the token endpoint from browser code hands your app's identity to anyone who opens devtools. Public-facing tools put a small server between the browser and Twitch.

The reason Twitch's third-party ecosystem is so large is that the barrier is so low: one public API, generous free access, and a platform where small questions genuinely matter to people. That is why the space is full of narrow tools that each answer one thing well, when was this account created, is this channel live, how fast is this category growing, which clips popped this week. Most of them are a weekend of work on top of one or two endpoints, and some of them grow into real products.

If you want to start, the path is short: register an application at the developer console, request an app access token, and call the users or streams endpoint for something you actually care about. The official documentation at dev.twitch.tv is current and well maintained, which is not something every platform can claim. Pick one piece of data Twitch collects but does not display, put a clean interface on it, and you are following the same recipe as most of the Twitch tools you already use.

#10504. Think AI "knows" what it’s doing? Scientists say think again

These are everyday words people use to describe what goes on in the human mind. But when those same terms are applied to artificial intelligence, they can unintentionally make machines seem more human than they really are.

"We use mental verbs all the time in our daily lives, so it makes sense that we might also use them when we talk about machines -- it helps us relate to them," said Jo Mackiewicz, professor of English at Iowa State. "But at the same time, when we apply mental verbs to machines, there's also a risk of blurring the line between what humans and AI can do."

Mackiewicz and Jeanine Aune, a teaching professor of English and director of the advanced communication program at Iowa State, are part of a research team that studied how writers describe AI using human-like language. This type of wording, known as anthropomorphism, assigns human traits to non-human systems. Their study, "Anthropomorphizing Artificial Intelligence: A Corpus Study of Mental Verbs Used with AI and ChatGPT," was published in Technical Communication Quarterly.

The research team also included Matthew J. Baker, associate professor of linguistics at Brigham Young University, and Jordan Smith, assistant professor of English at the University of Northern Colorado. Both previously studied at Iowa State University.

Why Human-Like Language About AI Can Be Misleading

According to the researchers, using mental verbs to describe AI can create a false impression. Words such as "think," "know," "understand," and "want" suggest that a system has thoughts, intentions, or awareness. In reality, AI does not possess beliefs or feelings. It produces responses by analyzing patterns in data, not by forming ideas or making conscious decisions.

Mackiewicz and Aune also pointed out that this kind of language can overstate what AI is capable of. Phrases like "AI decided" or "ChatGPT knows" can make systems seem more independent or intelligent than they actually are. This can lead to unrealistic expectations about how reliable or capable AI is.

There is also a broader concern. When AI is described as if it has intentions, it can distract from the humans behind it. Developers, engineers, and organizations are responsible for how these systems are built and used.

"Certain anthropomorphic phrases may even stick in readers' minds and can potentially shape public perception of AI in unhelpful ways," Aune said.

How News Writers Actually Use AI Language

To better understand how often this kind of language appears, the researchers analyzed the News on the Web (NOW) corpus. This massive dataset contains more than 20 billion words from English-language news articles published in 20 countries.

They focused on how frequently mental verbs such as "learns," "means," and "knows" were used alongside terms like AI and ChatGPT.

Mental Verbs Are Less Common Than Expected

The study found that news writers do not frequently pair AI-related terms with mental verbs.

While anthropomorphism is common in everyday speech, it appears far less often in news writing. "Anthropomorphism has been shown to be common in everyday speech, but we found there's far less usage in news writing," Mackiewicz said.

Among the examples identified, the word "needs" appeared most often with AI, showing up 661 times. For ChatGPT, "knows" was the most frequent pairing, but it appeared only 32 times.

The researchers noted that editorial standards may play a role. Associated Press guidelines, which discourage attributing human emotions or traits to AI, could be influencing how journalists write about these technologies.

Context Matters More Than the Words Themselves

Even when mental verbs were used, they were not always anthropomorphic.

For instance, the word "needs" often described basic requirements rather than human-like qualities. Phrases such as "AI needs large amounts of data" or "AI needs some human assistance" are similar to how people describe non-human systems like cars or recipes. In these cases, the language does not imply that AI has thoughts or desires.

In other cases, "needs" was used to express what should be done, such as "AI needs to be trained" or "AI needs to be implemented." Aune explained that these examples were often written in passive voice, which shifts responsibility back to human actors rather than the technology itself.

The study also showed that not all uses of mental verbs are equal. Some phrases move closer to suggesting human-like qualities.

For example, statements like "AI needs to understand the real world" can imply expectations tied to human reasoning, ethics, or awareness. These uses go beyond simple descriptions and begin to suggest deeper capabilities.

"These instances showed that anthropomorphizing isn't all-or-nothing and instead exists on a spectrum," Aune said

Overall, the researchers found that anthropomorphism in news coverage is both less frequent and more nuanced than many might assume.

"Overall, our analysis shows that anthropomorphization of AI in news writing is far less common -- and far more nuanced -- than we might think," Mackiewicz said. "Even the instances that did anthropomorphize AI varied widely in strength."

The findings highlight the importance of context. Simply counting words is not enough to understand how language shapes meaning.

"For writers, this nuance matters: the language we choose shapes how readers understand AI systems, their capabilities and the humans responsible for them," Mackiewicz said.

The research team also emphasized that these insights can help professionals think more carefully about how they describe AI in their work.

"Our findings can help technical and professional communication practitioners reflect on how they think about AI technologies as tools in their writing process and how they write about AI," the research team wrote in the published study.

As AI continues to develop, the way people talk about it will remain important. Mackiewicz and Aune said writers will need to stay mindful of how word choices influence perception.

Looking ahead, the team suggested that future studies could explore how different words shape understanding and whether even rare uses of anthropomorphic language have a strong impact on how people view AI.

#10500. Artificial neurons successfully communicate with living brain cells

Engineers at Northwestern University have created printed artificial neurons that go beyond imitation and can directly interact with real brain cells. These flexible, low-cost devices produce electrical signals that closely resemble those generated by living neurons, allowing them to activate biological brain tissue.

In experiments using slices of mouse brain, the artificial neurons successfully triggered responses in real neurons. This result shows a new level of compatibility between electronic devices and living neural systems.

Toward Brain Interfaces and Energy-Efficient AI

This advance moves researchers closer to electronics that can directly interface with the nervous system. Potential uses include brain-machine interfaces and neuroprosthetics, such as implants that could help restore hearing, vision, or movement.

The technology also points toward a new generation of computing systems inspired by the brain. By replicating how neurons communicate, future hardware could perform complex tasks using far less energy. The brain remains the most energy-efficient computing system known, and scientists hope to apply its principles to modern technology.

The study will be published on April 15 in the journal Nature Nanotechnology.

"The world we live in today is dominated by artificial intelligence (AI)," said Northwestern's Mark C. Hersam, who led the study. "The way you make AI smarter is by training it on more and more data. This data-intensive training leads to a massive power-consumption problem. Therefore, we have to come up with more efficient hardware to handle big data and AI. Because the brain is five orders of magnitude more energy efficient than a digital computer, it makes sense to look to the brain for inspiration for next-generation computing."

Hersam is an expert in brain-inspired computing and holds multiple roles at Northwestern University, including the Walter P. Murphy Professor of Materials Science and Engineering at the McCormick School of Engineering. He also is a professor of medicine at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine and a professor of chemistry at the Weinberg College of Arts and Sciences. In addition, he serves as chair of the department of materials science and engineering, director of the Materials Research Science and Engineering Center, and a member of the International Institute for Nanotechnology. He co-led the study with Vinod K. Sangwan, a research associate professor at McCormick.

Why the Brain Outperforms Traditional Silicon

Modern computers handle increasing workloads by packing billions of identical transistors onto rigid, two-dimensional silicon chips. Each component behaves the same way, and once manufactured, the system remains fixed.

The brain works very differently. It consists of many types of neurons, each with specialized roles, arranged in soft, three-dimensional networks. These networks are constantly changing, forming and adjusting connections as learning occurs.

"Silicon achieves complexity by having billions of identical devices," Hersam said. "Everything is the same, rigid and fixed once it's fabricated. The brain is the opposite. It's heterogeneous, dynamic and three-dimensional. To move in that direction, we need new materials and new ways to build electronics."

Although artificial neurons have been developed before, most produce overly simple signals. To achieve more complex behavior, engineers typically need large networks of devices, which increases energy use.

Printable Materials Enable Brain-Like Behavior

To better replicate real neural activity, Hersam's team built artificial neurons using soft, printable materials that more closely match the brain's structure. Their approach relies on electronic inks made from nanoscale flakes of molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), which acts as a semiconductor, and graphene, which serves as an electrical conductor. These materials were deposited onto flexible polymer surfaces using aerosol jet printing.

Previously, researchers treated the polymer in these inks as a flaw because it interfered with electrical performance. As a result, they removed it after printing. In this work, the team used that same feature to enhance the device.

"Instead of fully removing the polymer, we partially decompose it," he said. "Then, when we pass current through the device, we drive further decomposition of the polymer. This decomposition occurs in a spatially inhomogeneous manner, leading to formation of a conductive filament, such that all the current is constricted into a narrow region in space."

That narrow conductive path produces a sudden electrical response similar to a neuron firing. The resulting device can generate a wide variety of signals, including single spikes, continuous firing, and bursting patterns, closely resembling real neural communication.

Because each artificial neuron can produce more complex signals, fewer components are needed to perform advanced tasks. This could significantly improve computing efficiency.

Testing Artificial Neurons on Real Brain Tissue

To evaluate whether the artificial neurons could truly interact with living systems, the researchers partnered with Indira M. Raman, the Bill and Gayle Cook Professor of Neurobiology at Weinberg. Her team applied the artificial signals to slices of mouse cerebellum.

The results showed that the electrical spikes matched key biological properties, including their timing and duration. These signals reliably activated real neurons and triggered neural circuits in a way similar to natural brain activity.

"Other labs have tried to make artificial neurons with organic materials, and they spiked too slowly," Hersam said. "Or they used metal oxides, which are too fast. We are within a temporal range that was not previously demonstrated for artificial neurons. You can see the living neurons respond to our artificial neuron. So, we've demonstrated signals that are not only the right timescale but also the right spike shape to interact directly with living neurons."

Low-Cost, Sustainable Manufacturing and AI Implications

Beyond performance, the new approach offers environmental and practical advantages. The manufacturing process is simple and inexpensive, and the additive printing method places material only where it is needed, reducing waste.

Improving energy efficiency is especially important as artificial intelligence systems grow more demanding. Large data centers already consume vast amounts of power and require significant water for cooling.

"To meet the energy demands of AI, tech companies are building gigawatt data centers powered by dedicated nuclear power plants," Hersam said. "It is evident that this massive power consumption will limit further scaling of computing since it's hard to imagine a next-generation data center requiring 100 nuclear power plants. #10490. This AI knew the answers but didn’t understand the questions

Psychologists have long debated whether the human mind can be explained by a single, unified theory or if different functions such as attention and memory must be studied separately. Now, artificial intelligence (AI) is entering that debate, offering a new way to explore how the mind works.

In July 2025, a study published in Nature introduced an AI model called "Centaur." Built on standard large language models and refined using data from psychological experiments, Centaur was designed to simulate human cognitive behavior. It reportedly performed well across 160 tasks, including decision-making, executive control, and other mental processes. The results drew widespread attention and were seen as a possible step toward AI systems that could replicate human thinking more broadly.

A more recent study published in National Science Open challenges those claims. Researchers from Zhejiang University argue that Centaur's apparent success may come from overfitting. In other words, instead of understanding the tasks, the model may have learned to recognize patterns in the training data and reproduce expected answers.

To test this idea, the researchers created several new evaluation scenarios. In one example, they replaced the original multiple-choice prompts, which described specific psychological tasks, with the instruction "Please choose option A." If the model truly understood the task, it should have consistently selected option A. Instead, Centaur continued to choose the "correct answers" from the original dataset.

This behavior suggests that the model was not interpreting the meaning of the questions. Rather, it relied on learned statistical patterns to "guess" answers. The researchers compared this to a student who scores well by memorizing test formats without actually understanding the material.

The findings highlight the need for caution when assessing the abilities of large language models. While these systems can be highly effective at fitting data, their "black-box" nature makes it difficult to know how they arrive at their outputs. This can lead to issues such as hallucinations or misinterpretations. Careful and varied testing is essential to determine whether a model truly has the skills it appears to demonstrate.

The Real Challenge: Language Understanding

Although Centaur was presented as a model capable of simulating cognition, its biggest limitation appears to be in language comprehension. Specifically, it struggles to recognize and respond to the intent behind questions. The study suggests that achieving true language understanding may be one of the most important challenges in developing AI systems that can model human cognition more fully.
