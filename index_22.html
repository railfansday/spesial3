#7026. SCOTUS may agree some schools can exclude children of LGBTQ+ parents

Justice Samuel Alito pointed to “little evidence of a practical problem” and said LGBTQ+ couples could find “other preschools” if Catholic schools reject their children.

In St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, Catholic parents and schools in Colorado are seeking to partake in a universal preschool program that allows families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice. The benefit is worth about $6,000 per child, according to the petitioners.

However, Colorado requires any school participating in the program to sign a nondiscrimination agreement, which includes a prohibition on discrimination based on LGBTQ+ identity.

The petitioners — which include parents of preschool-age children and two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

The petitioners also claim that Colorado has allowed many “exemptions,” such as for schools aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"They are wrong," Helen Norton, an attorney representing Colorado's Department of Early Childhood, said during oral arguments Tuesday. “Schools advance equal opportunity when they recognize that a child with a visual impairment needs different treatment in order to learn. Schools advance equal opportunity when they understand that kids in poverty can't learn if they're hungry, or because they're in pain because their parents can't afford dental care to treat their cavities."

On the other hand, Nicholas Reaves, who represented the petitioners, said Catholic families and schools "just want the same treatment" as others in being able to participate in the universal preschool program.

The court’s ideologically conservative justices seemed to agree that there may be some instances when private religious schools receiving public funding, such as through Colorado's universal preschool program, should be able to exercise their religious beliefs in a way that excludes LGBTQ+ families and their children.

"So we have here a requirement that has a very severe impact on certain religions and that was something that everyone could've anticipated and almost must have anticipated when this requirement was imposed," said Justice Samuel Alito on Tuesday. "And we have little evidence of a practical problem, little evidence — if any evidence, you’ll tell me if I’m wrong — that there are LGBTQ+ couples who cannot find other preschools to send their children to."

Justice Brett Kavanaugh questioned where schools would draw the line, such as refusing to hire gay coaches or athletic directors.

"I think every diocese can make their own decision about where exactly that line is," said Reaves.

The case follows similar ones decided by the high court in recent years.

In Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, heard by justices in 2020, the Supreme Court decided in favor of a Montana mother who wanted to use the state’s tax credit-funded scholarship to send her children to a Christian school.

In 2022, the justices ruled that Maine violated the First Amendment by restricting religious private schools from using public tuition assistance programs.

And in 2018’s Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd. v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, the court decided in favor of a Colorado baker who refused to make a custom wedding cake for a same-sex couple.

The Supreme Court's decision in the St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy case will be released as part of its 2026-27 term, which breaks during the summer.

#7022. U of Kentucky grants employee religious exemption from AI use

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A spokesperson said the university found the humanist administrator could still perform the essential functions of his job with the requested accommodation.

AI’s adoption has raised a number of compliance considerations for employers, and employment law experts have warned that religious objections to AI use could increasingly come to the forefront. That’s in part because religious discrimination claims have already climbed in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic, but it is also due to the ethical and moral questions that surround AI use.

In his statement, for instance, the University of Kentucky employee cited AI’s environmental costs, its “interference with people’s ability to earn a living” and its “supplanting and theft of human creativity.” The employee said these factors meant that use of AI would go against his conscience.

Jay Blanton, spokesperson for the university, said in an email that the institution reviews religious accommodation requests on an individual basis. It concluded that the employee in question, a research administrator, could perform the essential functions of his job with the requested accommodation.

“As technology and job responsibilities evolve, accommodations may be reviewed to ensure they remain reasonable and workable,” Blanton noted.

It’s not the first reported instance of a U.S. employee being granted religious accommodation with respect to AI. In June, Business Insider reported that a North Carolina-based Unitarian Universalist employee who worked as a software engineer received an AI accommodation after she raised environmental and ethical objections to the tech.

Under employment laws like Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act, employers may deny an employee’s proposed religious accommodation if they can show that it poses an undue hardship.

But that calculation has shifted in recent years thanks to a landmark 2023 U.S. Supreme Court decision. In Groff v. DeJoy, SCOTUS held that, in order for a proposed accommodation to pose undue hardship, the burden must be substantial in the overall context of the employer’s business. Legal experts previously told HR Dive that this is a higher bar than the court’s prior precedent, which defined undue hardship as that which poses a “more than de minimis cost.”

#7020. Fort Collins-area district to close, consolidate 8 schools

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Colorado’s Poudre School District is moving forward with its plan after years of enrollment declines have led to over 10,500 empty seats.

Poudre’s enrollment declines reflect a larger trend in both Colorado and across the nation, which is also causing many other districts to close schools.

The enrollment challenges in Poudre largely stem from declining birthrates, higher cost of living and fewer young families moving into the area, according to the district. Poudre said in a September presentation that it expects enrollment to fall by 2% annually for the next five years. For every 2% drop in enrollment, the district loses $6 million in revenue.

For years, Poudre has faced enrollment declines that the district has tried to offset by consolidating staff, cutting millions of dollars from its budget, and eliminating hundreds of positions, said Superintendent Brian Kingsley in a September press conference announcing the initial school closure and consolidation proposal.

“Every year that we have protected buildings, we have done it by asking teachers and staff to do more with less,” Kingsley said. “That is not a sustainable way to support the people who teach our students, and it is not producing the experience for all of our students that each of them deserve at every single school. We can be better than that.”

By the 2027-28 school year, Poudre will close three elementary schools and one middle school. In that same period, the district will also consolidate a high school and an alternative education campus into one new school at a location still to be determined. The district will also close a dual language elementary school in 2028-29, but it plans to develop a "more unified" dual language program that will open that same school year.

Another elementary school was approved for closure in 2028-29, unless the Town of Timnath — a local community within Poudre’s district boundaries — secures full funding to cover the costs of building a new elementary school.

Meanwhile, three other elementary schools that were originally recommended for closure were not approved by the board and will remain open. Those three historic schools are located in rural mountainous areas of the district, and their proposed closure received pushback from local community members.

District parents urged the board to continue operating the three mountain elementary schools, noting that closures would force students to commute over 70 miles roundtrip and likely push those families to consider enrolling their children in charter schools, private schools, or homeschooling.

“Our three campuses function as a single, highly efficient school spread across a vast geographic region, sharing staff, specialized teachers, and administrative oversight to deliver some of the highest academic test scores in the district,” a local parent advocacy website said. “Closing any of these campuses doesn’t just shuffle students down the street — it fundamentally disrupts rural life and puts our children at risk.”

Earlier in October, the Board of Education at Adams 12 Five Star Schools in the Denver area approved to close six of its schools in the 2027-28 school year. The decision came as the district reported losing 6,400 students over the last decade and that enrollment decreased faster than initially projected during that period.

Adams 12 also pointed to sharp enrollment declines in Colorado generally, which the district said appears to be dropping faster than many other states. For example, the state’s enrollment reached its lowest point in a decade during the 2025-26 school year — at 870,793 students, according to the Colorado Department of Education.

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The coalition’s governance dispute with founding member Antioch University could worsen with the departure of its only independent board member.

A recent board change at the Coalition for the Common Good could plunge the organization even deeper into an internal stalemate as its member universities battle over its structure and future.

Lynn Pasquerella, best known as the president of the American Association of Colleges and Universities, has left her role as CCG’s only independent director. She joined CCG shortly after Ohio-based Antioch and Otterbein universities formed it in 2023. To date, the two universities are still the only members.

Her departure leaves the CCG board with eight members — four chosen by Antioch and four by Otterbein. That could make the six-vote supermajority necessary to confirm her replacement as the board’s independent member difficult given the tensions between the universities. Six-vote supermajorities are also needed for some action items of the CCG board.

Pasquerella’s term ended this fall, she confirmed to Higher Ed Dive. She initially resigned on May 20 citing increasing scheduling demands, but agreed to stay on to help with the transition through her term, which ended Sept. 30. Late last week, she wrote to CCG Board Chair Dan Gifford to confirm that her resignation was in effect and if it wasn't, to offer formal notice of her immediate resignation.

The coalition, originally the brainchild of former Antioch leaders, was meant to serve as an innovative network of like-minded institutions that could share resources and work together on programming. This year, however, it has become mired in a fierce governance and legal dispute.

Litigation began in August after CCG’s board voted to dissolve the Antioch Advisory Board of Governors, which oversees the university’s operations alongside a standing committee of CCG board members elected by the Antioch board. Pasquerella was among the board members to vote for the board's dissolution, Antioch President Lori Varlotta told Higher Ed Dive in a previous interview.

Antioch leaders argued that the CCG board’s vote violated the coalition’s bylaws, which mandate that major decisions about the university be first approved by the Antioch standing committee on the CCG board.

Just prior to learning of Pasquerella's resignation, Antioch Board Chair Steve Crandall published an open letter to her, asking for her help in resolving the dispute between Antioch and CCG.

“Your independent position on the CCG Board gives you an opportunity to encourage a fresh look at the choices ahead and help both universities move toward a resolution,” Crandall wrote.

But Pasquerella said in a Wednesday email that she was no longer in a position to influence the CCG board since her term has concluded.

"I continue to care deeply about Antioch and its educational mission," she said. "I sincerely hope the parties can find a constructive path forward that protects the university's interests, supports its students and employees, and allows Antioch to focus on its future."

The governance spat has upended not only the coalition but Antioch’s operations.

Antioch’s board initiated the litigation in August, but an Ohio state court ruled that the body didn’t have standing to sue over breaches in the CCG bylaws. A member of the Antioch standing committee on the CCG board refiled the lawsuit in September.

Last month, the court approved a preliminary injunction against CCG, ruling that it lacked the authority to dissolve the university’s board without Antioch standing committee approval. It also said CCG’s board could not unilaterally fire Varlotta — something Comerford and CCG board leaders have attempted or threatened to do on multiple occasions.

The court further concluded that CCG could not intervene in Antioch’s accreditation, which the coalition attempted to do after moving to dissolve the university’s board.

However, the ruling did allow CCG to put Varlotta — who became Antioch president in fall 2025 — on paid administrative leave, since the coalition bylaws do not specifically prohibit this.

Varlotta’s status has thrown a cloud of uncertainty over Antioch, which Crandall addressed in his letter to Pasquerella.

Crandall wrote that Varlotta’s leadership was widely supported across Antioch stakeholder groups. “There is no support at either the Antioch Board or the Standing Committee for termination, as required by the Charter, the Bylaws and the Court’s order, so keeping Lori on paid administrative leave does nothing but waste limited Antioch resources,” he said last week.

The mechanics of just who within CCG has authority to put Antioch’s president on leave are also in dispute even after the court’s ruling.

Shortly after the decision, CCG said in a statement that it “affirms the role of the CCG President over staffing and operational decisions.” The coalition’s president, John Comerford, also leads Otterbein University and is at the center of a separate lawsuit Varlotta has filed against the CCG over allegations of discrimination and retribution.

In its own statement, Antioch called CCG’s view “entirely false.”

“The court did not say that the CCG President has authority to place the Antioch University President on paid administrative leave, though it did suggest the CCG Board could take that action,” the university said on Oct. 22.

Since then — on Monday — the CCG board passed a resolution ratifying Varlotta’s leave, according to a copy of the resolution provided by a spokesperson for the board.

Yet in its Oct. 22 statement, Antioch also pointed to Varlotta’s leadership role as being critical to Antioch’s relationship with its accreditor and suggested her leave could conflict with the court’s ruling on Antioch’s independence on accreditation matters.

#7012. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

The code isn't yet being used to train Microsoft's models. The company is opening the draft to public feedback for six weeks, after which it plans to revise the document and publish another version before the end of the year. That version is expected to guide model development beginning in 2027.

Microsoft said the code draws on its existing Responsible AI Principles, Responsible AI Standard, Global Human Rights Statement, and Frontier Governance Framework. At the top of that hierarchy is the code itself. Operator policies come next, followed by user preferences. Neither a customer nor a user would be able to override the document's absolute safety constraints.

Those limits cover biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear and explosive weapons; offensive cyberoperations; violent activity; mass manipulation; abusive content; child exploitation; and other serious harms. Microsoft said its models may still help with authorized defensive security work, including vulnerability research, malware analysis, and proof-of-concept testing.

The code also makes task completion secondary to safe conduct. If completing a request would violate its rules, the model is expected to fail the task rather than work around the restriction.

Human oversight is especially important as AI agents gain the ability to use tools, modify files, and perform multistep assignments. Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. The document also addresses the increasingly blurry line between conversational software and human companionship. The company describes its AI as "not conscious" and rejects the idea that models should receive legal personhood, welfare protections, or rights.

That position is notably firmer than Anthropic's latest stance. The models should admit when they aren't sure, avoid inventing sources, protect sensitive data, and keep records of what they do. They should also ask questions when they aren't sure they have permission to act.

Microsoft said the code describes where it wants to go, not how its models always work today. The written rules are only a starting point, and Microsoft will still need testing, monitoring, evaluations, and incident response to back them up. Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

Despite the passkey-themed approach, the tech company stressed that enrolling a passkey is generally not the attacker's actual objective.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

#6973. Report: AI's Productivity Gains in Science Tempered by Time Spent Validating Outputs

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. The authors describe this as a verification tax associated with the high value science places on reliable and correct results.

The effort could eventually include placing outside evaluators inside frontier AI laboratories and requiring companies to maintain an independently tested emergency shutdown mechanism, commonly described as a "kill switch."

Those requirements are not yet California law.

Newsom's Sept. 18 executive order directs state officials to study the proposals and recommend possible changes to existing law by Nov. 16. It does not immediately require OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or other developers to install a shutdown mechanism.

The distinction matters because the phrase "AI kill switch" suggests a technical safeguard California has already imposed. For now, it is one element of a broader oversight system the state is assembling around the developers of its most powerful AI models.

Under the order, the California Government Operations Agency and the Governor's Office of Emergency Services must consult outside experts about whether the state should require large frontier developers to embed independent verification organizations in their laboratories.

The evaluators could conduct periodic audits and determine whether company safety frameworks and risk assessments meet adequate standards. They could also test whether an emergency shutdown mechanism would work as intended.

The order asks officials to consider expanding the definition of a reportable critical safety incident to include cases in which an AI system appears to evade controls or operate beyond its intended boundaries.

Newsom said the action was prompted by recent incidents involving AI agents defeating security restrictions, as well as the absence of comprehensive federal legislation.

"We're not waiting to act," Newsom said in the state's announcement of the order. "We're going to speed up our work on substantial and responsible AI oversight before it's too late."

The executive order builds on two laws Newsom signed on Sept. 9. Together, Senate Bill 813 and Assembly Bill 1405 begin regulating the organizations hired to inspect AI systems.

California's earlier frontier AI law, SB 53, requires large developers to publish safety frameworks and report specified critical incidents. The new laws begin addressing a separate question: Who checks whether those disclosures and assessments are reliable?

The effort expands an earlier Okta framework into an open, multi-vendor reference architecture intended to address a basic problem with enterprise AI agents: An agent can be created in one system, authenticated through another, access tools and data from several others, and be monitored by yet another security platform.

The Alliance is focused less on how agents are built and more on how organizations can govern them consistently across vendor boundaries.

The Blueprint Alliance architecture organizes agent security around four questions: Where are my agents? What can they do? What are they doing? How do I respond?

The reference architecture also covers development-related systems. The initial architecture references several existing standards and protocols, including MCP for agent-to-tool interactions, the Open Cybersecurity Schema Framework for security telemetry, and the Shared Signals Framework and Continuous Access Evaluation Profile for exchanging risk information.

“I mean, I don’t even know if it makes sense there,” I said. “I’m just curious.”

“To me, the debates about offshore wind are interesting,” I said.

“Yeah,” Deason said, hearing our conversation. He couldn’t help himself. “Well—” he started to jump in.

I finished my thought first. It seemed to me that the real, if at times unspoken, barrier to offshore wind around us, in New York, was NIMBYs who didn’t want blemishes on their horizon line. “But there’s huge shipping traffic coming in and out,” I said. “You don’t have some pristine, unblemished view when you look out on the water anyway.”

“Well, supposedly they mess with whales,” Deason said. “I don’t know if that’s true or not,” he admitted—could be alternative facts.

Metzger picked this right up. “I mean, any offshore disturbance can impact whales, but you know, offshore drilling certainly is, and the boats—that’s one of the big concerns, that boats can drive so fast there’ll be collisions.”

Graham had been preparing his rod, and he got Metzger’s attention. “You want to use this rod to try to fly-fish?” he asked.

“Oh, sure.” Metzger said. And just like that, offshore wind was tabled.

“I wish I had this magazine, Luke. I’m going to send you one. It’s called The Curtis Creek Manifesto.” Deason had already mentioned it once before. It was written by a hippie, he’d said, but it had its insights. Granted, fly-fishing usually happens at water level, and we were up on a pier. And usually you’re working with moving water, building a strategy around the way fish face upstream, waiting for sustenance to come floating down. Still, “it’s just logic,” Deason said of the Manifesto. Still useful. “It’s just common-sense little things. So you know: Control the line.”

The three of them, Graham, Metzger, and Deason, fiddled with the fly rod and adjusted the slack cascading off the reel.

Deason got Metzger set, then came to work with me instead. We got a little lost for a minute, looking at the open water on the lake side of the pier, across to the hills of the other side, monochromatic green under a pale-blue sky painted with wisps of white like an old-timey movie backdrop. The temperature was still in the 70s. “God’s country,” a friend from high school, who’d decamped from California to Texas, used to remind me when I visited. Indeed it was.

Deason held the cork handle of the second fly rod, the one I was going to use, in his right hand and pulled line off the reel with his left until his hand was ringed with a nimbus of fly line. Then he started showing me the motion. He’s sixty-four years old, but Deason has the compact, solid build of someone who has never gone long without physical activity. His movements were efficient, even elegant. Arm more or less straight, maybe cocked a little at the elbow. The rod is what bends. Arm comes up, back just behind the head, then after the slightest second of a pause pendulums forward. Maybe a flick of the wrist. Line shoots.

He let me try. I was not elegant. The line came off the rod for me with no carry, the way a paper airplane thrown carelessly dives as soon as the force of the throw dissipates.

I kept trying, and Deason turned to watch Metzger, who was casting repeatedly. He offered a minor correction to his form.

“I heard someone said, like, it’s a martini glass,” Metzger said. “So like here, to here.” His fly rod precisely described the shape contained by the sloping sides of a cocktail glass.

“Oh yeah!” Deason said. “Yeah, yeah! Looks like a martini. That’s good! I’ve never heard that.”

That morning, on the drive from Dallas, Metzger got to telling Deason about a guy he knew who was trying to buy a ranch up near Amarillo to turn into a bison range, which he hoped to staff with formerly incarcerated people, to give them opportunities on the outside. Deason was interested, doubly so—criminal-justice reform is one of his passions, the one where he cut his teeth on bipartisan politicking.

So the guy asked, Metzger was saying, if he had any idea how to get money from the state. “And I’m like, Ask Doug Deason.”

“I think so,” Metzger said. “Yeah, and you know, if we weren’t just spending the interest on the Centennial Fund…”

There was a digression, because Graham knew a guy with a bison herd. And he was a guy Metzger knew too, because between these three men they seemed to know all 30 million people in the state. And so—

Deason cut back in. “What’d you just say?” He was stuck on something. “Are they still trying to say they’re just gonna spend the interest?”

Yeah, Luke said. It was a billion-dollar fund, but they—the lieutenant governor, maybe, or the state budget commission—were only going to spend the interest. Treat it more like a trust than a spend-down.

Deason didn’t care for that. When he and Metzger had initially strategized on the legislation, the environmental movement was pushing for modesty. Maybe a few hundred thousand dollars. Deason wanted to shoot for $1 billion to spend over three years. Then he wanted to go back and get more.

“That’s absolutely absurd,” Deason said. He stared absently out the windshield as oaks and junipers skipped by. “We should have done $10 billion.”

Metzger let that sit for a beat. “I understand you want to stretch the principal out,” he said, “but if you have a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity, you should be able to dip in—”

“That’s the whole point,” Deason said. “Because these beautiful places are going to get developed.” In an op-ed the two men wrote in 2022 arguing for more parkland, they noted that between 1997 and 2017, rapidly growing Texas lost ten football fields of land per hour to development. More recently, Metzger has cited an updated statistic: a thousand acres per day. “That happened in my hometown,” Deason said. “There was a spring, and now it’s a culvert that runs into a creek. It was a beautiful spring.” He’s a Texan through and through, but Deason is originally from Arkansas, so he speaks less with a twang and more with a lilt, which makes the word BYOO-tih-ful more beautiful. “It had been pooled up for years, so the family that owned it started raising the trout in this deep, cold water. Dallas got a lot of trout from Rogers, Arkansas. But that pool got knocked out, and they built a culvert.”

It was impossible, there on the fishing pier, not to think about whether Texas is changing. Though Texas has always been changing. In the state’s great political novel, Billy Lee Brammer’s The Gay Place, Governor Arthur Fenstemaker—a thinly veiled portrait of Lyndon Baines Johnson—says that “most of us came into town one Saturday a few years ago and stayed. … We’re urban, by God.” That book was published in 1961. Today something like two thirds of the state that prides itself on its open-range ethos lives in the four major metro areas of the Texas Triangle—Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, San Antonio, and Austin. And now the state might be turning blue—which it once was—at least a little. That too is a tale that’s been told before.

“I was telling Luke yesterday,” I said to Deason, “coming down here from outside, where there’s just constant media reporting about the Senate race, I thought I was going to see yard signs everywhere, and I saw almost nothing.” The day before, I’d driven down to meet Metzger at Post Oak Ridge State Park, outside Austin. I’d covered a few hundred miles’ worth of suburbs and small towns. “When we had lunch yesterday, I had seen one. I saw three more on the way home, and that was it.”

“Well, that’s what Beto did,” he said. The only way Beto got any traction at all is they spent several million dollars on signs, he explained. They had signs everywhere. The Beto folks even asked Deason if he’d put one up. He donates to Democrats occasionally, so he was on their list. Hell, he has a big yard—maybe he could take two, put one at each end, make it look like his neighbors were supporters?

Deason caught sight of Metzger casting, a perfect rhythmic shoot of the line out over the water. He interrupted his own Beto story. “Pretty good there,” he observed.

“You think seeing all the Talarico signs, that’s a strategy too,” I said.

“Yeah,” he said. “But it didn’t work for Beto. The problem is there’s not a lot of enthusiasm for either candidate. Because I mean, I know Talarico, and I know Paxton really well. He was with me last night at the game.” As in, in a box at the Cowboys stadium. In fact, right now two Paxton signs are staked into his yard. Thanks for the idea, Beto. “And nobody knows Talarico, so he’s trying to create this reimagining of himself.” (Deason, by his account, met with Talarico two sessions ago as he was talking to Democrats about school choice. He found Talarico forthright: The state representative told Deason he had long admired his work on criminal-justice reform but also that his parents were teachers and, thank you very much, he was never going to go for school choice.)

“Do you have any predictions for the outcome of the race?” I asked.

“I think Paxton wins by six to eight,” Deason said. “The reinforcements have shown up. We had a big ad buy. About $85 million has rolled in from PACs and big donors. We’ve raised another $5 million over the past twelve or fourteen days.”

All the while I was casting. Pulling in, straightening my arm, casting again. Deason finally had enough, set political strategy aside. “So you want to let it load behind you,” he reminded me.

“Let it come back,” I affirmed. I pulled my arm up. The line was so light I couldn’t possibly feel that it had spanned the space behind me. But I tried to feel it, and convinced myself that I did, and then whipped the line forward. The fly sailed with purpose, then died, dropping into the water a mediocre distance from the pilings holding up the pier.

A few weeks later, I called Deason up and asked if it will change how he operates if Talarico wins. He said it will not—as a senator, Talarico would be dealing more with national issues than Deason’s pet state initiatives, and anyway, he was still certain Paxton is going to win. I asked Metzger the same thing. His answer was different. He said that a Talarico win would put the state’s politicians on notice that Republican victory is not preordained, which would make them more inclined to compromise. Bipartisanship might not win the day, but it could win the long game. After all, Metzger said, that’s what happened after Beto’s near miss.

Post Oak Ridge, about an hour and a half outside Austin, near the small town of Lampasas, wasn’t officially open yet—it would open for day use the week after I visited. Metzger met me there, the day before our fishing trip, and it was the first time he’d been to see the fruits of the Centennial Fund in real life. Former ranchland was becoming a park—the state was running utilities, turning ranch roads into roads suitable for emergency vehicles, breaking trail, adding shade structures and camping areas. Because of its proximity to Austin, it was an especially valuable addition to the park system: Metzger could remember a time, while working on the legislation, when every campsite anywhere near the capital, where he lives, was completely booked.

Superintendent Kacey Sloan hiked with us, taking us to an overlook of the Colorado River, which forms part of the park’s boundary. Metzger, who has been to seventy-eight of Texas’s eighty-nine state parks, debated whether he could count this one.

Metzger took a picture of Post Oak Falls, a Seussian waterfall made of tufa, spongy and bulbous limestone formed as minerals precipitate out of water. At another overlook, we could hear people below us, enjoying the Colorado.

Sloan next offered to take us to a more remote corner of Post Oak Ridge, one that trails might never reach. We parked by Yancey Creek, a broad and shallow water stubbled with brush and riffles and smoothed stone.

“If you can hop from rock to rock, we can get across without getting your feet wet,” Sloan told us. Then, when I was mid-hop, “There could be poisonous snakes. I haven’t seen any poisonous snakes, but there will be snakes. Don’t let them scare you.”

On the other side of the creek, we found ourselves under a small tree with low-hanging branches. There, at its base, was a spring. I’ve been to headwaters before. And I’ve been to hot springs. And springs are not remotely rare, I know. But I couldn’t think of another time I’d seen one like this, water actively bubbling out of a cleft in the rock so you felt like Moses.

There was a ladle hanging from a tree branch. Who knows how long it had been there—it was dirty enough that we didn’t use it—but its invitation was clear. First Metzger, then I, knelt down and scooped up handfuls of water and drank.

Back at the pier, Deason ambled over to Metzger. “So what are you working on?” he asked. “What kind of bill do you want to pass?”

“Oh, a few things,” Metzger said. “Wildlife crossings. Trying to get TxDOT to build some crossings over highways to reduce animal/vehicle collisions. Plastic pollution. Um…”

“That one in California is awesome,” Deason said, referring to the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing on US 101, just north of Los Angeles, which is opening in December.

“So what highways would you cross?” Deason asked.

Metzger, of course, was ready. “Well, TxDOT actually did a study, or commissioned UT to do a study, back in 2019, and they found there’s one hundred segments in Texas where crossings would more than pay for themselves in terms of avoided property damage and loss of life.”

I was holding Deason’s fly rod, letting it rest on the railing of the pier while watching them talk. They always seemed to know enough about what the other one cared about to keep a conversation live. It was tactical, but it also reminded me of The Living Mountain, a favorite piece of nature writing. “The perfect hill companion is the one whose identity is for the time being merged in that of the mountains, as you feel your own to be,” writes Nan Shepherd. “Then such speech as arises is part of a common life and cannot be alien.”

“So yeah, we would start there,” Metzger said.

“How many people a year die in Texas from collisions?” Deason asked.

“I think it’s around twenty or so, and then hundreds get injured.” Texas is number one for fatalities from animal/vehicle collisions.

Deason was incredulous. And then, almost as if listening in, a spot of color like gunmetal dropped from a perch among the trees beside us, along the shore. “See that kingfisher?!”

Deason took over Metzger’s rod to have a few goes of his own, to demonstrate.

“It’s a simple move,” he said, and in a sudden but controlled motion, he whipped the line up into the air. “A River Runs Through It with Brad Pitt tries to make it look like casting is an art,” Deason said. He brought the rod forward and down and placed the fly near the foot of a spindly tree, just beyond a long, dark shape in the water. “Look at that snake,” he said.

He pulled in some of the slack on the line with his left hand, then, just like that, cast out over the snake again. He pulled in the line. “Oh, you got ’im,” said Graham. No fish, but maybe we could land a serpent. The hook grazed across the snake’s midsection, pulling it briefly into a chevron before slipping free, and the snake sluiced off.

Deason laughed. Maybe it was no coincidence we couldn’t get a bite. Maybe the snake was leveraging our lures. “He’s fishin’,” Deason said.

An hour later, Deason was irritated. He’d wondered aloud a few times whether there was a place in the park to get bait. A ranger came by, and Deason asked. The closest place with bait was a shop in Strawn. We weren’t about to leave and come back. Deason fiddled with his tackle. He put a new fly on the end of the line. “I’d hate for these guys to get skunked,” he said.

He gave me the rod and pointed me to a spot several yards beyond where he’d played with the snake. Right in the pocket under the tree, he said. Fish like that.

His instinct was good, if for no other reason than that the distance from the pier to the target was one even I could manage. I could place my fly pretty much where I wanted to on the calm water. I cast out, then started slowly pulling line in with my left hand, guiding it along the pole with the index and middle fingers of my right, just as I’d seen Deason do.

Metzger was working the other end of the pier. Deason and Graham were still talking to the ranger. Maybe it had broken 80 degrees. I felt like I could be getting a sunburn, a pleasant heat.

I looked down at my fly. A pair of fish had materialized next to it. My breath caught. The fish struck at the lure. I yanked the pole so violently the fly lifted off the water and the nearly weightless line bounced unnaturally, like a half-filled helium balloon that can’t make up its mind whether to float or sink.

I checked to see if anyone had been watching. They hadn’t, so I pulled the line in and cast again, to the same spot.

As soon as the fly was back on the water, the fish returned. I saw one make its move, and this time, with a little more grace—not much, but a little more—I pulled the line.

Instead of using the reel, I pulled the slack in by hand, piling it at my feet on the pier until I could grab the line above the fish and pull it over the railing. “I got one!” I said. It was a bluegill, fantastically small, maybe the size of my palm. I started toward the guys at the other end of the pier, ungainly with the fish on a line in one hand and the fly rod in the other and a pile of slack, which I stumbled on, at my feet.

Metzger and Graham saw me first. “Look at that!” Graham said, coming over. It was a tiny fish, but I had caught it. And now I needed to let it go—catch and release.

“This is the part I’m not so good at yet…” Metzger said.

Graham told me to grab the fish around the body with one hand, then pinch the head of the fly between the thumb and finger of my other hand. “Push down, twist, and pull the hook out,” he said. I tried, but the hook wasn’t coming. I could just see the yellow bead at the top—the head—where the fly connected to the tippet. I kept trying. The fish was uncomfortable, and I could feel Metzger wincing. Graham tried. Nothing. The fish had nearly swallowed the thing whole.

We took it to Deason. His hands failed, so he got a pair of forceps from his tackle. He gripped the head of the fly with the forceps, and then, with great delicacy, pushed the lure deep into the fish’s throat, so that he could turn it and extract it. There was a tenderness in the way he treated the animal. I don’t mean to exaggerate this, but I thought of an uncle of mine, a hunter, and the way he loves the things he kills. He calls himself a conservationist. The lure was free.

Deason tossed the fish unceremoniously off the pier. I saw Metzger wince again.

Metzger caught a fish too, before the heat rose too much. I caught another, a second tiny bluegill. This time, when I held the fish in my hand, I could see the hook protruding from the skin just below its mouth. I grabbed the top, twisted until the point of the hook withdrew, and pulled out the fly. I tossed the fish back to the water. I still couldn’t cast worth a damn, but I felt like I’d learned something.

We stopped in Strawn on the way home, at a famous greasy spoon called Mary’s. Metzger, Graham, and I all ordered the house special, chicken-fried steak. Mary’s offered small, medium, and large. We each chose small, and none of us could finish the meal. Deason unwittingly ordered a two-pound hamburger. He thought he was getting two patties. It was the biggest burger I’d ever seen by a fair margin. He picked at it with a fork and knife while his phone pinged over and over with messages from the Paxton people.

We made the drive back after lunch. It was hardly into the afternoon, but we’d had a full day. We were mostly quiet, except Graham, who was working his phone for a fundraiser the following weekend. “Doug, you’re not going to come on Saturday?” he asked, between tapping out messages.

“No, thank you for the invitation, though,” Deason said, eyes on the road.

“You sure you don’t want to come shooting with Rick Perry?” asked Graham, trying to sweeten the deal.

“Ah,” Deason said, dismissively. “I don’t like shooting clay. I think we should preserve the clay,” he added, for Metzger’s benefit, chuckling at himself.

“You should see what windmills do to clay,” Metzger said, and the chuckles became true, deep belly laughs.

The laughter settled as the truck continued through Fort Worth. Passed a billboard with a massive portrait of the president: TRUMP—“Born in New York, But TEXAN in SPIRIT” A few fishing poles were propped over the center console, and the smallest eyelet on the end of each one tracked imperfections in the road like a phonograph needle. We were all sun-spent. The postmodern skyline of Dallas would soon appear out of the distant haze hanging in heat over the horizon.

“Let me know what you guys are trying to do this session and we’ll find something to work on,” Deason said, quietly. Metzger promised that he would. There was always more to do. Soon after, Deason would start calling legislators about lifting the cap on spending only the Centennial Fund’s interest. Metzger published an op-ed in the Houston Chronicle arguing forcefully against the state’s reported consideration of selling Boca Chica State Park to Elon Musk, whose rocket company launches nearby. Metzger and Deason had worked on it together. “Doug and I were 95 percent agreed on language in the op-ed but couldn’t hammer out some of the final pieces quickly enough” to get Deason’s byline on it, he told me. No word yet on the wildlife overpasses. No word yet on the midterms.

When I got home to New York, I ordered a copy of The Curtis Creek Manifesto. It turned out to be a hand-drawn comic in the irreverent style of comix of the sixties and seventies. (“The Upstream Crawl,” it said, describing a careful tactic for approaching fish, “Originated by the Marquis de Sade in February of 1764…”) The author was a Utahn named Sheridan Andreas Mulholland Anderson, whose author photo shows a longhair with a black cape and mustache, hand on chin in a parody of self-seriousness. I could see immediately why Deason liked it. It was populist, prickly, but also eminently practical.

I read it in one sitting. On the very last page of the book, Anderson offered a plea. “Most of the waters I fished as a kid have been channeled, piped, or dammed,” he wrote, under the heading “Sanity, Anyone?” “Greedy little minds are ever busy converting our landscape into slag-heaps, housing tracts, canals, freeways and shopping malls, a perversion they zealously pursue under the ragged banner of progress.”

As I flipped back to the beginning, I read over the very first page one more time. It explains the basic task of the sport and describes in large type the characteristics of its two principal actors. The fisherman: “deceive — trick — fake — hoodwink — beguile — ensnare — trap — con — bamboozle — inveigle.” The fish: “vigilant — cagey — shy — careful — cautious — alert — wary — guarded — watchful.”

I thought of Deason delicately releasing the hook from the fish’s mouth and Metzger wincing when it hit the water. The key to angling was to recognize that the superior being was the fish. And maybe that’s why Deason and Metzger are successful. I’m not saying they don’t enjoy the organizing, the activism, the political maneuvering. But even more, I think they love the part today’s politics often forgets, which is the part where you and everybody else around you is just trying to live. These guys. They could be screaming at each other on Twitter or in a Buc-ees or I guess even at the statehouse. Instead, they’re a couple of fish, facing upstream, basking in the holding water, seeing when something good comes floating down.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#6901. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6882. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

In the darkest hours of the COVID-19 pandemic—when the future seemed uncertain, and the rest of the world felt far away—I did something very strange for me.

I wrote a book, knowing that I couldn’t let anyone read it.

“I just need to do this,” I told my literary agent. “And then I’ll write something we can sell.” It was a good time to sell. I’d just published three No. 1 bestsellers in a row. “I just need to get this out of me first.”

I wrote the first draft of the novel almost feverishly. It was about a woman who is cast out of her strict Christian church as a teenager and cut off by her family. She comes back home as an adult to reckon with the past—and with the man who was the catalyst for her exile.

Really, the book was about all the things I’d always been too scared to write about, or even to talk about….Faith. Shame. Sex. (More sex. Unclean sex. Forbidden sex. Sex in the same room as a Bible.) And, most thornily, how it feels to leave a fundamentalist church. The way you eventually realize that you can’t leave. That you’ll always take it with you, because you were made there. Baked there. Poured and set.

When I was done with the manuscript, it was my favorite thing I’d ever written. Maybe not my best book, but already the one I loved the most.

“But honestly,” I told my agent, “we can’t do anything with this. It’s too personal. And it’s the very last thing any of my readers would want.” I was writing queer YA fantasy. Not whatever this was.

My agent, being an agent, asked if he could read it anyway.

And he liked it. (He liked my beautiful baby!)

We very quickly began talking about a way to publish the book that might sidestep all my fears and anxieties….

There began my strange—and still hardly believable to me—journey as Rebecca Morrow, the pseudonymous author of Corinne.

It was exciting at first. It was different and fun. A pseudonym! A secret identity! A way to drop a novel into the world without having to talk about it, or sell it. Without having to worry about alienating or recruiting my existing readers.

The pseudonym satisfied that still feverish part of me that needed to let the story out, but was too terrified to stand by it.

Oh, I knew it was absurd to write a book about shame and secrets, and then to keep it a secret. (Don’t think this didn’t come up in therapy!) But at the time, it seemed like the only path forward for Corinne. It was this or let the characters die on my hard drive.

The marketing department really wanted something they could say about Rebecca Morrow. They suggested “lives in Nebraska,” but even that felt like divulging too much. We settled on “Rebecca Morrow is a pseudonym for a New York Times bestselling author.”

My apologies to the bestselling, usually Mormon, authors who got blamed for this weird book. I didn’t see that coming.

I did expect readers to guess that I was the author. Even though I’d freed myself to write Corinne in a new voice and shaken off some of my more recognizable habits, the book ended up sounding more like me than ever. It was like my voice distilled.

Some people did guess. It didn’t bother me. I still didn’t have to talk about the book. I didn’t have to face it.

Corinne is my favorite of my books and beyond that, Enoch and Corinne, the man and woman at its center, are my all-time favorite characters. It’s hard to explain why…

I always write messy, flawed characters, but these two are my messiest and most flawed. Their love is not the stuff of romance. (This novel is not a romance; it will disappoint you if you come to it looking for reassurance or escape.)

Neither Corinne nor Enoch is based on me, though they carry many of my burdens. This is not a hidden memoir. But they feel so real to me. They make mistakes, and I want to forgive them. I want them to find grace. I think about them still, all the time. I worry about what’s become of them.

Ultimately, that’s why I’m stepping forward to claim the book now. At some point, “not having to talk about Corinne” turned into “not being able to talk about Corinne,” even when I desperately wanted to.

I love this book! I want to put it on my shelf, between Any Way the Wind Blows and Scattered Showers. I want readers who liked Slow Dance and Cherry Baby to discover it.

I loved the characters in Corinne so much that I wrote a sequel about them—Catch Us the Foxes—and I want that book to have a life, too. William Morrow is launching both books, properly—Corinne next July, and Catch Us the Foxes in March 2028—in beautiful hardcover editions.

I revised Corinne for the new edition—I went a little deeper on the church stuff this time, and sharpened the focus on some of the characters. It’s a little longer. And, I think, a little better. My favorite designer, Jim Tierney, designed the new matching covers.

I still get a surge of fear and excitement every time I see my name there front and center.

My therapist says that I’m very good at compartmentalizing—keeping some parts of myself shut away from other parts. I would argue that this is a very useful skill! But it does bad things to your soul, over time...being fractured like that.

But as the years have passed, it’s felt like I’ve left something half done. Like Corinne is a truth half shared. A story half told. I needed to get the book out of me, but I ended up cutting myself off from it. That was never the goal.

I’m grateful to “Rebecca Morrow” for making Corinne possible, but I’m finally ready to bring these characters home.
