#7148. Broward schools ‘no longer the only game in town’ as enrollment drops

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Florida district is doubling down on student recruitment and retention by stepping up marketing and engagement efforts.

This is the second installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

When Valerie Wanza started as a teacher in Florida's Broward County Public Schools in 1992, the district was at the beginning of what would be explosive growth in student population over the next decade.

Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, Broward County Public Schools saw such an increase in enrollment that by 2004, more than 100 of its 315 schools were deemed "critically overcrowded."

Now, 34 years later as the district's chief strategy and innovation officer, Wanza is part of a team of administrators working to turn around an enrollment decline that began in the 2015-16 school year. The student population plummeted by 17.5% over the next decade and is forecast to continue decreasing through at least the 2030-31 school year, according to district data.

The district attributed the drop to various factors: lower birth rates leading to fewer children entering schools, rising housing costs that make it harder for families to live in the desirable area bordering the Atlantic Ocean, fewer immigrant students due to shifts in federal policies, and growing competition from private school choice options.

And with the area's school choice proliferation, Wanza said, Broward County Public Schools is "no longer the only game in town."

Now, to stay financially healthy, the district is taking a comprehensive approach that includes consolidating and closing some school campuses, cutting at least 1,000 staff positions, and implementing other measures to save money. It's also attempting to boost enrollment by vigorously recruiting new students through media campaigns, personalized phone calls, and hosting events to drum up interest in and excitement for the district's schools.

Broward is even turning to an outside vendor for help. Earlier this year, the district approved a one-year contract with student recruitment firm Caissa K12 to help connect with families who have left its public schools, determine their reasons for doing so, attempt to resolve those concerns and inform them about the district's benefits and schooling options.

The performance-based contract said Broward County Public Schools will pay Caissa K12 $850 per recruited student who enrolls in a district school, up to 176 students or a maximum payment of $150,000.

“If we aren’t real about, No. 1, recognizing what adjustments we need to make, and then be bold enough to make them, we’re going to become the Kmart … of the public schools,” Wanza said, referring to the once-dominant discount retailer that filed for bankruptcy in 2002 amid growing competition and other pressures.

The enrollment decline that Broward County Public Schools is experiencing mirrors the challenges faced by many other school districts across the country.

While falling birth rates and rising school choice are often to blame, the multitude of factors can be complex and specific to each region's ability to draw new residents with young families. Nationally, enrollment in traditional public schools is expected to drop from 54.7 million in 2021 to 49.4 million in 2035, according to a 2025 analysis from the Brookings Institution based on U.S. Census and other federal data.

On the other hand, some states are predicted to see increases in public school student populations between fall 2022 and fall 2031. Among those are Idaho (+11%), North Dakota (+7.4%) and Tennessee (+2%), according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Enrollment in Florida's public schools is projected to increase by 3% over the same time period.

From 2015-16 through 2025-26, Broward County Public Schools lost 17.5% of its student population, or 38,640 students. That translated to a loss of $352 million in per-pupil revenue over that 11-year span. In fiscal year 2026, which ended June 30, the district expected to show an $80 million shortfall because of continued enrollment loss.

The district has already closed seven schools since the 2025-26 school year and is exploring other closures, consolidations and grade configurations for the future. New data released this fall shows district enrollment dropped by another 10,272 for the 2026-27 school year.

Even so, with 315 schools educating 224,324 students, including at charter schools, Broward County Public Schools is still the 6th largest district in the U.S., according to its website.

Broward leaders are carefully analyzing data that can explain why families aren't enrolling their students in county schools. Joseph Beck, the district's director of demographics and enrollment planning, said his team is constantly coming up with ways to look at the data differently to better understand why students are coming or going.

For instance, the data shows that about 20,000 students, on average, withdraw from Broward County Public Schools annually. That number, which doesn't include graduating seniors, has been consistent over many years, Beck said.

The district also investigates how many new students enroll each year, a figure that can be affected by immigration policy changes. For instance, the district had 3,400 fewer newcomer students in 2025-26 than it did the previous year, Beck said.

The data also shows that 1,700 students enrolled in the 2024-25 school year transferred to a private school the following year using a Florida Family Empowerment Scholarship to help pay tuition.

At the state level, about 561,000 students participated in taxpayer-funded scholarship programs during the 2025-26 school year, according to a Florida Senate analysis. That same year, the state's public schools enrolled about 2.8 million students, state data shows.

Across Broward County, some 35,000 students participated in one of Florida's taxpayer-supported private school choice programs in the 2025-26 school year.

Beck said the data further shows that many of the county's private school choice participants weren't previously enrolled in the district's public schools. "They just completely bypass us altogether," Beck said.

He and other Broward County Public School leaders are aiming to reverse that.

Farrah Wilson, Broward County Public Schools' director of marketing and strategic communications, rapidly ticks off the efforts being made to court new students and their families. There are mailers, social media campaigns, TV ads, school tours, home visits and more.

As public schools across the country get more intentional and strategic about recruitment, they are taking a playbook from private schools. That includes hosting school tours, having "shadow days" for prospective students, and sending mailers to families encouraging them to add public schools to their list of options.

We have the best programs. We have the best teachers. We have the best facilities, athletics. We offer a comprehensive experience that many of our charter and private schools can't offer.

Broward County Public Schools' director of marketing and strategic communications

Broward County is home to 245 private schools serving about 54,000 students, or about 18% of students in the county, according to Private School Review, a site that provides information on K-12 private schools for families.

But competition for students can also come from nearby public school systems.

In a mailer that Broward sent out in mid-July, it targeted families living across the district's borders to the north in Palm Beach County and to the south in Miami-Dade County.

"This is just a way to let parents know that there are options, and if they are interested, they can go to one of our schools on the county line and participate and take one of the open seats," Wilson said.

The mailers led to one student from Palm Beach County and 17 students from Miami-Dade County enrolling in Broward County Public Schools, the district said.

While it might seem unfair to try to poach other districts' students, Broward administrators said it goes both ways. The School District of Palm Beach County has likewise attempted to recruit Broward students living near its border, Broward officials said.

The Palm Beach system shares information about its schools through various channels, and marketing reaches families across the region, including those in neighboring counties like Broward, said Sean Cooley, chief of communications and enrollment for the Palm Beach district, in an Oct. 29 email. The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

Removing the wage and benefit requirements will prevent the elimination of 106,000 Head Start classroom slots.

The change is just one part of a broader, ongoing effort by the Trump administration to overhaul the Head Start program.

The National Head Start Association, a nonprofit organization that represents Head Start families, providers and educators, said in a Friday statement that underfunding is straining the program's ability to pay staff competitively while maintaining student enrollment levels.

NHSA also said that removing federal wage and benefit requirements "does not change competitive pressures or eliminate the staffing shortages that continue to limit some programs' ability to open classrooms and serve more eligible children and families."

NHSA Executive Director Yasmina Vinci said in a statement, "A strong Head Start program depends on a stable, qualified workforce, and programs must have the resources necessary to recruit, retain, and support the professionals who serve children and families every day."

In a statement responding to the final rule, the National Association for the Education of Young Children said, "HHS frames this as a choice between paying educators and serving children, but a funded slot without a qualified teacher serves no one."

The final rule is to take effect 60 days after publication.

#7056. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."
