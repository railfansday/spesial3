#7108. Senate committee’s FY27 plan gives Education Department 19% boost

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Funding would increase for special education and stay level for Title I, according to a Senate Appropriations Committee proposal.

Key K-12 programs would be level-funded or slightly boosted for fiscal year 2027 under a budget plan proposed by the Republican-led Senate Appropriations Committee as the deadline looms for enacting a funding bill.

Appropriations for programs such as Title I, Part A for low-income school communities ($18.4 billion), Title III for English learners ($890 million) and safe schools and citizenship education ($431 million) would stay the same as FY26 appropriations. Funding for the Office for Civil Rights would remain at $140 million, according to the spending plan.

The proposal recommends increases for several other programs, such as an additional $50 million for special education education programs ($15.5 billion), $60 million more for charter school funding ($500 million), and $5 million more for 21st Century Community Learning Centers, which help fund after-school and summer activities ($1.3 billion).

Overall, the Senate Appropriations committee’s budget proposal recommends $93.9 billion for the Education Department in FY27, a 19% increase over FY26 appropriations.

The FY27 proposal "makes meaningful investments" in programs such as childcare and education, said Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., in a Monday statement. Capito is chair of the Senate Appropriations Labor, Health and Human Services, Education and Related Agencies Subcommittee.

But the full committee's vice chair, Sen. Patty Murray, D-Wash., said in a Monday statement that the larger Labor, Health and Human Services, Education and Related Agencies proposed funding bill "fails to rein in this administration — to stop the dismantlement of the Department of Education, prevent [President Donald] Trump from politicizing every federal grant, and so much more. We need to invest way more to help students thrive, protect people’s health, and help families afford the basics, like child care and their heating bill.”

A separate budget proposal for the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services recommends raising funding for Head Start early childhood education programs by $50 million for a total of $12.4 billion.

FY27 began Oct. 1, but before the deadline, Congress enacted a continuing resolution to delay decisions for an overall final federal budget until Dec. 11. By that date, Congress will either need to finalize the FY27 appropriations or enact another continuing resolution. If lawmakers do neither, there could be a government shutdown.

In June, the House Appropriations Committee advanced its FY27 spending plan that recommended an overall allocation of $71 billion, or a 10% cut, to the Education Department. That proposal included a $2 billion cut for Title I funding, a $46 million boost for special education programs and the elimination of the Office of English Language Acquisition.

Both the House and Senate committees' bills differ from Trump's proposed budget, which sought to cut Education Department spending by 3% to $76.5 billion by consolidating or eliminating several programs, such as English learner supports and Teacher Quality Partnership grants.

The Trump administration's spending proposal also recommended the elimination and consolidation of several Individuals with Disabilities Education Act national programs like teacher training and technical assistance, but that recommendation is not reflected in the House or Senate committees' budget plans.

The paths for the House and Senate committees' spending bills are unclear given the advancing fiscal deadline and partisan politics at play, said Tara Thomas, senior government affairs manager at AASA, The School Superintendents Association, in a Nov. 17 post on AASA’s website.

Starting the first week of January, Democrats will be the majority party in both the House and Senate and will take over key committee leadership roles.

#7040. 7 Gilded Age Homes Where You Can Live Like the Astors

In honor of The Gilded Age’s fourth season, here are the period mansions-turned-hotels you can stay in right now.

Above, Castle Hill Inn in Newport, Rhode Island.

Gilded Age barons of industry constructed extravagant homes—year-round mansions and summer “cottages”—that were more palaces to their wealth than practical residences. Notable families like the Vanderbilts, Carnegies, and Astors once had houses dotted across New York City and throughout fashionable summer retreats like Newport, Rhode Island. Many of these elaborate structures have since fallen to the ravages of time, with the staggering costs of upkeep rendering their use as private residences all but obsolete. Yet, though some of these private homes have disappeared, our curiosity about them has only increased. Since HBO’s hit show The Gilded Age debuted, interest in visiting the era’s architectural marvels has surged, and in Newport, which is home to one of the country’s largest concentrations of Gilded Age mansions, there’s been a steady rise in visitors touring the town’s mansions specifically because of the show.

Visiting the opulent, and often wildly impractical, mansions that inspired the fictional lives of The Gilded Age’s characters has its charms, but rather than just taking a tour, why not spend the night? Up and down the East Coast, a handful of Gilded Age mansions are preserved not as museums, but as hotels—inviting curious travelers to do more than just visit and admire the history, but to sleep within the jewel boxes of their walls.

Castle Hill Inn is a different kind of Gilded Age mansion, one that turns to timber and antiquities over marble and gilding. The home’s original owner, Alexander Agassiz, was a Harvard professor of natural sciences who made his fortune in copper mining. With his newfound wealth, Agassiz built his summer home—Castle Hill—on a relatively isolated parcel of forty acres, a fifteen minute drive away from the pomp and pageantry of the Vanderbilt’s side of town. He used the home as a summer research lab, hosting students in its many rooms and running the lab in what is today the parlor, while also entertaining a who’s who of literary luminaries, including Edith Wharton and Mark Twain.

Today, Castle Hill Inn is one of the most famous places to stay in Newport, not only for its luxurious rooms, afternoon tea, and award-winning restaurant, but also for its sweeping ocean-front lawn and seaside views. Thorton Wilder lived in the turret suite—Agassiz’s former observatory and today the hotel’s most beautiful room—while writing his last book, and Grace Kelly lived in one of the beach cottages during the 1955 filming of “High Society.” Stopping by Castle Hill Inn for a drink and taking a short walk to its still in-use lighthouse remains a summer essential in Newport, but there is nothing quite like staying the night.

Hotel Chanler is, unlike Castle Hill, right in the thick of Newport’s Gilded Age neighborhood, and it’s clear from its location that it was built (to use a period expression) to keep up with the Joneses. The opulent mansion was constructed in 1873 as the summer “cottage” of New York Congressman John Winthrop Chanler and his wife Margaret Astor Ward. Chanler hosted an elaborate range of guests at his summer home, including President Theodore Roosevelt and poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. The home, which Chanler called “Cliff Lawn,” was the very first mansion to be built on the Cliff Walk, now a public walkway with views of these historic homes. Over the years, the mansion went from being a private residence to a museum and later a girl’s school. It wasn’t until 2003 that it opened as the twenty-room Chanler Hotel. Today, it’s ornately decorated in various period-specific styles such as Regency, Louis XVI, Renaissance, and English Tudor. Last spring, The Chanler opened a more pared down restaurant, Remy’s Loose. Remy’s feels fit for Mr. Chanler and his guests, who we can imagine sprawling out across the chenille, fringed edged slipper chairs, and enjoying the just fussy-enough French menu.

The Fifth Avenue Hotel, which opened its doors in late 2023, has a multi-layered history rooted in the Gilded Age. In 1856, legendary hostess and doyenne of New York society, Charlotte Goodridge, commissioned the construction of a lavish private home and carriage house at the coveted corner of Fifth Avenue and 28th Street. Goodridge filled her mansion with treasures from around the globe and entertained lavishly. But following her death, Goodridge’s home met the same fate as much of the neighborhood and was torn down. However, rather than being replaced by a skyscraper like much of the surrounding area, Goodridge’s mansion was reborn in 1907 as a striking five story neo-Renaissance palazzo design by McKim, Mead & White, the architects behind many of the New York’s most famous early 20th century buildings including The Morgan Library and Museum, the Bowery Savings Bank, and the Metropolitan Club. More recently, a decades-long renovation overseen by Flâneur Hospitality, Martin Brudnizki Design Studio, and architect Perkins Eastman transformed the building into a 153-room hotel, with a restored limestone and brick façade, an added-in glass tower, a ballroom fit for a Goodridge party, and a European inspired restaurant, Café Carmellini. The hotel now has a Gilded Age-inspired look, complete with polished marble floors, grand arched windows, rich color palettes, and art and curiosities from around the world.

Hotel Bardo may have opened in 2024, but its history extends much further back. It was built in 1888 as a private home for Lewis Kayton, a businessman from Baltimore who settled in Savannah. Kayton tapped architects Alfred Eichberg and Hyman Witcover to create his brick and terracotta mansion right on the city’s central Forsyth Park. In the nearly 140 years of its existence, the Romanesque Revival building has served as a private home, a funeral home, and an inn before becoming Hotel Bardo. The original Lewis Kayton home now functions as the hotel’s coastal Italian restaurant, Saint Bibiana. Though it remains true to its original bones, the space also feels decidedly modern, thanks to its playful touches and large-scale, panoramic paintings. A rear addition houses 149 guest rooms that feel very contemporary with their rattan accents and hues of pink, nude, and green. A large pool, glass roofed terrace, and a brand-new spa, which feels more like a wellness retreat, further bring the home into the present day.

Today, it’s the only hotel on the eighteen-mile-long Cumberland Island, and remains a highly coveted escape thanks to its 200-acre property filled with live oaks, marshland, and surroundings complete with wild horses and pristine white sand beaches. The Carnegie family still owns the property, making the luxurious hotel still feel a bit like a private home. The result was Glenmere, a hilltop mansion designed by leading Gilded Age architects Carrere and Hastings. Completed in 1911, the home drew heavily on the European style popular in that period, and Glenmere’s architects filled the home with pieces sourced from across France and Italy, including marble columns, fountains, hand carved mantlepieces, and a surplus of furniture. For the grounds, Goelet tapped Beatrix Farrand, largely considered to be America’s first female landscape architect, who created a sunken Italianate Garden that led to the house’s own lake, Glenmere Lake. Murals by artist Alden Twachtman adorned the center courtyard of the home and Italian-style iron balustrades and window grilles were specially designed and created by master blacksmith, Samuel Yellin. Goelet enjoyed his retreat for nearly 25 years, but after his death it went through many chapters, transforming into a hotel and then back into a private home. Glenmere was purchased in 2010 by Alan Stenberg and Daniel DeSimone, and underwent a multi-year renovation to turn it, once again, into a hotel. Palm Beach interior designer Scott Snyder breathed some new life into the property, and although today’s look feels more contemporary than historic, many of the rooms take their names from icons of the past, like the Vanderbilt Suite and the Goelet Suite. With just fifteen rooms and 150 acres, the hotel feels like a true private hideaway. And Glenmere’s sprawling spa makes use of enough marble to make even a Vanderbilt jealous.

After a total rehabilitation, the John Gilman house opened as a hotel in 2015. Interior designer Joszi Meskan was tapped to bring the property to life, reimagining it as the home of a well-traveled woman of the world, filled with exquisite treasures and sumptuous attention to detail with nine suites, eight guest rooms, a library, and conservatory. #6966. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Well, sort of, as Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios points out. “When we look back at certain interiors from the 2000s, there was such a commitment to achieving a particular look that every element in the room was chosen to reinforce it,” the Georgia designer says. “A home should feel like a collection of things you genuinely love, not as though you’ve walked onto a movie set designed to represent a particular style.”

When you cherry-pick trends and design details from the past, you’re able to create a home that honors the past without appearing stuck in the past. “The goal isn’t to make a home look like it belongs to another decade,” Vollrath says. “You want to bring back the beauty and character of those decorative details while creating something that feels authentic to both the house and its owners.”

Below, designers share trends from the past that the next generation of design lovers adore.

For older generations, a swivel chair might conjure memories of sitting in your family’s den, but designers promise these movin’, groovin’ seats have been revived for the 21st century. “Swivel barrel chairs defined early-2000s transitional interiors, usually in a bright flat polyester-blend upholstery,” explains Hallie Malitsky, principal designer of Hallie Rose Design in Brooklyn. “The version coming back today is a different experience entirely.”

Today’s iterations, she insists, are subtler. Cheap polyester has yielded to rich, tactile fabrics like wool, linen, and velvet. For a timely twist, she also encourages trading the fire engine red and butter yellow popular in the 2000s for burgundy and ochre. “Surround it with natural materials like wood, linen, and stone, and [your chair] will stop being the loudest thing in the room,” she recommends.

When Malitsky thinks of early aughts design, she immediately envisions the gigantic walnut media center that plagued many of her friends’ family rooms. “Dark wood cabinetry is a notorious culprit of bad 2000s design, but the wood wasn’t the issue,” she says. “It was the fact that there was so much of it.”

Now that dark wood tones are making a comeback, Malitsky says to use them sparingly. Two to three walnut or ebony pieces per room will make enough of a statement. And, no: Unlike the living rooms of yore, your furniture doesn’t have to match.

“What I love about their return is that we’re incorporating these pieces in a much more collected way,” shares Vollrath. “Rather than purchasing an entire matching furniture collection, we’re mixing periods and bringing in individual pieces that have character and history.”

The early aughts had lots of elaborate cabinetry, replete with corbels, substantial cornices, and beautifully detailed range hoods. As everyone—yes, even younger generations—continue to favor more traditional interiors, we’re seeing a renewed interest in ornamented cabinetry.

“There’s something really special about a kitchen that feels as though it could have been there for generations, or even reminds you of your grandmother’s kitchen,” Vollrath says. “Those decorative flourishes bring a sense of history, craftsmanship, and permanence that can be difficult to achieve with a more streamlined approach.”

“We’re seeing a broader interest in architectural detailing throughout the home, from wainscoting and wall paneling to ceiling beams and substantial moldings,” she adds. “Homeowners are becoming more interested in understanding these elements, considering them during renovations, and making them a priority in new construction.”

According to the Tennessee-based designer, the resurgence is a break from the picture-perfect kitchens older generations have pined over. “Gen-Z values practicality over perfection,” she explains. “I think it will show up in their interiors.” And why stop at appliances when you can bring that lived-in feel to the rest of the space? “They don’t hate clutter the way millennials do,” she adds. “We’ll see details like cookbooks in the kitchens and hall trees with tennis rackets on them.”

Though Scott hasn’t seen the Venetian Gold granite that ruled Y2K just yet, he’s noticing many homeowners are willing to get creative with their countertops.

“We've moved beyond using only Carrara marble in kitchens,” he explains.

Ditto for quartz, which some designers believe is on the outs. Instead, Scott says travertine is huge again and granite is poised to make the comeback. If you want to keep these materials from appearing dated, he encourages you to reach for stones with bolding veining and inclusions.

#6937. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6920. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

AI agents are moving from experiments into everyday enterprise environments, but the security controls around them are not keeping pace, according to enterprise identity security company SailPoint. In its latest Horizons of Identity Security report, SailPoint found that 79% of enterprises already have AI agents running in production, while just 2% have deployed identity security tools specifically designed to govern them.

That gap could become harder to ignore as organizations give agents more autonomy. Agents can increasingly access applications, sensitive data, and credentials, and in some cases take actions on a user's behalf, effectively creating a new class of identity that IT and security teams need to manage.

SailPoint, which develops identity security and access governance technology for enterprises, is responding with a collection of new identity security capabilities announced at its Navigate 2026 conference. The company is expanding its Agentic Fabric to discover "shadow AI" agents and provide runtime authorization and controls capable of immediately disabling an agent.

The company also introduced what it calls SailPoint Autonomous Agents, specialized AI agents designed to govern other agents. The security agents can monitor runtime activity, intercept threats, and adjust permissions while allowing legitimate agents to continue operating, SailPoint said.

The idea reflects one of the challenges created by increasingly autonomous AI systems: Traditional human approval processes may not be fast enough to govern software capable of taking large numbers of actions on its own.

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. Organizations can then revoke access, disable an identity or AI agent, assign an owner or automate enforcement.

Just-in-Time provisioning is another part of the strategy. Rather than giving people, service accounts, and AI agents permanent access, permissions can be granted when needed and subjected to conditions such as reauthentication or a business justification.

Capabilities across SailPoint's Agentic Fabric, Human Fabric, and Atlas platforms are beginning to roll out globally. A-ISPM and several other announced features are expected in the company's fourth quarter of fiscal 2027.

"Autonomous agents are no longer just experimental copilots; they are becoming the primary execution layer of modern enterprises," SailPoint President Matt Mills said.
