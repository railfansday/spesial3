#7137. How student showcases can extend project-based learning’s benefits

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools uses “genius hours” and student presentations to deepen students’ investment in the learning process.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

#7134. How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

#7117. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

#7063. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#7048. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6989. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. #6947. I Tried a Korean Lash Lift. Did It Really Live Up to the Hype?

The buzzy treatment promises lifted, fluttery lashes without extensions. Here are my results.

We may earn commission from links on this page, but we only recommend products we love. Promise.

Confession: I’ll try almost anything in the name of longer, more fluttery lashes. That’s especially true if the thing shortens my makeup routine and gets me out the door a little faster in the morning (read: lets me stay in bed even just a few minutes longer). So I was, of course, very excited when I discovered a beauty treatment that promised to accomplish both: the Korean lash lift.

I’d previously heard some not-so-great things about lash lifts. The chemical solution used during the process—typically ammonium thioglycolate (TGA)—comes with a risk of dryness, breakage, and damage, especially if the lift is performed incorrectly or too frequently. But Korean lash lifts have been gaining serious attention (Pinterest reports that searches for the aesthetic treatment are up more than 400 percent) as a potentially gentler alternative, since they can feature formulas and techniques designed to minimize stress on the lashes.

And as someone with stubbornly straight lashes, I was particularly intrigued. To me, nothing ruins a good makeup day quite like lashes that refuse to cooperate, and trust me, I’ve put in the work to find products that make mine behave. If you’re wondering, after testing dozens of eyelash curlers and mascaras, I’ve landed on my undefeated duo: the Kevyn Aucoin Eyelash Curler and Tarte Tartelette Tubing Mascara. But even those two can’t always convince my lashes to hold a curl all day.

So, when I learned that a Korean lash lift could potentially give me weeks of lifted, fluttery lashes, no curler required, I knew I had to try it. Now that I’ve lived with the results for two months—including through a vacation, a wedding, and an apartment move—I have a lot of thoughts.

So, first things first: A Korean lash lift isn’t the same thing as eyelash extensions. Rather than attaching individual fibers to your natural lashes, the treatment uses chemical solutions to reshape the lashes you already have to give them a lifted, curled appearance. It’s the same basic concept as getting a perm for a whole head of curls, but on a much smaller scale.

That technique is similar to traditional lash lifts; the biggest distinction typically comes down to the process and products used. For example, traditional lash lifts tend to use perming formulas with ammonium thioglycolate (TGA), whereas Korean ones usually use cysteamine hydrochloride. The latter chemical is often touted as a gentler or safer alternative, but the truth of the matter is that “we do not yet have sufficient high-quality comparative clinical studies demonstrating that they consistently produce fewer ocular complications than traditional thioglycolate-based lash lifts,” says ophthalmologist Sheri Rowen, MD.

Korean lash lifts are also glueless, meaning they use balm instead of glue to get them to stick to the shield that makes the lashes stand up and have that classic curl.

“Korean lash lifts are often designed with an emphasis on more controlled processing and conditioning of the lashes, which may reduce some of the dryness and brittleness associated with chemical treatments,” says Dr. Rowen. However, this doesn’t automatically mean it’s better or safer than any traditional lash lift; all that depends on how the perming chemicals are applied.

“My overall recommendation is that patients should not assume a lash lift is safer simply because it is labeled ‘Korean,’ ‘gentle,’ or ‘conditioning,’” says Dr. Rowen. “The most important considerations are the ingredients, proper application, careful protection of the ocular surface, and avoiding excessive or repeated chemical processing.”

Still, if the treatment is done well, you can expect a natural-looking lift that makes your existing lashes appear longer, more separated, and more defined without the need for extensions.

For my appointment, I visited Two of a Kind, a lash and brow studio in Santa Monica that offers lash lifts, specifically Korean and what their team calls “hybrid,” meaning they use techniques from both methods to customize the results. The entire treatment took about 40 minutes, although my lash technician mentioned that she’s particularly quick. If you’re planning an appointment, I’d budget closer to an hour or potentially longer, depending on your technician and the specific service.

It was my first time ever getting a lash lift, so I didn’t have anything to compare it to. But Two of a Kind’s founder Jordan Feise told me another difference between the traditional and Korean methods: The former uses one shield that shapes the lashes throughout the appointment. The latter uses two shields, always starting with a flat shield (to straighten the lashes and get closer to the root for more lift, Feise explains), then swapping in a curved shield (to lock in the shape and create a curl, she adds). By using two shields, it separates the softening step and the shaping step, which Feise says gives the technician more control over how your lashes ultimately look. “Using two shields during a lash lift allows us to customize the shape of the lashes during the treatment for a more lifted, natural-looking result,” says Feise. “Together, they give us more control over the final result, helping create a beautiful lift without over-curling the lashes.”

Here’s how the appointment went for me: I laid down and my lash tech cleansed my lashes, removing any makeup, product residue, and oil to prep them for treatment. Then, the flat shield was applied to my lid along the upper lash line. She also made sure to separate my lower lashes from my upper ones. Next, the lifting begins. The initial lift is done using balm and the flat shield, which is a thin, flat pad (instead of a curved rod you’d see with a traditional lash lift). This straightens and lifts the lashes. After processing, the tech switched to that traditional curved lift shield to shape and curl. More processing, then neutralizing the formula (to make sure my eyes/contacts wouldn’t sting after), and plenty of conditioning, and I was all set.

During the appointment, I did what any beauty editor would do: I asked approximately a million questions.

How long would my results last? Around two months, she told me. Could I wear mascara and eye shadow afterward? Absolutely, after the initial 24-hour aftercare period. What about getting my lashes wet? Also fine after 24 hours. And would I have to do anything special to maintain the results?

Not really. She recommended applying a lash conditioner a few times during the first couple of weeks to keep the hairs soft and supple. (Full disclosure: I was a bad patient and only remembered to use it once. I still had great results.)

Basically, after the first 24 hours, I could go back to my normal routine—showering, washing my face, wearing eye makeup, and all—without worrying about ruining my lashes. “Even if I sleep on my face?” I asked. Yep, even then.

I have to admit, I was skeptical. The idea that my lashes could stay perfectly curled through weeks of sleeping, showering, and generally living my life seemed a little too good to be true, honestly. But I was very excited to find out.

Spoiler alert: I was so, so wrong to doubt.

For two months straight, my lashes looked unbelievably long, lifted, and curled, yet still felt soft and looked completely natural. And the best part? I didn’t have to do much of anything to keep them that way.

In fact, I ended up putting my new lashes through what I can only describe as an endurance test. First, there was my birthday trip to Hawaii, where I spent every day swimming in the ocean. Then came a wedding, where plenty of happy tears were shed. Throw in more travel, moving apartments, and a whole lot of stress-induced eye rubbing, and let’s just say I wasn’t exactly treating my lashes like delicate little flowers. Yet, through all of it, they kept their curl.

Initially, I skipped mascara altogether because my lashes looked so good on their own. But because I hadn’t paired my lift with a lash tint—a popular add-on that darkens your lashes—I eventually started incorporating mascara back into my routine for a little extra definition.

The one thing that stayed the same throughout the months was that I no longer needed my eyelash curler. Like, at all. Instead of spending time trying to convince my lashes to cooperate, I could simply run a clean spoolie through them to separate each lash, swipe on a little mascara, and head out the door. A dream come true.

And when I didn’t feel like wearing makeup, a little tinted sunscreen or moisturizer was all I needed to feel put together because my lashes already looked like I’d spent time on them. You know that TikTok trend that’s all about the high-maintenance things we do to stay low-maintenance? Yeah, a Korean lash lift falls squarely in that category.

That was probably my favorite part of it all: The results were noticeable without looking done. Of course, I didn’t have the dramatic, obviously enhanced look of extensions, but my natural lashes had enough lift and definition to make my eyes look more awake and fluttery. It was effortless, understated glam, which is exactly my go-to for my everyday makeup.

By the three-month mark, my lashes had gradually returned to their natural (read: stubby and stick-straight) state. But I was very happy to notice zero brittleness, unusual fallout, or other obvious signs of damage. A win!

That would be a resounding yes. After experiencing nearly three happy months with my Korean lash lift, I can confidently say that it’s one of the most worthwhile beauty treatments I’ve tried in a long time. I absolutely loved the look, the experience, all of it.

All this is coming from someone whose job is to constantly test new products and treatments in the name of beauty journalism. Plenty of them deliver impressive results, but far fewer actually make my everyday life easier. And even fewer genuinely excite me. This one did all three.

I loved how natural my lashes looked, how well the curl held up through trials and tribulations, and how little maintenance was required. But what really sold me was the convenience. Being able to wake up with lashes that already looked lifted and fluttery—and shave several minutes off my makeup routine in the process—was something I appreciated every single morning. It just made me feel so much more confident. One thing to note: It can get pricey. At Two of a Kind Brows, one appointment will set you back $120 for a lift and $160 for a lift and tint.

Would I recommend a Korean lash lift? I’d say it’s absolutely worth it, provided you go to a qualified professional and don’t overdo it by getting lash lifts too often. But once you’ve tried one, you won’t look back.

#6944. Designers Say THIS Is the Secret to Making Framed Posters Look Like Expensive Art

Your prized prints deserve better than a thumbtack.

Posters have come a long way since your dorm-room days. A vintage travel ad, a favorite movie poster, or a concert print you’ve kept for decades can bring as much character to a room as an original painting. The key to giving them a more polished look? A thoughtful frame, creative placement, and the confidence to display what you love.

"Never overthink putting posters up in your home, especially concert posters," says interior designer J.D. Bell. "Your home should reflect your personality, interests, and what you love."

Here, designers share their favorite ways to give posters the elevated treatment they deserve.

The quickest way to transform a poster into a statement piece is to ditch the inexpensive clip frame in favor of something more substantial. Consider rich wood, sleek metal, or even an ornate antique frame that contrasts with the poster's graphic quality.

"The key to making them feel elevated is to treat them with the same consideration you would a fine piece of art," says designer Hattie Sparks Collins. "A beautiful frame, thoughtful installation, and a little juxtaposition go a long way."

For particularly colorful or busy posters, a generous mat can provide visual breathing room, while a frame that picks up one of the artwork's less prominent colors creates a subtle, sophisticated connection.

Have a stack of concert posters gathering dust? Turn them into a gallery wall that tells your story.

Bell recently displayed a collection of vibrant Dead & Company concert posters against black-and-gray Zak + Fox patterned wallpaper in a client's home. Rather than using identical frames, he selected a mix of black styles to introduce texture while maintaining cohesion.

"The darker backdrop really made the colorful artwork pop," he explains. The collection was installed in a hallway leading to the client's office, a fitting tribute to his love of music.

The takeaway? Don't be afraid to experiment with patterned walls, unexpected locations, and subtly mismatched frames.

One of the most compelling ways to display posters is alongside furnishings that feel decidedly more traditional. Think a framed vintage movie poster above an antique chest, or a bold graphic print surrounded by rich textiles and classic upholstery.

"I really love pairing graphic, colorful posters with antiques, interesting textiles, and more traditional furnishings," Collins says. "That tension between high/low and old/new is what makes a room feel collected instead of decorated."

Ultimately, the most stylish poster is one that means something to you. Forget whether it's rare, valuable, or considered fine art. A beautifully framed memento from your favorite concert can become the most interesting piece in the room.

As Bell puts it, "What matters is making those pieces feel special and intentional. The right framing can do a lot for a poster and make it feel like a real piece of art."

#6941. Patrick Stewart Was a Serious Watch Guy. Here Are 5 of the Late Hollywood Legend's Best.

From a treasured Rolex to his beloved IWC, the late actor had a lifelong appreciation for the art of watchmaking.

“Someone once told me that time was a predator that stalked us all our lives," begins Captain Jean-Luc Picard, played by Sir Patrick Stewart in Star Trek Generations, in one of his most memorable lines. "I rather believe that time is a companion who goes with us on a journey, reminds us to cherish every moment, because it will never come again.”

Those musings on time feel particularly poignant now, following the British actor's death. There’s something rather comforting about the idea of time as a companion, particularly when you consider Stewart’s lifelong fascination with the very objects we use to measure it.

Because, as it turns out, Patrick Stewart was quite the watch guy. Not simply someone who happened to wear an expensive timepiece on the red carpet, but a genuine enthusiast with an appreciation for the craftsmanship, history, and ingenuity behind them. He bought his first watch at 18 and, by his own admission, never threw one away.

“The watch industry never ceases to amaze me,” he told the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie in 2019. “Because a watch is more than just an object that gives the time. Behind every watch there is an idea, a design, a concept, an innovation, and the watchmaker's desire to create something that is greater than anything that has ever existed before.”

It's an enthusiasm that stayed with him throughout his life, from the Rolex he reportedly spent almost all his savings on after arriving in Hollywood to his enduring affection for IWC Schaffhausen. And, naturally, there were some rather lovely watches along the way.

So, in celebration of an extraordinary career and a life well lived, we've taken a look back at some of Patrick Stewart's finest watch moments.

Perhaps the most meaningful watch in Stewart's story is one for which we don't have an exact reference. Long before he became one of Hollywood's most recognizable actors, Stewart was given a rather unexpected piece of career advice by the actress Eva Marie Saint. She advised him that appearances mattered, and that an expensive watch might help convince producers he was already a successful actor. So, she took him to a Beverly Hills jeweller, where he picked out a Rolex. The only problem? It cost him his entire savings. “I was eating spaghetti and boiled potatoes for months after that!” he recalled in a 2019 interview with the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie.

It's touching anecdote given Stewart's modest upbringing. Having grown up in poverty, he admitted that it took him years to feel comfortable spending money on beautiful things. But eventually, he came to realize there was nothing wrong with enjoying the rewards of his success.

Tissot Le Locle Powermatic 80 to the 2017 Canadian Grand Prix

One of Stewart's most entertaining watch moments came at the 2017 Canadian Grand Prix, when he joined Daniel Ricciardo on the podium and celebrated the Australian driver's third-place finish by drinking champagne from his racing boot. Yes, really.

And while most people were understandably distracted by the sight of Captain Picard downing champagne from a sweaty shoe, there was a rather lovely watch on his wrist, a Tissot Le Locle Powermatic 80. The reasonably priced Swiss automatic features a silver guilloché dial, Roman numerals, a black leather strap, and an impressive 80-hour power reserve. Proof that even a relatively affordable dress watch can look the part.

Long before his appearances at watchmaking galas, Stewart was already making a convincing case for the understated dress watch. In this archival portrait, the actor wears a small, gold-tone, rectangular timepiece, seemingly a Cartier Tank, on a dark leather strap. Paired with a pale linen-style suit and a knitted white top, it's a particularly handsome combination.

First introduced in 1917, the Cartier Tank has become one of watchmaking's most enduring designs, worn by everyone from Andy Warhol to Muhammad Ali. Its appeal lies in its simplicity: a rectangular case, elegant proportions, and a design that has barely needed changing in more than a century. And judging by this photograph, Stewart understood the appeal of a good dress watch long before his later affection for IWC became public knowledge.

IWC Portofino Automatic to the 2018 Academy Awards

When it came to dressing for the red carpet, Stewart understood the appeal of keeping things classic. At the 90th Academy Awards in 2018, he opted for an IWC Portofino Automatic, reference IW356504, a rather beautiful dress watch that proved you don't need an enormous, diamond-covered timepiece to make an impression. Housed in a 40mm case of 18-carat rose gold, the watch features a silver-plated dial, slim gold hour markers, and a discreet date window at three o'clock. It's finished with a dark brown alligator leather strap and powered by an automatic movement. There's nothing particularly flashy about it, which is precisely what makes it so appealing.

IWC Portugieser Chronograph Classic at the 2017 SIHH Gala

By 2017, Stewart's appreciation for fine watchmaking had brought him into the company of some of the industry's most celebrated names. That January, he attended IWC Schaffhausen's Decoding the Beauty of Time gala in Geneva alongside his wife, Sunny Ozell, sporting a rather handsome IWC Portugieser Chronograph Classic.

First introduced in 2013, the reference 3904 is a particularly elegant piece of watchmaking. Featuring a 42mm case, distinctive Arabic numerals, and a railway-track minute scale, it's a slightly sportier proposition than the Portofino. Inside sits IWC's automatic calibre 89361, complete with a flyback chronograph function and an impressive 68-hour power reserve. The evening celebrated the Swiss manufacturer's renewed Da Vinci collection, but Stewart's choice of wristwear showcased another of IWC's most recognizable designs. And given that he once named IWC as his favorite watchmaker, it's hardly surprising that he had such good taste.
