#7042. International student enrollment fell 2.7% in 2025-26

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The annual Open Doors report also found signs losses are poised to continue, with the number of new foreign students plummeting 17.7% from the prior year.

U.S. colleges hosted roughly 1.1 million international students in the 2025-26 academic year, an annual decrease of 2.7%, according to the annual Open Doors report released Monday.

A 22.4% drop in international master’s enrollment, down to 237,655 students, helped drive the overall loss, the report found. Enrollment of foreign undergraduates stayed largely level — dipping just 0.3% to 356,165 — while the number of doctoral students increased 3.1% to 158,152.

The report, from the Institute of International Education and the U.S. Department of State, found signs of a weakening international enrollment pipeline that could lead to further losses in the coming years. New international enrollment — foreign students attending their institution for the first time — plummeted 17.7% from 2024-25, marking the second consecutive year of declines.

Alongside the Open Doors report, IIE released a preliminary fall survey of 809 colleges conducted with 10 higher education associations. They found that international enrollment declined 9% at surveyed institutions this fall, driven by a 15% drop in new foreign students and a 15% decrease in international graduate students.

Nearly 9 out of 10 surveyed colleges said the visa process has hurt foreign enrollment, and 70% blamed travel restrictions. Additionally, 60% cited competition from other countries for international students.

“There are more countries actively investing in and recruiting international students,” Mirka Martel, IIE’s head of research, evaluation and learning, said during a press call last week. “As a result, this often means that there are more destination opportunities.”

Just over half of the surveyed colleges that reported growth in new international student enrollment attributed the gains to recruitment efforts and campus athletics programs. Another 44% cited outreach efforts to foreign students who had already applied and been admitted.

Surveyed colleges also reported recruiting international students already within the U.S. Over half, 57%, said they reached out to foreign students studying at U.S. high schools, and 48% sought out international undergraduates for graduate programs.

The countries the U.S. has traditionally relied on for a steady stream of international students — namely, India and China — are sending fewer citizens to study here, according to the broader Open Doors report.

While India retained the top spot, sending 339,435 students in 2025-26, that’s a 6.5% decline from the year prior. China, whose college student population in the U.S. has ticked steadily downward since the pandemic, sent 254,458 students. That’s a 4.3% decrease from 2024-25.

Despite overall enrollment declines, the Open Doors report found an increase in Optional Practical Training participation. The program allows student visa holders to work in their field of study in the U.S. for up to three years following graduation. In 2025-26, 335,785 foreign students took part in OPT, up 14.1% from the previous year.

Martel said that the growth in OPT over the past four years is due to the post-pandemic rebound in international graduate students. Those students are still making their way through the OPT pipeline, she said.

However, early indicators suggest OPT participation could soon suffer declines as well. IIE’s preliminary fall poll, conducted in October, found that OPT students declined 6% year over year across surveyed colleges.

The drop comes amid news that OPT could get significantly more expensive for colleges.

Early last month, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security proposed up to $100,000 in new fees for each international student a college recommends for OPT.

Many colleges have told the Trump administration they won’t be able to afford the fee. However, the second Trump administration has largely enacted regulations affecting higher ed with few changes from its proposals.

The latest Open Doors data reinforces what many colleges and higher ed groups have flagged — that the international student pipeline is constricting.

A new analysis of federal data from the Presidents’ Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration found that fewer than 1.1 million international students were studying in the U.S. as of last month, down 6.7% from October 2024.

Miriam Feldblum, the organization’s president and CEO, called the data a warning sign for the U.S.

“The administration’s policies are creating uncertainty at every stage of an international student’s journey,” Feldblum said in a Thursday statement. “While we can’t attribute the decline to any single policy, the cumulative effect makes the United States a less predictable destination and puts our international talent pipeline at risk.”

In a separate survey of some 200 U.S. colleges released last week, nearly 6 out of 10 said they saw fewer international graduate students in fall 2026. The average reported decline was 13%.

Most respondents, 90%, said restrictive federal policies posed a significant obstacle to international enrollment, with some citing the colossal new fees the Trump administration has sought to impose on H-1B visas and the OPT program.

The Common App in August said that first-year international applicants using its platform declined 10% year over year during the 2025-26 cycle.

And public institutions across the country — including the University of Colorado, Pennsylvania State University, Central Michigan University and Ohio State University — have reported significant drops in international enrollment this fall. Nearly 6 in 10 international students in the U.S. enroll at public colleges, according to Martel.

#7041. In modern higher ed, rigor is a function of design, not a delivery format

Academic rigor in online courses is now a non-negotiable leadership priority because the entire higher-education ecosystem has shifted: online learning is no longer supplemental. It is the learning environment for millions of students. The stakes for quality, credibility and transferability have never been higher.

That rigor should be judged by one thing above all: how effective the learning experience is and whether it produces measurable outcomes. At Sophia Learning, we're constantly assessing whether those outcomes prepare students for future academic or professional success. This matters especially as institutions explore flexible learning pathways, including self-paced environments. When those environments are well-designed, grounded in mastery-based progression, aligned assessments and continual course improvement, they strengthen academic quality. And when they're validated externally through a faculty-led process, they can align with institutional standards for both rigor and transferability.

Academic rigor is not a function of delivery modality. Whether learning occurs in a traditional classroom, online or through a self-paced environment, academic quality is determined by clearly articulated learning outcomes and alignment between instruction, practice and assessment.

That is equally true with mastery-based progression. Learners advance by demonstrating competency. Multiple assessment methods (formative and summative) provide a more complete picture of learning. It’s an approach that allows every student who has the grit to succeed.

This approach also relies on continuous course iteration. Courses evolve to address students’ needs based on learner performance data, to reflect changes in the field as understood by faculty and to strengthen academic integrity.

Because of their relative novelty, self-paced models are often subject to a higher level of scrutiny. Accordingly, there should be higher investments in design and technology and quality assurance review processes. These investments result in courses that are both flexible and academically sound but are based on a new and emerging set of definitions.

Structured course design creates consistency even when learners progress at different rates. Clear learning objectives, sequenced content, robust and embedded practice opportunities and aligned assessments provide a support system to help students succeed and meet high academic expectations.

In many cases, flexibility improves learning outcomes. Self-paced environments allow learners to spend additional time on challenging concepts, revisit instructional materials and practice until mastery is achieved. This deeper engagement can improve both comprehension and transfer to future academic situations and employment.

Flexible learning pathways are particularly valuable for nontraditional students, including working adults, military-affiliated learners, caregivers and individuals balancing multiple responsibilities. For these students, set schedules can create barriers to persistence and completion. Self-paced learning removes many of those barriers while maintaining high standards and expectations.

Similar to traditional learning models, self-paced learning relies on faculty-led evaluation. At Sophia, experience is essential: Sophia Learning content and assessment subject matter experts (SMEs) have an average of 17 years of teaching experience. Seventy-one percent of Sophia Learning content SMEs have a PhD or equivalent degree. This ensures that rigorous academic standards are met and that course content matches students’ needs.

As some institutions increasingly incorporate alternative credit pathways into their degree programs, external validation can play a critical role in maintaining confidence and trust while ensuring transferability.

Independent review processes provide an additional layer of quality assurance beyond internal course development and evaluation. For example, Sophia Learning collaborates with our 115 institutional partners to review courses and articulate them into degree programs that align with institutional academic requirements. Nearly 3,000 colleges and universities have reviewed Sophia Learning transcripts as part of transfer credit evaluation processes.

Sophia courses also undergo review through the American Council on Education® (ACE) credit recommendation process, in which external faculty evaluators assess course quality, learning outcomes and assessment practices. These reviews provide institutions with an independent perspective on academic alignment and transferability.

Importantly, external validation complements rather than replaces institutional oversight and the institution’s internal quality assurance systems. Together, these processes provide a multilayered framework that supports academic integrity while enabling innovation in course delivery and credential pathways.

Ultimately, discussions about rigor should be grounded not only in course design principles but also in measurable student outcomes.

Evidence from institutional partnerships increasingly demonstrates that outcome-aligned, flexible learning pathways can boost student success. Students who use Sophia to augment their degree programs at other institutions often exhibit stronger persistence and continued engagement in their programs.

When learning experiences are intentionally designed around measurable outcomes and mastery, flexibility and academic rigor reinforce each other.

Higher education is entering an era in which access, flexibility and quality must evolve to prepare learners for today’s employment demands. As institutions seek new ways to support learner success, traditional assumptions about rigor deserve reconsideration.

Academic quality must be ensured by continual assessment and validation. Courses need to address the academic and professional needs of the greater learner population.

It should be measured by the most important factor of all: the positive impact it has on learner outcomes. When we provide options that meet post traditional learners’ needs, we can improve persistence, academic performance and progress toward degree completion.

Increases in dual credit and Advanced Placement participation in Illinois were particularly notable for Black students and students with disabilities.

Previous research shows links between success in programs like dual credit and AP, and greater college access and persistence.

Statewide, participation in dual credit courses rose by 17.1% overall in 2023-24 and another 9.1% in 2024-25, as compared with 1.6% and 6.9% in the two previous school years, respectively. Statewide participation in AP courses rose 6.6% in both 2023-24 and 2024-25, as compared with 2.3% the previous year and a participation drop of 5.9% in 2021-22.

While these and other gains were made in the immediate aftermath of the legislation, the study did not attempt to prove a causal relationship, said Sarah Cashdollar, associate director of the Illinois Workforce and Education Research Collaborative.

“Many other things are happening in the state that could be responsible,” she said, including amendments to the state’s Dual Credit Quality Act and higher state appropriations for dual credit and AP courses. “The state, the legislature, and all those who support it are throwing a lot at this issue in trying to improve participation in programs,” she said. “It’s hard for researchers to disentangle the effects of each of them separately.”

Nonetheless, the data describes a positive trend, said Meg Bates, IWERC director. “Certainly, it would have been a disappointment to see it go in the other direction,” she said. We’re excited to see it, and we’ll keep watching it. Hopefully we can do that kind of causal study in the future.”

The IWERC study bolsters other research that shows enrollment in rigorous courses is critical for underrepresented populations, Bates said.

For dual credit, the study found participation rates in 2023-24 increased from the previous year by 28.4% for Latino students and 25.6% for Black students. The year-over-year participation rate increase for Asian students was 15.6% and 13.9% for White students.

For AP, the increases were 9.2% for Latino students, 8.5% for Black students, 4.6% for White students and 3.4% for Asian students.

“Sometimes, we see a general trend that looks one way, but when you go underneath the hood, changes in certain students’ participation” runs counter to the trend, she said. “In this case, we got to see a little bit of both.”

However, she added, “That’s great, seeing more Black and Latino students, and more English learners. But because it’s going up for everyone, some of our gaps are widening.”

Those gaps should spur further research, Bates said, because educators and policymakers don’t want to see student experiences become more asymmetrical. “Following up with districts to understand the ‘why’ is very important.”

Cashdollar would like to see more research into the spikes in participation for English learners and Latino students.

“Was this group previously overlooked despite being capable of taking these classes — so the policy did what it was intended to do, and qualify more students?” she said. “A better understanding of all the different factors at play would be really useful.”

Bates believes a policy like automatic enrollment is uniquely equipped to target English learners. “Educating English learners can be costly for districts and can be hard to staff because you need bilingual educators,” she said.

This is a "nudge" to include English learners in this coursework," Cashdollar said.

"That’s a huge takeaway and something advocates are very excited about.”

#6976. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. #6948. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

The brand’s story begins in 1918, when entrepreneur Kamekichi Yamazaki established the Shokosha Watch Research Institute in Tokyo with the goal of providing a domestically produced alternative to imported Swiss and American watches. "In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

#6900. The Ultimate Guide to the Rolex Daytona, the World’s Most Wanted Watch

From the hand-wound watches dealers struggled to sell to the steel model that commands an impressive premium today.

In September, four Rolex Daytonas sold at a charity auction in London for a combined £37.3 million (about $49.5 million). The most expensive, at £10.3 ($13.7) million, had a turquoise dial and a ring of pink sapphires. That same month, Rolex unveiled a steel-and-platinum Daytona with a white enamel dial and a speed scale that recalled the original from 1963.

The regular steel Daytona with a white dial costs $16,900 from Rolex, if you can get one. In September, WatchCharts put its secondary-market value around $35,000. It wasn’t always like this. In its early years, according to Sotheby’s, retailers discounted Daytonas to clear stock.

Rolex launched the Cosmograph in 1963. An early advert called it the “Le Mans,” after the French endurance race. The name that stuck came from Daytona, Florida, where cars raced on hard-packed sand before the city had a speedway.

The first model, reference 6239, had a movement you wound by hand and two buttons to operate the stopwatch. Rolex put the speed scale on the steel bezel, where a driver could read it at a glance. The three small counters stood out against the dial, black on a light face or light on a black one.

By the 1970s, two models had emerged that collectors still prize today. The 6263 had a black acrylic bezel, and the 6265 had a metal one. Both had screw-down collars around the stopwatch buttons. On some, the word Daytona curves in red above the bottom counter. Collectors call these “Big Reds.”

Joanne Woodward gave Newman a 6239 around the time he made Winning, the 1969 film that sparked his interest in racing. She had “DRIVE CAREFULLY ME” engraved on the back. “There’s something almost magical about it,” James Cox, who later owned the watch, told Phillips. In 2017 it sold for $17.75 million, then an auction record for a wristwatch.

A “Paul Newman” usually refers to a type of dial. Look for the unusual numerals and small square-ended markers in the counters. The dials were unpopular when new. By 2013, Christie’s could sell 50 Daytonas in a single Geneva auction for $13.2 million. The top lot was a steel watch with a Paul Newman dial. It fetched $1.09 million, four years before the actor’s own watch went to auction.

On one particularly rare black-dial 6263, the word Oyster appears below Rolex Cosmograph. Collectors call it the Oyster Sotto, from the Italian for under. Phillips sold one in 2025 for 1.39 million Swiss francs (approximately $1.67 million). “One mistake people make is that they expect everything to fit neatly into a specific set of rules,” Geoff Hess, global head of watches at Sotheby’s, once explained. A dial can be genuine Rolex and still have come from a different watch.

In 1988 the steel Daytona became the 16520. It grew to 40mm, gained raised gold hour markers and a glossier dial, and wound itself as you wore it. Rolex extensively reworked Zenith’s El Primero movement for the watch. It was the first automatic Daytona and the model that established the basic dimensions of the watch Rolex still makes today.

On some early examples, a gap makes the word Cosmograph appear to float beneath the rest of the lettering. On certain black dials, the rings around the counters have turned brown with age. Those are known as Patrizzi dials, after the auctioneer Osvaldo Patrizzi, who noticed the change.

Three steel references are worth knowing. The 116520, introduced in 2000, has a steel bezel and the first chronograph movement Rolex developed and made itself. The 116500LN, from 2016, brought back the look of the black acrylic bezel in ceramic. The current 126500LN, introduced in 2023, has a slim steel rim around that ceramic bezel, a revised dial, and a new movement that runs for about three days when fully wound. The two ceramic models can look remarkably similar in listing photographs.

The unicorn is a hand-wound 6265 that Phillips described in 2018 as the only known vintage Daytona in white gold. It sold for 5.94 million Swiss francs (about $7.1 million). The Rainbow gets its name from the graduated sapphires around its bezel. A white-gold example made around 1994, nearly two decades before the production versions appeared, sold in 2024 for 5.505 million Swiss francs (approximately $6.1 million).

Sixty years after that early advertisement, Rolex returned to Le Mans. Its 2023 watch for the race’s centenary has a stopwatch that counts the full 24 hours. The regular Daytona counts to 12.

An official Rolex retailer is the place to start if you want a new one at list price. Retailers manage their own allocations, so availability varies. Rolex addressed the shortage in 2021. “The scarcity of our products is not a strategy on our part,” it said, explaining that it could not meet demand without compromising quality.

On the secondary market, compare the same reference and dial. The WatchCharts September estimate for the current black-dial steel model was about $29,000, roughly $6,000 less than the white. If you’re buying vintage, ask a specialist dealer or auction house for a condition report. Find out whether the case has been heavily polished, which parts have been replaced, and whether the dial is correct for that particular watch. On a Daytona, those details can explain more about the price than the reference number.
