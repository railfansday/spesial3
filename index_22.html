Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer greets House Minority Whip Katherine Clark at the Center for American Progress Action Fund’s “Results to Roadmap” event on Thursday. (Tom Williams/CQ Roll Call)

Landscape architect Ed Hollander and his firm Hollander Design collaborated on the 1.7-acre property’s outdoor living spaces, while outfitting the gardens with what he calls “maritime habitat” plantings, a mix of native and non-native species “that are adapted to this sunny, salty, breezy environment.”

A leading Democrat on the House Financial Services Committee introduced legislation Thursday aimed at helping regulators use technology to better spot financial stress turbocharged by artificial intelligence and around-the-clock payment systems.

The draft bill, from Rep. Bill Foster, D-Ill., would require the Federal Reserve to work with other regulators to develop, test and publicly release model software that would improve the ability to assess market conditions in real time and respond quickly to cybersecurity and other threats.

The bill is designed to help regulators keep pace with new risks "driven largely by the development of state-of-the-art AI models," according to a summary.

The legislation is intended to help regulators provide financing to banks during crises and wind down failing banks. The model software would reduce the amount of manual compliance work that smaller financial institutions would have to do by providing standard data formats for regulatory reporting and tools to manage and transmit the information to regulators, according to a Foster spokesperson.

“With advances in AI creating new cybersecurity threats and financial risks, it's more important than ever that we modernize the way we monitor the health of our financial institutions,” Foster said in a statement. “We cannot afford to rely on outdated systems to oversee an increasingly complex financial sector.”

Foster, who has a Ph.D. in physics and has been a leader on AI issues on the Financial Services panel, is in line to become chair of the Financial Institutions Subcommittee next year.

He has been mulling a challenge to ranking member Maxine Waters, D-Calif., to chair the full committee in the new Congress. But Foster is now “leaning toward not running,” a person familiar with his thinking said.

Waters already has a confirmed challenger in Rep. Sean Casten, D-Ill., who on Thursday circulated a "Dear Colleague" letter seeking the support of House Democrats for his bid.

Casten touted his “career at the intersection of business, science and public policy." Before entering Congress, he worked in clean energy and co-founded a company that converted industrial waste energy into usable power.

In his letter, Casten cited the need to lower housing and insurance costs, protect consumers from fraud, address risks posed by cryptocurrency and prediction markets and guard against the next financial crisis. He also promised to hold the Trump administration accountable for ethical lapses.

He's got a tough road ahead of him, however. Every Financial Services Democrat except Casten, Foster and Rep. Sam Liccardo of California signed an Oct. 21 letter to House Democrats in support of Waters. That show of support came after Casten made it known he was interested and it was clear that Foster had been considering his own bid.

Although Foster may not compete for the chairmanship, he has recently talked to Waters about changing how the committee operates.

“He wants a variety of members to play a role in legislation brought up in committee,” the source familiar with his thinking said. He’s also seeking more input on witnesses who testify at subcommittee hearings.

The draft bill he released on Thursday covers a topic that Foster is likely to pursue throughout the 120th Congress. He often asks the most questions about the use of AI in financial services at hearings and offers the most amendments related to the issue at markups.

Under the bill, model software would be developed for banking records and operations, regulatory reporting, stress testing, anti-money laundering and fraud screening, AI risk monitoring and other activities.

The software must be open source, publicly available and tested for reliability and accuracy, according to the summary. It would be tested using synthetic or legally permitted historical data rather than actual customer accounts. Regulators would not take financial or supervisory actions during the study.

The software developed during the study would not have to be adopted; rather, it “should be viewed as a starting point for further modernization,” the summary says.

All software models would have to be published within one year of enactment of the bill and a final report assessing feasibility, costs and savings, competition and other criteria, would be due to Congress within two years.

#7205. Longtime West Virginia Sen. Rockefeller dies

Sen. John D. Rockefeller IV, D-W.Va., questions witnesses during a Senate Finance hearing on health care on June 23, 2011. (Scott J. Ferrell/CQ Roll Call)

Former Sen. Jay Rockefeller, a five-term West Virginia Democrat and champion for the rural poor, has died at the age of 89.

Rockefeller, who retired after the 2014 elections, played key roles on the tax-writing Senate Finance Committee, where he helped write landmark children’s health insurance legislation, as well as the Commerce, Science and Transportation and Intelligence panels which he chaired.

A staunchly progressive lawmaker, he represented a deeply conservative state that turned further to the right in his later years. Rockefeller, scion of a famous New York family renowned for its wealth, never wavered in his convictions and retired rather than face a potential backlash from voters upset by the then-Obama administration’s attempts to rein in the coal industry.

Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., who challenged Rockefeller for his seat before he decided not to seek reelection, released a statement Friday praising her predecessor’s legacy.

“Jay dedicated his life to serving West Virginia and our nation, leaving a lasting legacy. He was always a true gentleman who treated me with respect, kindness, and encouragement,” Capito said. “Over the years, we developed a wonderful friendship that transcended politics, and I will always be grateful for that.”

West Virginia’s Republican Gov. Patrick Morrisey — himself a transplanted Northerner, with roots in Brooklyn and New Jersey — lauded Rockefeller’s record on health care and aid for coal miners and veterans in their adopted state.

“His work helped shape our state and left a lasting mark on the lives of generations of West Virginians,” Morrisey said.

Rockefeller was born in New York City on June 18, 1937, the great-grandson of Standard Oil Co. founder John D. Rockefeller, who was once considered the world’s richest man. Rockefeller attended Harvard University, graduating with an A.B. in 1961. His passion was for the Far East and he spent three years studying at the International Christian University in Tokyo.

He returned to the states and was appointed by President John F. Kennedy to the National Advisory Council of the Peace Corps in 1961, spearheaded the Peace Corps initial program in the Philippines. He worked for a brief time in what was then known as U.S. Department of Far Eastern Affairs before deciding to embark on a public service role of a more personal nature. He enrolling as a VISTA volunteer, in the program which would later be known as AmeriCorps, and moved to the small mining community of Emmons, W.Va., in 1964 at age 27.

In West Virginia, Rockefeller found his life’s work. He described the experience as being “reborn,” and spoke often the poverty and hardship he encountered living in the small town. “I could not leave,” he said of West Virginia. “I [had] become so devoted to those people and the unfairness."

He had planned to remain only one year, but decided to stay and became active in local politics. He broke from his family's long tradition of Republican loyalty and ran successfully for the House of Delegates as a Democrat in 1966. Two years later, he was elected secretary of state.

Rockefeller lost a bid for governor in 1972, but he became the president of West Virginia Wesleyan College and continued to place roots in the state. He married Sharon Percy Rockefeller in 1967 and they started their family. When he ran for governor again in 1976, he won, and served two terms before winning election to the U.S. Senate in 1984, ultimately serving five terms before retiring in 2014.

Rockefeller was determined to increase economic opportunities for West Virginia, for whom he felt was forgotten and abandoned by the rest of the country. He called the people of his state “the hardest-working people I know,” and believed that bringing more business opportunities would help lift the state out of severe rural poverty and the dependence on the coal industry.

Rockefeller traveled to Japan and worked tirelessly to bring a Toyota manufacturing plant to Buffalo, W.Va, one of over twenty Japanese companies he’d brought to West Virginia. He fought for fair trade policies and targeted tax relief to protect West Virginia industries such as steel and chemicals.

Coal mining had long been the economic backbone of West Virginia, but Rockefeller felt more must be done to protect miners, many of whom worked in unsafe conditions or fell ill with pneumoconiosis, or black lung disease, after years of inhaling coal dust.

But defying the coal industry came with a political cost. When Rockefeller originally ran for governor in 1972, he had opposed mountaintop mining, also called strip mining, or the practice of removing large amounts of earth and rock in order to access underground seams of coal. When he ran again in 1976, he reversed his position and won.

But even as he ascended to the Senate, Rockefeller remained both friend and foe to the coal industry.

Despite growing up on the Upper East Side of Manhattan, figuratively and literally hundreds of miles away from coal mines, Rockefeller understood its deep roots in West Virginia’s economy and culture. He believed it was the miners — rather than industry — for whom he was defending.

He hung a century-old photo of a coal miner in his Senate office in the Hart building, and would point to the photo and remark to visitors that “this is why I’m here and who I work for.”

Rockefeller fought to secure pensions for miners, especially as coal companies would file for bankruptcy and immediately restructure to do away with pension plans.

In 1990, Rockefeller was one of only 11 senators to vote against the bipartisan Clean Air Act, which he felt disadvantaged workers in West Virginia. But those close to him said the decision weighed heavily on him, and Rockefeller privately worried that the lack of such regulations would pollute the air and destroy waterways, the brunt of which would be born by the same disadvantaged people whom he vowed to protect.

He believed strongly in the deliberative aspects of the legislative process, consuming great amounts of reading material to familiarize himself with the issues. During a time of increasing partisan rancor, Rockefeller still reached across the aisle to find a willing partner, even when there was not one to be found.

In 2010, as the Environmental Protection Agency’s greenhouse gas regulations were coming to fruition, Rockefeller came up with a compromise to delay implementation for two years.

He viewed himself as a broker between industry interests and the hard working people from coal-states like West Virginia. He had hoped that his years of standing up for coal miners and West Virginia interests would foster enough goodwill among industries primed to oppose any compromise that was less than an all-out rejection of the existing EPA statutes.

But industry walked away from Rockefeller, preferring a hard-line opposition to EPA regulations, and lobbied other senators to vote against Rockefeller’s measure. Furious, Rockefeller took to the floor of the Senate to denounce the coal industry and its leaders, an unprecedented action for a popular coal-state senator.

“The reality is that many who run the coal industry today would rather attack false enemies and deny real problems then find solutions,” he said on the Senate floor in June 2012. “Scare tactics are a cynical waste of time, money and, worst of all, coal miners’ hopes. But sadly, these coal operators have closed themselves off from any other opposing voices and few dared to speak out for change — even though it’s been staring them in the face for years.”

His fury against the coal industry was heard loudly, many speculating that he would retire rather than face reelection.

He had won reelection to the Senate handily during his five campaigns. In 2008, his final election, he was reelected with nearly 64 percent of the vote. But six months following his fated coal speech, in January 2013, Rockefeller announced he would retire at the end of his fifth term.

Rockefeller’s legacy may best be known for his fight to expand health care for vulnerable populations, including children, the economically disadvantaged and the elderly. Rockefeller served as one of the chief congressional champions for President Bill Clinton’s proposed health care overhaul, and later he played a critical role and was a loud progressive voice in writing and passing the Affordable Care Act in the Senate.

Rockefeller believed strongly in a public health insurance option, offering it as an amendment in the Senate Finance Committee during the ACA markup. A public-run insurance plan, he said, would put patients before profits, and help those people, especially West Virginians, with high health care costs and poor coverage who were left “out in the cold.”

"These are people," Rockefeller said, visibly emotional during his closing arguments to the Senate Finance Committee. "Eleven-year-old kids. These are families, and we have to respect them. And you respect them by giving them a choice."

The amendment was rejected. Rockefeller pivoted to strengthening Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program provisions, believing that poor and disadvantaged families could seek refuge within those programs. CHIP had been one of Rockefeller’s signature legislative victories, helping to write that program initially in 1997 and later working on a major expansion that became law in 2009.

Despite his initial concerns and later disappointments — at one time saying publicly he could not support the measure as it stood — he later added his support to the ACA, citing it as a win for all Americans and actively lobbied his colleagues to support it.

Rockefeller was also known for his role on the Senate Intelligence Committee. A former chairman, he was critical of the nation's mismanagement of the Iraq war and clashed frequently with Republicans over the treatment of detainees. After he retired from Congress, Rockefeller served as a distinguished fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations.

Respect for Rockefeller ran deep on Capitol Hill. He was beloved by staff and known as a thoughtful, deliberative lawmaker. He had a fondness for classical music and served as a board member of the Bach Consort, an organization in Washington devoted to the study and performance of the music of Bach and his contemporaries. He admired art and Asian culture, and served as a board member of the Asia Society.

Rockefeller is survived by his wife, Sharon Percy Rockefeller; four children: John D. Rockefeller V, Valerie Rockefeller, Charles Rockefeller and Justin Rockefeller; and nine grandchildren.

Above: A color-drenched den on Long Island, New York, designed by General Assembly.

Homes are getting smarter, but sellers are also advertising places to unplug. Zillow’s 2027 Home Trends Report finds growing mentions of both AI and tech-free spaces, along with color-drenched rooms, home offices, and “catios.”

The report tracks year-over-year changes in mentions of hundreds of features across millions of for-sale listings. The results reflect how sellers and agents are marketing homes—not a survey of buyers or evidence that a particular feature will help a property sell. Still, they offer a look at the amenities and design details increasingly making their way into listing descriptions. Here are the main takeaways.

Mentions of AI are up 119 percent, the largest increase in this year’s report. Zillow points to AI-powered appliances among the features sellers are advertising, while mentions of app-controlled home systems rose 34 percent.

Spaces to unplug are also appearing 34 percent more often. The report describes an interest in both technology that simplifies daily tasks and designated places to take a break from it.

“Digital detox is the new luxury,” says Amanda Pendleton, Zillow’s home trends expert, in a statement. “Homes are getting smarter, but buyers also want tech-free zones where nothing needs charging. Sellers and their real estate agents are now highlighting spaces to unplug.”

Color-drenched spaces—rooms where the same color extends across the walls, ceiling, and trim—are up 84 percent in listing mentions, making them one of the report’s biggest increases. Zillow sees the approach as part of a move toward bolder, more polished interiors. Exterior finishes are gaining ground, too: painted brick is up 27 percent, and board-and-batten siding rose 24 percent.

Color drenching takes a shade beyond the walls and onto the ceiling, trim, and other architectural details. The approach has even spawned a bathroom variation: tile drenching, which extends the same tile across surfaces for a continuous look.

The language used to describe these homes is changing alongside the features. Mentions of “elevated” rose 71 percent, while “cozy” fell 22 percent, “eclectic” dropped 19 percent, and “boho” declined 13 percent. Cozy remains one of the most common words in listings, but it’s appearing less frequently than it did a year ago.

Kitchen language is moving in the other direction. Mentions of “chef’s kitchens” fell 21 percent, and “gourmet kitchens” declined 15 percent.

Mentions of multigenerational living jumped 58 percent. Zillow connects the increase to homes accommodating the “sandwich generation,” or people caring for children and older family members.

Features that can serve different ages and needs are also appearing more frequently. Grab bars are up 18 percent, wider doorways are up 13 percent, and nurseries rose 9 percent. The report places these changes alongside the growing emphasis on flexible rooms and spaces with more than one use.

The home spa is changing. Mentions of spa-inspired bathrooms rose 60 percent, and cold plunge pools increased 44 percent. Jacuzzis and jetted tubs, meanwhile, each fell about 9 percent.

Spaces for exercise are also gaining ground. Home gyms are up 18 percent, while golf simulators rose 29 percent. Together, the findings cover both workout spaces and amenities marketed around rest and recovery, rather than a single version of the home wellness room.

Cats get a category of their own this year. “Catios”—screened-in outdoor spaces that let indoor cats spend time outside—are appearing in 32 percent more listings. A place to get some air, watch the birds, and keep an eye on the neighbors—the cat has a wish list, too.

#7203. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.
