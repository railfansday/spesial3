#7071. U of Kentucky grants employee religious exemption from AI use

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A spokesperson said the university found the humanist administrator could still perform the essential functions of his job with the requested accommodation.

AI’s adoption has raised a number of compliance considerations for employers, and employment law experts have warned that religious objections to AI use could increasingly come to the forefront. That’s in part because religious discrimination claims have already climbed in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic, but it is also due to the ethical and moral questions that surround AI use.

In his statement, for instance, the University of Kentucky employee cited AI’s environmental costs, its “interference with people’s ability to earn a living” and its “supplanting and theft of human creativity.” The employee said these factors meant that use of AI would go against his conscience.

Jay Blanton, spokesperson for the university, said in an email that the institution reviews religious accommodation requests on an individual basis. It concluded that the employee in question, a research administrator, could perform the essential functions of his job with the requested accommodation.

“As technology and job responsibilities evolve, accommodations may be reviewed to ensure they remain reasonable and workable,” Blanton noted.

It’s not the first reported instance of a U.S. employee being granted religious accommodation with respect to AI. In June, Business Insider reported that a North Carolina-based Unitarian Universalist employee who worked as a software engineer received an AI accommodation after she raised environmental and ethical objections to the tech.

Under employment laws like Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act, employers may deny an employee’s proposed religious accommodation if they can show that it poses an undue hardship.

But that calculation has shifted in recent years thanks to a landmark 2023 U.S. Supreme Court decision. In Groff v. DeJoy, SCOTUS held that, in order for a proposed accommodation to pose undue hardship, the burden must be substantial in the overall context of the employer’s business. Legal experts previously told HR Dive that this is a higher bar than the court’s prior precedent, which defined undue hardship as that which poses a “more than de minimis cost.”

“The court did not say that the CCG President has authority to place the Antioch University President on paid administrative leave, though it did suggest the CCG Board could take that action,” the university said on Oct. 22.

#7062. Students with discharged IDR loans now face huge tax bills

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Borrowers will be taxed on student debt canceled under the program in 2026 and could owe up to $12,000 more in April, per a new analysis.

Under the pandemic-era American Rescue Plan Act, discharged student loan debt did not count as income on federal taxes from Dec. 31, 2020, to Dec. 31, 2025. But since Congress allowed that provision to expire, IDR loans discharged in 2026 and beyond will appear on borrowers’ tax bills.

Protect Borrowers called on federal lawmakers to intervene and permanently exempt discharged IDR loans, as well as discharged loans through the Repayment Assistance Plan, from federal taxes in the next budget reconciliation process.

The average discharged IDR loan is worth about $50,000, according to the nonproft. Its analysis said that sum often outstrips the value of the initial loan, due to borrowers’ low income-based monthly payments not covering the cost of ballooning interest.

Single borrowers without children with discharged IDR loans would pay over $10,000 in federal taxes if they earned $40,000 in 2026 and nearly $20,000 if they earned $80,000.

After payroll and state taxes, these borrowers “could effectively see their take-home income cut nearly in half,” the report said.

Families with children and a lower household income could see the highest share of their annual earnings absorbed by the tax increase. A household of four with $40,000 in annual income can expect to pay an additional $10,558 on top of their base expected tax bill — $1,704 in new taxes and $8,854 in lost tax credits.

“Treating such cancelled debt as taxable income will hit many borrowers with an unprecedented tax bomb and force them to replace their debt to the U.S. Department of Education with debt to the Internal Revenue Service,” the analysis said.

The change is also expected to have a disproportionate impact on borrowers in the South, as they typically have larger loan balances and lower incomes than the U.S. average.

The massive expected increase in taxes will put most borrowers in an impossible financial position, after they’ve already “endured significant financial hardship for a majority of their adult lives,” Protect Borrowers said.

As of 2022, half of borrowers who had their IDR student debt canceled didn’t have a bank account or nonretirement savings, the nonprofit said, citing survey data from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Like Protect Borrowers, a coalition of Democratic Senators last November called on the Trump administration to exempt canceled IDR loans from income taxation.

Following Tuesday’s midterms, Democrats are set to gain control of both chambers of Congress and such a change could stand a better chance of passing. President Donald Trump still holds veto power.

#7037. How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

#7009. How student showcases can extend project-based learning’s benefits

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools uses “genius hours” and student presentations to deepen students’ investment in the learning process.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

#7004. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

When comparing the teacher pay gap with over 30 countries, Brookings found that U.S. teachers only make two-thirds of what other college graduates earn.

Public school teacher pay in the U.S. lags significantly when compared to other industrialized countries, according to a late October analysis by the Brown Center on Education Policy at the Brookings Institution.

Examining average pay for teachers at the primary school level versus that of other college graduates across more than 30 countries, Brookings found the U.S. ranked last — and that its primary school teachers earn about two-thirds of what other college graduates are paid annually.

That pay gap is wider than what the Economic Policy Institute has found in its ongoing teacher pay penalty studies. According to a late August EPI report, U.S. teachers in 2025 earned about 25% less than college graduates in other professions.

In its report, Brookings described the U.S. teacher pay gap as “striking” and said that one possible explanation for the disparity may be widening income gaps in the U.S.

The teacher pay gap seems to widen particularly for teachers who fall in the middle-to-high range of the salary scale, according to Brookings.

Teacher salaries and benefits typically account as the largest expense in school district budgets.

But in examining federal data from the National Center for Education Statistics, Brookings found that the share of public school expenditures on teacher salaries has dropped over the last 100 years. For instance, the report said, teacher salaries made up 57% of public school expenditures in 1919, compared to 26% in 2019.

Brookings added that one explanation for this trend is that school districts have chosen to increase their staffing capacity instead of increasing teacher salaries.

“If you go back 100 years, more than half of school spending went to teacher salaries. Where the data ends — just before the start of the COVID-19 pandemic — the fraction going to teacher salaries had fallen to a quarter,” Brookings said. “Basically, schools are spending more money, just not on teacher salaries.”

The latest findings from Brookings come as some education policy experts have suggested schools should lean into strategic teacher pay approaches — especially as districts have to tighten their budgets due to challenges from widespread enrollment declines. Strategic teacher pay policies can include increasing teacher salaries in hard-to-staff schools and subjects, as well as boosting pay for high-performing teachers.

The National Education Association reported in April that the average teacher salary was $74,495 during the 2024-25 school year. Despite that being a 3.5% increase from the previous school year, the nation’s largest teacher’s union said, the latest teacher pay figures reflect a 5% decline in national averages compared to a decade ago when adjusting for inflation.

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

The U.S. pre-K-12 public education system shrunk slightly, with fewer students, schools and school districts recorded in the 2023-24 school year compared to the 2021-22 school year, according to figures compiled by the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

For the 2023-24 school year, a slightly higher rate of high school boys — compared to high school girls — were enrolled in Algebra I, calculus, and physics. Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Public school pre-K-12 enrollment dipped slightly from 50 million in 2021-22 to 49.7 million in 2023-24. Of those students in 2023-24, 44% were White, 30% were Hispanic or Latino of any race, 15% were Black, 6% were Asian and 5% were two or more races.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. That's a slight increase from 2021-22, when 14% of students were served by IDEA and 3% of students were served under Section 504.

Twelve percent of students were English learners in 2021-22, compared to 11% in 2023-24.

#6974. DOL final rule revamps antidiscrimination requirements for apprenticeships

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the time, then-Acting Secretary of Labor Julie Su said in a press release, “Equity and job quality have marked the most successful Registered Apprenticeship programs for workers and employers alike. This proposed rule codifies the Department of Labor’s strong commitment to these principles.”

#6955. Inside the Lavish Estates Where The Gilded Age Was Filmed

These locations are straight out of 1880s high society.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Why should the Brits have all the fun? The writer of Downton Abbey, Julian Fellowes, went stateside with the HBO series The Gilded Age, which tells the story of New York City high society in the 1880s amid the halcyon days of the industrial boom. The series explores the tension between old-money elites and their escalating feud with the nouveau riche neighbors who threaten the established social order. Meanwhile in real life, powerhouse families like the Vanderbilts and the Carnegies left their opulent footprints on Newport, Rhode Island, in the form of their famously ostentatious and absolutely gargantuan “summer cottages.” Circumstances which lend themselves to set-design magic.

In honor of season four of The Gilded Age, which premiered November 1, we’re revisiting the real-life settings that have helped bring the series to life. The social alliances may shift, but the architecture remains a dependable scene-stealer. Below, tour 21 properties that rank among the East Coast’s architectural crown jewels, from historic house museums to lavish mansions.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s ballroom and George Russell’s bedroom, as well as the billiard room.

These so-called dollar princesses and their families lived in Manhattan and spent their summers in Newport, Rhode Island. Of course, these industry magnates didn’t consider their infrequent visits (some only came a few weeks in a year) as a reason to exercise restraint. The mammoth Breakers mansion is one of four so-called summer “cottages” (read: castles) that were completed just blocks from each other in a building boom between 1892 and 1902. The mansion was designed by Richard Morris Hunt in the Renaissance Revival style, as a less-than-subtle symbol of the Vanderbilt family’s social and financial preeminence in the Gilded Age. It was architectural one-upmanship that is considered the grandest of all the manses, featuring ornate ballrooms, extensive art collections, manicured gardens, and the latest (then) in modern technology, including electric lights. It has 70 rooms, an interior using marble imported from Italy and Africa, and a library mantel purchased from a château in France.

“When you walk into the dining room of The Breakers, with its painting of the goddess Aurora on the ceiling 50 feet above you or see 500,000 cubic feet of marble in a variety of different hues, you can’t help but be awestruck by the incredible wealth of these Gilded Age tycoons,” says Trudy Coxe, the chief executive officer and executive director of The Preservation Society of Newport County, which has under its stewardship six of the grand mansions featured in the series.

The preservation society worked closely with the filming crew to ensure the safety of the objects and spaces. “We had long discussions about the billiard table at The Breakers,” she explains. “It could not be removed, but it was important to the scene being filmed. Rather than moving it, we worked with their props department to put an alternative covering on the table to simulate an actual game of pool.”

In the series, the Breakers’ neo–Italian Renaissance great hall and music room are featured as Bertha Russell’s (played by Carrie Coon) ballroom, which we see in the final ball scene. George Russell (Morgan Spector) also discusses business while playing billiards in the mansion’s massive billiard room.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s bedroom in the Russell residence

Marble House was built from 1888 to 1892 as a summer cottage for Alva and William Kissam Vanderbilt. It was also designed by Richard Morris Hunt, but in the Beaux-Arts style. For an American house, it was unparalleled in opulence, with a temple-front portico resembling that of the White House, which had been completed 92 years earlier in 1800 (lofty, no?).

Mrs. Russell’s bedroom, built on a soundstage in Bethpage, New York, was inspired by real-life socialite Alva Vanderbilt’s bedroom in the Marble House. The French Louis XV bed was painted and reupholstered in salmon-pink velvet, and the custom bedding used 300 yards of fabric that have been draped, cinched, and swagged.

And that’s just the textiles. Most of the room’s furniture is refurbished antique and everything in the room has been painted to match. Sometimes, Shaw notes, it was difficult to paint a lovely wooden antique piece. “The scenic artists would look at us and say, ‘You really want me to paint over the chestnut? Are you sure?’” says Shaw. “Yes.”

Consuelo Vanderbilt’s (Alva and William’s child) monochrome red bedroom in the mansion also serves as George Russell’s bedroom in the show.

Features as: The exterior of Sylvia Chamberlain’s house

Rosecliff was built for Nevada silver heiress Theresa Fair Oelrichs, whose father, James Graham Fair, was one of the four partners in the Comstock Lode. The famed architectural firm McKim, Mead & White was commissioned to design the mansion, which was modeled after the Grand Trianon, the garden retreat of French kings at Versailles. The French Baroque Revival gem was designed for entertaining at a grand scale—and it certainly fulfilled its purpose. Fair Oelrichs would become known as one of the three great hostesses of Newport.

The white front facade of this mansion, which recently got a $7.4 million renovation, is a stand-in for Sylvia Chamberlain’s (played by Jeanne Tripplehorn) house. You’ll see it in Episode 7, when Tom Raikes (Thomas Cocquerel) pays a visit.

Features as: The servant kitchen and the real-life Sarah Berwind’s bedroom as parts of the Russell residence

The Elms is a Classical Revival dwelling that was completed in 1901. Architect Horace Trumbauer designed it for the coal baron Edward Julius Berwind, taking inspiration from the 18th-century d’Asnières French château. It also houses an impressive collection of paintings and tapestries, as well as the latest technology of its age.

In The Gilded Age Seasons 1 and 2, The Elms stands in as part of the Russells’ lavish residence, including scenes in the kitchen and Bertha Russell’s bedroom. But it truly steps into the spotlight in Season 3, when it hosts a dazzling ball. And as a long-awaited confession of love looms (we won’t spoil from whom), the opulent Newport mansion proves to be the perfect setting for high drama and high society.

Each guest is granted a cinematic entrance, illuminated by a golden glow that bathes the grand façade—a new addition this season, as electricity is now becoming available. “The house’s interiors would not have been electrified yet, but people were beginning to use it for parties,” Shaw says. “That would mean they would have to have their own generator installed somewhere on the property to light up those lights.” To accomplish this, the crew went to specialty shops that make reproduction bulbs. “The original bulbs were much bigger than what we have in a household today,” Shaw adds. “Some of the lightbulbs we used were 10 inches in diameter, which is how big they were then.”

Features as: The exterior is used for Caroline Astor’s Beechwood house; the boardinghouse is used for a scene with Oscar van Rhijn; and another room serves as Agnes van Rhijn’s bedroom

Completed in 1852, Château-sur-Mer is one of the first grand Bellevue Avenue mansions of the Gilded Age in Newport, built by architect Seth C. Bradford for William Shepard Wetmore. Its interiors were designed by Ogden Codman Jr., who co-authored Edith Wharton’s Decoration of Houses. The mansion was host to many major parties, including the Fête Champêtre, an elaborate country picnic for more than 2,000 guests in 1857, as well as a debutante ball for Edith Wetmore in 1889. Wetmore’s son, George Peabody Wetmore, went on to have a distinguished political career as the governor of Rhode Island and, later, as a U.S. senator.

In The Gilded Age, the home’s exterior was used for Caroline Astor’s (Donna Murphy) Beechwood House. Features as: The office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes

Hunter House was constructed in 1748 at the harborfront near the north end of the Newport Historic District at a time when the city was a bustling seaport in the British empire. The house was owned by wealthy merchants and overlooked the wharves that serviced their trading ships. It’s one of the finest examples of Colonial architecture in Newport, according to the Preservation Society. Inside, its resident families furnished the house with decorative arts produced by expert craftsmen, much of which has been preserved today. Such a luxurious lifestyle, however, depended upon the labor of enslaved people of African descent; recent research by the Preservation Society has identified at least 13 enslaved individuals who lived and worked at Hunter House. Today’s Hunter House honors their perseverance and incalculable contributions to Newport’s past.

Features as: The Astor family summer home

Belcourt was completed in 1894 for Oliver Hazard Perry Belmont, a prominent American socialite and coachman who served in the U.S. House of Representatives. Like many of the Newport mansions, the Château-esque structure was completed by architect Richard Morris Hunt; it was designed to mimic the hunting lodge of Versailles, built for Louis XIII of France, but with a special ask to accommodate Oliver’s collection of horses and automobiles. In 1896, Oliver gave the house to his second wife, the former Alva Vanderbilt, who designed the neighboring Newport mansion Marble House. Alva used Belcourt as a gathering place for the elite of society, hosting lavish parties and balls and attracting notable figures in politics, business, and the arts.

Built in 1867 by prolific Boston tea and china merchant Robert M. Cushing, the Ledges was originally the summer home of the Cushing family. The International Tennis Hall of Fame and its perfectly preserved grass courts are in the Newport Casino, a National Historic Landmark property. The historic seven-acre estate was commissioned by New York Herald publisher James Gordon Bennett as a social club for the elite summer residents of Newport. The Newport News boasted three days after it opened in July 1880: “It is doubtful if a livelier place can be found.” Today, the space is recognized as the official hall of fame for the sport of tennis globally. In the eighth episode of The Gilded Age Season 1, the characters are seen promenading around the piazza that overlooks what is known today as Horseshoe Court. Toward the end of the 19th century, Colonel Elliott Fitch Shepard, a lawyer who founded the New York State Bar Association in 1876, purchased more than 500 acres on a hill high above the Hudson River north of Tarrytown. The result was a 75-room Victorian structure that mimicked 18th-century Italian villa architecture. Think: hand-carved mahogany paneling, a grand stairway near the front entrance, high patterned ceilings, and elaborate cornice work. Unfortunately, Shepard died before it was completed in 1893, and it passed into the hands of William Rockefeller and Frank Vanderlip. In an age of encroaching AI, designers are turning to heritage looks to create bespoke interiors with personality. That may be one reason decorative marbling is having a moment.

"People are tired of flat, safe walls,” says designer Cara Woodhouse. “After years of greige, clients want surfaces with a point of view, and marbling has an artisanal history behind it. It's also flexible: it can read painterly, graphic, or soft depending on the colorway."

Its versatility is part of the appeal. “It doesn't necessarily have to be traditional or even executed on paper,” adds designer Sasha Bikoff. “It's really about the movement of color and the organic, unpredictable patterns that emerge, which can translate beautifully across wallpaper, furniture, textiles, and decorative objects.”

The paper marbling technique has a long history. In 10th-century Japan, artisans created “floating ink” designs by placing ink on water and manipulating it as it moved. By the 15th century, the practice was known in Turkey as “the art of clouds,” before making its way to Venetian and Florentine bookbinders.

The painterly look is appealing because its undulating lines feel expressive and vibrant without harsh edges, says Jo Plant, chief creative officer at Pooky, which is a brand well-known for its marbled lampshades.

Designers are bringing marbling into all kinds of interiors, from murals and upholstery to lampshades and even sink skirts. Analisse Taft-Gersten, cofounder of the 1818 Collective, used billowy marbled silk to elevate a powder room. And studios like Rule of Three are experimenting with the technique in floral fabrics that resemble vines.

Should you need further convincing, Woodhouse puts it best: "Marbling is pattern without repetition. No two passes are the same, so a wall reads more like art than wallpaper. It brings depth and a handmade quality to a room that might otherwise feel too finished."

Here are nine designer-approved ways to bring marbled motifs into your home.

Designer Michelle Fahmy of Haus of Meeshie likes things cheeky and playful. So it was a no-brainer to introduce groovy swirls into a Culver City pied-à-terre via a large-scale mural from Mitchell Black.

“I wanted something dramatic for the stairwell wall which bleeds into the living room and is the first thing you see when you walk in the door,” she says. “When I came across this wallpaper, it stopped me in my tracks! Usually, you see this paper marbling technique done on fabric or in smaller applications, like a lampshade, so this was unusual in scale and exactly the conversation starter I was looking for.”

For Bikoff, interior design should feel like a natural extension of a home’s architectural language. To bring her own sense of color, movement, and personality into a project within a Zaha Hadid building, she opted for Pierre Frey wallpaper with a liquid, dripping marble effect rather than a traditional, tightly swirled pattern.

“I love how the elongated, flowing lines create a sense of movement, almost as though the walls are melting or the colors are cascading down the surface,” Bikoff says. “Traditional marbling can feel very classical, whereas this interpretation feels more contemporary, psychedelic, and expressive.”

The dialogue between the wallpaper and Hadid's architecture is all about challenging conventional forms and embracing organic movement. “I wanted the room to feel immersive, almost like stepping inside a piece of abstract art,” she adds.

It was love at first sight. Marta St. Amour, interior designer and project manager at Braswell Design + Build was enchanted by the beautiful movement and layered tones of amber, soft blue, and sandy beige portrayed in the Lee Jofa Taplow wallpaper.

“The pattern became the inspiration for the bathroom's color palette, tying together the warm veining of Taj Mahal quartzite, rich caramel-colored shower tile, and soft blue tile behind the freestanding tub,” she says.

“We wanted the tub to feel like a statement piece, and the wallpaper's organic, flowing pattern complements its sculptural shape and warm metallic finish.”

To capture the spirit of the marbling technique but in a contemporary way, Bikoff opted for resin chairs with the telltale pattern in the above space.

“The way the colors blend and swirl through the resin gives them this incredible sense of depth and movement, almost like a liquid material that has been frozen in time,” she says.

Juxtaposing materials can challenge the perception of design. Here, the chairs feel like functional sculptures as they balance the visual richness of marble and the playful transparency of resin.

“They bring together color, texture, and fluidity while reinforcing the playful, futuristic spirit of the space."

If a full room of swirls is a bit much for you, settle on something smaller-scale, such as a lampshade. Pooky’s hand-marbled shades show off the dance of acrylic paint.

“Marbling is having a resurgence because we’re all craving craft, texture, and character in our homes,” says Plant. “People want pieces that feel human and handmade rather than mass-produced.” Adding a marbled shade is like decor candy that can add just the right amount of maximalism.

What makes the marbling technique unique is that the patterns are inherently organic, and so they lend themselves to pairing well with other organic shapes. Taft-Gersten anchored this powder room with a silk sink skirt featuring Rule of Three fabric that almost looks like it was kissed by bubbles.

“There’s a rhythm to it, but also an irregularity that keeps your eye moving; it never feels rigid,” Taft-Gersten says. The print also echoes the convex pebble mirrors above.

I'd call it soft modern with an edge: rounded forms, blush tones, and a pattern that keeps it from going saccharine. The swirl does the work so the furniture can stay simple and sculptural."

The look of poured paint over a rug is one that will surely raise an eyebrow. Floor_Story designer Bekah Smith was experimenting with duct tape when she stumbled upon unexpected patterns that look like creases and shadows.

“There’s a similar sense of spontaneity to marbling, where patterns emerge through experimentation rather than being entirely predetermined,” Smith says.

For those looking for a ripple effect without the psychedelic swirls, consider ceramics as a way to dabble in the style without being so literal. Henry Holland Studio employs a traditional Japanese ceramic technique, nerikomi, to create irregular earthenware marbled motifs.

Put another way, for every dollar Gartner expects to be spent on generative AI models this year, more than $52 will be spent on AI infrastructure.

It is a striking measure of how the economics of the AI boom are changing. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other models may be the most visible products of the generative AI era, but much of the money is going to the enormous buildout of servers, semiconductors, networks, cloud capacity, and other infrastructure required to support them.

"The buildout of AI data center capacity is the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken," John-David Lovelock, distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, said in announcing the forecast.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to increase 49.5% this year, from about $1.79 trillion in 2025 to $2.67 trillion in 2026. Infrastructure represents $1.484 trillion of that total. The next-largest categories are AI services, at $576.5 billion, and AI software, at $461.6 billion. Spending on AI agents and assistants is expected to reach $29.2 billion, only slightly more than the $28.3 billion Gartner forecasts for generative AI models.

The numbers also show just how quickly expectations are changing. In January, Gartner forecast $2.53 trillion in total AI spending for 2026, including $1.37 trillion for infrastructure. By May, those forecasts had risen to $2.60 trillion and $1.43 trillion, respectively. The latest forecast raises them again, to $2.67 trillion overall and $1.48 trillion for infrastructure.

Since January, Gartner has added roughly $143 billion to its 2026 AI spending estimate. About $118 billion of that increase, or roughly 83%, comes from a higher infrastructure forecast.

That is significant because infrastructure was already, by far, the largest component of Gartner's AI spending estimate. As the firm's expectations for the AI market have risen during 2026, most of the additional money has accumulated there.

The infrastructure category is broad. Gartner includes AI-optimized cloud infrastructure, servers, networking, AI processors, and devices. Demand for those systems remains strong despite rising memory prices, Lovelock said.

"The capacity growth from hyperscalers and service providers purchasing AI-optimized servers will continue to be the largest single area of spending," he said.

AI is changing which human capabilities matter most at work, yet employees are reporting that some of those same capabilities are being eroded, according to a new study from IBM. Critical thinking, judgment and the ability to evaluate AI output sit at the center of that tension.

The IBM Institute for Business Value study, "Designing the Thinking Organization," is based on two surveys conducted with Oxford Economics from April through June 2026. IBM surveyed 1,500 CHROs and senior executives responsible for workforce strategy across 21 geographies and 23 industries, along with 8,800 full-time employees across 28 countries.

The findings extend beyond HR. More than half of employees surveyed — 52% — said their assigned tasks changed during the past year as AI reshaped day-to-day responsibilities. #6909. Report: AI's Productivity Gains in Science Tempered by Time Spent Validating Outputs

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. The authors describe this as a verification tax associated with the high value science places on reliable and correct results.
