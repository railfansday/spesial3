EPI points to several factors that are negatively impacting districts’ abilities to pay more competitive wages for school bus drivers. Those include the expiration of federal COVID-19 emergency relief funds, ongoing federal and state funding cuts, and the expansion of private school choice.

Additionally, increased costs like higher diesel prices are straining public school budgets, the report said.

#7177. How can districts keep CTE programs relevant as tech evolves?

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The rapid pace of change can make it difficult for even employers to have clear answers for workplace readiness, an industry expert says.

How can leaders of high school career and technical education programs ensure those offerings remain relevant in preparing students to adapt in a rapidly changing workforce?

“That question is one everyone is trying to answer right now. I’m not sure there is one clear answer,” said Alisha Hyslop, chief policy, research and content officer at the Association for Career and Technical Education.

Hyslop said the main piece of advice for educators is to work closely with businesses and industries to ensure programs are preparing students with the skills they’ll need to be successful — and that’s probably the same, at least on the surface, as it would have been 10 or 20 years ago.

But there are important distinctions in 2026, Hyslop said.

Because technology is changing so rapidly, employers don’t always have a clear answer for workplace readiness approaches, she said.

Hyslop said ACTE is also hearing that students need to understand basic, foundational artificial intelligence skills and how to use them. Additionally, students should continue to "build up durable skills," which is "really critical," she said.

Despite employers’ own lack of clarity at times, regular conversations should definitely continue, Hyslop said.

“There should be a give-and-take between the CTE educators, and folks working on curriculum and instruction in CTE, and the employer community,” she said. And although technology will continue to rapidly evolve, “students are still going to need critical thinking and communications skills, and the ability to work in teams, and evaluate information — and all of those foundational skills that apply across technologies.”

CTE programs focused on information technology are changing the most rapidly, although Hyslop said she has seen plenty of research to show that across the spectrum of career fields, new AI tools are being implemented and deployed. One example is the use of the technology in the agriculture field to monitor soil health and predict crop growth, she said.

“We had some examples from a school in Bentonville [Ark.] that was using AI in health science programs. They had created a meal planning app for mothers with gestational diabetes, or for pregnant women who could get answers to specific questions; those were joint projects between healthcare and technology students," Hyslop said. "There were lots of applications in digital media, and lots of ethical considerations that students are exploring as they go through that process.”

While some industries are more impacted by the advent of AI and other changes in the workplace than others, at first glance, people might think it’s mainly an issue in technology classes, Hyslop said. “And that is not the case. Because we’re seeing applications across career areas, once students get into the workplace.”

During a panel discussion at a recent ACTE conference focused on the use of AI in CTE programs, Hyslop said, students emphasized how important it was that they learned foundational skills first.

One student pointed out the approach of using a calculator to solve math problems, she recalled. “But that’s not how we teach students in kindergarten. We teach them basic mathematical functions first, and then introduce the calculator to do more complicated things.”

By learning the foundational level skills in CTE programs, they could evaluate the answers given by AI more competently, the students said. For instance, a student studying automotive mechanics could examine AI-generated instructions on how to fix cars.

“If you had no real understanding of those instructions, you wouldn’t know if it made a mistake,” she said. “That was a really important takeaway from that panel — that even students realized that AI, or whatever the next technology is, is still going to need people to instruct, evaluate and modify all of these technologies.”

Patricof asked me to come and talk about what I had learned about longevity and the possibility of the 100-year life. It included a former CEO of a Fortune 100 company, a vice chairman emeritus at one of the largest banks on the planet, a major real estate developer, and a man who has his name on one of the most prestigious health systems in New York. And those are just the ones who agreed to be identified—some asked to remain anonymous.

You get the idea: This was not an everyday group of 90-somethings but rather a gathering of highly accomplished, successful men who continue to thrive in a variety of industries and disciplines. That only made me more interested, though I imagined it might take some work to translate their habits and the ways they were spending their time into things more accessible to everyday people.

Luckily, I would turn out to be wrong about that.

As the men assembled in a private room at Patroon, a popular Manhattan restaurant, I saw that many of them already knew each other, some from as far back as grade school. As Patricof went over ground rules, including no talk of politics (both parties were represented) and no talk of sex (though Viagra did find its way into the conversation), they moved around the room, shaking hands, asking about wives and kids, and explaining what was keeping them busy.

That was my first clue that the behaviors that helped these men stay robust into their tenth decade had little to do with wealth. Research has shown that cultivating deep, healthy relationships is one of the best things you can do to increase your chances of having a long, healthy life. These men were living examples of what that looks like, and it’s free. (Though not if you have your lunches at Patroon.)

These men took care of three major aspects of their lives: health, head, and heart. First, they were committed to a fitness regimen and healthy eating habits. Second, they were highly engaged and curious individuals who valued a strong sense of purpose. Third, each of them had deep relationships with loved ones, whether a spouse, family, or friends.

You may notice that these are all universal virtues. They’re also things that can give you a sense of fulfillment if you’re only in your 80s, or 70s, or even younger. They aren’t solely investments in the future. You do them now, and they benefit you now. Which is a good feeling and precisely what makes longevity worth it in the first place. As one of the men said, “If today is the last day of my life, I’ve had a helluva run.”

“At 90, I tried boxing at a gym near my home in Harlem,” said George Hirsch, 92, before adding, proudly, that he went a full round. Hirsch was the longtime chairman of the New York Roadrunners running club. While he is no longer out doing road races, he is on a treadmill four or five days a week in a walk-run regimen. He supplements that with rowing, the elliptical machine, weights, and, apparently, the occasional prizefight, in a 60-minute routine.

For a gathering men who were 90 or older, this was a remarkably healthy group. It’s true they all have had access to the best health care, but none had problems with issues that come for everyone, like cognitive decline, diabetes, and heart disease. That word routine seemed to be the key. Routine, regimen—take your pick. These men make a point of committing to physical activity rather than just dabbling in it. One told me, “I think the secret to my life physically is that I’ve worked with the same trainer three days a week for 29 years,” adding that “even when I travel, I take my iPad and work out with him wherever I am.”

In some cases, these routines would be the envy of any younger man! The men had all played some type of sports when they were younger and carried that behavior deep into their adult lives. Since movement and exercise are the top natural medicines for a long, healthy life, these guys were on it. Activities included walking, weight training, stationary bicycling, swimming, pickleball, tennis, and downhill skiing. Two of them have a regular tennis game, and one of the oldest in the group is still an avid golfer. Most of them go to a gym or have a personal trainer. While a few admitted that they were slower as they got older—a sign of remarkable maturity in a man, to admit that—they were all active.

They are also conscious of what they eat. They agreed that they consume less food than in the past and drink very little, if at all. As Hirsch said, “I never drank much, maybe a beer or glass of wine with a meal. But I don’t drink anymore—it just doesn’t mean that much to me.”

Their attention to eating habits stood out. One thing that especially interested them was GLP-1’s, because of their impact on diet but also other potential benefits. As I shared what I’ve learned about longevity, including the frontiers of research on the topic, they asked question after question about the emergence of GLP-1’s and other classes of drugs that may enhance individual longevity. That kind of constant, engaged questioning was a hallmark of the group.

Only one of the men at the lunch defined himself as “retired.” The others said they were, at most, “semiretired.” While they are not necessarily working full throttle in what was their main career, to a man they all have very busy days—and that busyness, I found, is driven by a voracious curiosity, which is one of the best ways to strengthen and maintain brain health.

Ken Langone is a great example. He came from a working-class family in Queens and is still in awe of his good fortune. He’s taken it to mean that, as long as he’s able, he should be doing everything he can not to squander what he has. “I won big, and I can’t tell you how grateful I am,” he said, adding, “We should be giving, not taking.”

He is in his office three days a week and works from home two days a week, engaged in a public board, three private boards, his family office, and other investments. His commitment to public health is incredibly important to him; he serves as chair emeritus of NYU Langone Health.

“At 91,” Langone says, “my biggest issue is boredom, sitting around with nothing to do.”

Jim Freund, also 91, was a high-powered attorney into his early 60s, did a stint as a mediator after that, and now has a robust life as a writer, photographer, and musician.

He’s written 20 fiction and nonfiction books and has produced 25 albums of music featuring him at the piano. For six years, he has circulated a monthly blog for more than 500 friends and colleagues that includes the details of various projects he’s working on—an essay or short fiction, a piece of music, a portfolio of photos.

One expression of this curiosity is that all the men want to keep current. They follow the news, are dedicated readers, and have a deep curiosity about emerging trends. They don’t just read print; they’re on their phones, Kindles, Audible—anywhere there’s action. And AI—they’re on ChatGPT like everyone else. Some even have digital assistants.

But being current doesn’t just mean trendspotting. It can also mean having hobbies and projects that force you to engage with the world as it is. All of these men had ambitious new endeavors in the works.

Hirsch now has a bimonthly podcast with the running legend Amby Burfoot called “Running: State of the Sport.” Dan Rose, 97, former president and chairman of Rose Associates, the company that built Pentagon City and One Financial Center in Boston, is writing a new book. Patricof and his wife are taking Yiddish lessons. At least one man reported that he was finally learning the piano.

For all of them, it is important that their activities, whatever they may be, connect to deeper meaning and purpose.

Larry Leeds, 97, the former CEO and chairman of the apparel company Manhattan Industries, now works at Buckingham Capital Management five days a week buying and selling securities. It allows him to keep up with trends and investments. But it’s far from the most important thing to which he’s dedicated his energy.

Leeds hopes the leading line in his obituary is the $27 million he raised to launch the Cary Leeds Center for Tennis and Learning in the South Bronx in 2015. Dedicated to athletic instruction and academic enrichment for underprivileged youth—health and head—the center is also an expression of the most important thing in Leeds’s life: his family. It is named after his son, who competed in six US Opens and five Wimbledon championships before dying at the age of 45.

Sitting there in Patroon, I heard stories of how these men had committed to keeping themselves healthy and how their curiosity led them to continue to engage with the world at a point in their lives when they could just as easily have settled into insular routine. But it was the lunch itself that testified to their commitment to their hearts. Ninety years in, they were still making a point of getting together, speaking, laughing, and growing closer to other people.

Upon arriving, as we sat and introduced ourselves, Patricof explained that he wanted to do this lunch not only to learn from one another at this stage of life but also to simply enjoy socializing with peers—like many women do on a regular basis. Women live five to six more years than men; maybe it is their commitment to deep relationships, he suggested, that is one of the key factors of their greater longevity.

Women came up in another way too: Everyone attributed their health and longevity to having a loving partner.

The majority of the men have had incredibly long marriages. Nine of the 12 had been married for more than 60 years. Two had been married for 70 years. Then there was Freund, who joked that he, too, has been married for 60 years—just not all to the same woman. (He is married to his second wife, and they are at 41 years and counting.)

They all also have children and grandchildren, whom they are in touch with more than a few times a week—something any of us who are parents can do.

Rose has a unique ritual. “We have a family video conference every Sunday night with family from all parts of the country. It’s life enhancing and a time to connect and give everyone a chance to share accomplishments and disappointments with each other,” he explained.

At this stage of their lives, many have lost longtime friends, but they all strive to make new friends, some young, some old. In fact, there’s already a plan in place to replicate the lunch so that a bunch of other 90-somethings can be invited into the conversation. Here’s the thing I noticed about this: What got these guys excited about another lunch wasn’t simply that they enjoyed each other’s company or having a laugh with peers about Viagra. They were also inspired by one other. That must count for something.

I walked away inspired, too, and in awe of how these men live. They have to walk a line that most of us don’t yet, and maybe never will. Part of being alive—especially for this group—is thinking about the future, trying to figure out what’s next, planning another pursuit, but now they all admit that their horizon of looking out, say, 25 years is no longer a reality.

In the face of that tension, they’ve found a way to be optimistic about their future while at the same time savoring the present. None complained about getting older. None talked about their aches and pains. Yes, they faced growing limitations, but they all seemed to take them in stride.

“I do my best to love my life and try to have the same intensity and diligence that I had in my younger years,” said Leeds.

Langone said, “When I can no longer take care of myself and I’m totally dependent on someone else, then that’s the time to go,” adding that “God has his plan.”

And Patricof has his. He’s already publicly stated that he plans to live to 114, which would be 22 more years. I pointed out that the world’s oldest verified living person, Ethel Caterham in the UK, just turned 117, and I challenged Alan to break that record. He gladly told me that he accepts the challenge, and I don’t doubt he’ll make a real run at it. I have to admit I have an ulterior motive: I look forward to my lunch with centenarians. And supercentenarians.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

He thought about getting a PS5 for EA Sports College Football in 2024 but ultimately passed.This guy doesn’t pay attention to video games—or he only pays attention to the odd AAA release—and he is expecting to pay $400 for the key to experiencing this generation’s only game that matters. He might not even know that the game itself is going for $80, a rarity that only Nintendo has tried, with Mario Kart World. When he walks up to the counter, this hypothetical man will be paying nearly $900 to be able to play GTA VI. And the worst part? He’s not hypothetical. Starting Thursday, there are going to be thousands of real people in this very situation. Here’s why and what you can do—can you do anything?—about it.

GTA VI has taken over a decade to make (that’s longer than it took Rome to build the Colosseum), and estimates put the budget somewhere north of $1 billion. Rockstar has pushed back, though, with head of publishing Jennifer Kolbe telling Bloomberg that the rumor of the budget surpassing the $1.5 billion budget of the Burj Khalifa is just that—a rumor. Still, it’s not a stretch to call this the biggest media release of all time.

It’s also the biggest test of gaming’s resiliency to price hikes—price hikes that (like everything else, it seems) tie back to AI.

“When [EA Sports College Football] came back a couple of years ago, there was an influx of people who hadn’t bought a console yet,” said Mat Piscatella, a video game industry analyst and advisor at Circana. “Those folks at the time mostly chose either the Xbox Series S or the PlayStation 5 Digital, so the least expensive options to get in.”

The main cause is that the multibillion-dollar gaming industry is in a crisis, a RAM crisis. The RAMageddon, or RAMpocalypse, is a global supply shortage of memory chips that is primarily caused by the post-2024 boom in generative AI. Cleanview, a market-intelligence company, reported that the total number of data centers rose from 361 in 2016 to 1,278 in 2026, with more than five hundred more being built. Data centers are giant warehouses full of computer systems, complex computer systems requiring unprecedented amounts of RAM.

RAM stands for random-access memory. Memory, in this instance, doesn’t mean storage space. RAM is your computer’s “short-term memory,” essentially how many different things your computer’s brain can process at once. The more RAM your computer has, the more it can handle simultaneously. And according to JP Morgan Global Research, prices have risen more than 400 percent in the past two years.

That gets us back to GTA VI and the world of video game consoles. Modern games, with their massive open worlds, highly detailed graphics, and complex physics simulations, require a ton of RAM. As computer parts become scarcer and more expensive, the price of a console goes up. Thus, consoles that—besides the higher-tier PlayStation 5 Pro—have not had meaningful updates in half a decade are now hundreds of dollars more expensive than they were at launch.

Because contrary to what people know about the old gaming business models, the machines themselves still need to turn a profit. It didn’t use to be this way. In the PS2 era, consoles were sold at a loss, with games being the moneymaker. Things changed, audiences grew and moved elsewhere, and—somewhere between microtransactions and Xbox Game Pass—platform holders needed (or preferred) to make money on hardware again. So if a console is more expensive to manufacture, Sony and Microsoft aren’t going to eat that price. Consumers are seeing that increase directly, perhaps more directly and more acutely than we’ve ever seen any increase in technology prices.

Here’s our advice: Don’t wait. The AI push shows no signs of slowing down, and it’s likely that this RAM pipeline bottleneck won’t fix itself for a year or two. This holiday season is getting underway with a supply and demand squeeze similar to what we saw in 2020, when these consoles originally launched.

If you can swing it, you’ll probably want to buy a new console, but the PlayStation 5 Pro is already impossible to find brand-new. It might come down to shopping for renewed and refurbished consoles. These give you a very slight discount, but really they just give you a console that you can plug in and fire up GTA VI on. Welcome to the future of console gaming: refurbished six-year-old consoles.

#7034. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.
