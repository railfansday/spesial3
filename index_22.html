The student population plummeted by 17.5% over the next decade and is forecast to continue decreasing through at least the 2030-31 school year, according to district data.

Now, to stay financially healthy, the district is taking a comprehensive approach that includes consolidating and closing some school campuses, cutting at least 1,000 staff positions, and implementing other measures to save money. They found that international enrollment declined 9% at surveyed institutions this fall, driven by a 15% drop in new foreign students and a 15% decrease in international graduate students.

Nearly 9 out of 10 surveyed colleges said the visa process has hurt foreign enrollment, and 70% blamed travel restrictions. Additionally, 60% cited competition from other countries for international students.

“There are more countries actively investing in and recruiting international students,” Mirka Martel, IIE’s head of research, evaluation and learning, said during a press call last week. “As a result, this often means that there are more destination opportunities.”

Just over half of the surveyed colleges that reported growth in new international student enrollment attributed the gains to recruitment efforts and campus athletics programs. Another 44% cited outreach efforts to foreign students who had already applied and been admitted.

Surveyed colleges also reported recruiting international students already within the U.S. Over half, 57%, said they reached out to foreign students studying at U.S. high schools, and 48% sought out international undergraduates for graduate programs.

The countries the U.S. has traditionally relied on for a steady stream of international students — namely, India and China — are sending fewer citizens to study here, according to the broader Open Doors report.

While India retained the top spot, sending 339,435 students in 2025-26, that’s a 6.5% decline from the year prior. China, whose college student population in the U.S. has ticked steadily downward since the pandemic, sent 254,458 students. That’s a 4.3% decrease from 2024-25.

Despite overall enrollment declines, the Open Doors report found an increase in Optional Practical Training participation. The program allows student visa holders to work in their field of study in the U.S. for up to three years following graduation. In 2025-26, 335,785 foreign students took part in OPT, up 14.1% from the previous year.

Martel said that the growth in OPT over the past four years is due to the post-pandemic rebound in international graduate students. Those students are still making their way through the OPT pipeline, she said.

However, early indicators suggest OPT participation could soon suffer declines as well. IIE’s preliminary fall poll, conducted in October, found that OPT students declined 6% year over year across surveyed colleges.

#6944. Just Turn the Heat On? That Might Be Why Your Skin Suddenly Feels So Dry

A few weeks ago, I rented a big cabin outside of the city with friends. It was the first cold weekend in New York, the kind where you might actually need to throw on the heavy jackets that were too much for those 55-degree fall days. Nestled in this mountain house, we cozied up around a fire in fleece sweatshirts and fuzzy blankets. Before bed, we turned the heat on. I slept cozy like a baby in the womb, but immediately upon waking, my skin felt tight and dry. I coated myself in moisturizer, but it was no use. My face—which is usually oily and acne-prone—was dull and rough, like it’d never produced a drop of oil in its 29 years.

At first, I thought it was a sign of the times. I’m about to turn 30, and my skin’s just getting drier. But when I headed back to Brooklyn, where my stuffy apartment produces enough natural heat on its own to not need the radiator cranked up, my skin went back to normal.

I’ve heard of this phenomenon before—when your skin gets dehydrated once you turn on the heat in your house—but this was my first experience. And I was determined to get to the bottom of it before my radiator goes on for good and the winter uglies take over. Two dermatologists weighed in—and it looks like this isn’t necessarily preventable but totally treatable.

“Indoor heating lowers the relative humidity in the air, which makes moisture evaporate from your skin more quickly,” says dermatologist Annette Czernik, MD. “Our skin naturally produces oils that help protect it and keep moisture in, but sometimes that's not enough when the air is really dry.” You might apply moisturizer and feel like you didn’t even do anything, she adds.

You’re also not always in the clear if you have oily skin, says Dr. Czernik. “A lot of people don't realize that your skin can be oily and dehydrated at the same time,” she explains. “This is because your skin can still produce oil while losing moisture from the dry air.”

You may also feel tightness, roughness, stinging, flaking, and even itching. “As your skin loses more moisture, its protective barrier can become weaker,” says Dr. Czernik, hence why you can experience some irritation as your skin isn’t as strong enough to fight it off. The air can also make your skin look dull or even feel more sensitive, since it’s working so hard to hold onto the moisture it needs, explains Dr. Czernik. “In more severe cases, you might experience redness, peeling, or even small cracks in your skin,” she adds.

Naturally, with colder air outside, your skin might get drier too, only exacerbating the issue. “When the temperature drops outside, the air usually holds less moisture,” says Dr. Czernik. “Then, when you go indoors, you're exposed to heated air that can also be very dry.” You’re basically losing moisture on both sides, no matter what you do. “Going back and forth between the cold outdoors and warm indoors can make that dryness more noticeable,” she adds. “This is why someone might have perfectly normal skin during the summer but struggle with dryness all winter.”

“When you turn the heat on, the air inside your home can become very dry,” explains dermatologist Ariel Ostad, MD. And one of the easiest ways to add moisture back into the air is a humidifier. “This can help slow down how quickly your skin loses water throughout the day or night,” Dr. Ostad adds.

He recommends getting a bedside humidifier, like the Canopy Bedside Humidifier, for your bedroom and running it while you’re sleeping. “You may notice that your skin feels less tight and dry when you wake up in the morning,” he says. “It can also help with dry lips and irritated skin.” But remember to clean it regularly, as bacteria and mold can build up in your device.

“If your skin starts getting drier, I would first look at the products you're using every day,” says Dr. Ostad. Both Dr. Ostad and Dr. Czernik recommend switching to a thicker moisturizer. Ideally, find one that both moisturizes (meaning it contains ingredients that pull water into your skin, like squalane, hyaluronic acid, panthenol, and glycerin) and seals in hydration (aka ingredients that act as a physical barrier to reduce moisture loss, including ceramides, oils, butters, and silicones). But you could also opt for two moisturizers nightly. I prefer to use a lightweight moisturizer, then follow it up with a barrier cream.

“It's also a good idea to apply your moisturizer right after washing your face or getting out of the shower,” adds Dr. Czernik. If your skin is still damp, even better. This helps pull all that water into your skin (which helps plump it up and decrease dullness), plus it eliminates time for moisture to evaporate from your skin.

Just as hot air can pull water from your skin, so can hot water, explains Dr. Czernik. It doesn’t mean you need to do a daily cold plunge, but opt for mild to warm water temperatures, as scalding water can also increase skin irritation.

Some of the products in your routine could actually be making your dryness worse. Put down all the strong exfoliants, whether it’s your salicylic acid cleanser, an acid-based peel pad, or a retinol serum.

Instead, Dr. Ostad suggests using a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser “that won't strip your skin of its natural oils.” And if you love your exfoliants, you don’t have to ditch them completely, he adds, but you might want to cut back to once or twice per week at most. “You don't need to completely change your skincare routine, but making a few small adjustments can really help during the colder months,” he says.

“If your skin is just a little dry, you can usually manage it at home with a good moisturizer,” says Dr. Ostad. “However, if the dryness continues for a few weeks despite changing your skincare routine, I would recommend seeing a dermatologist.” If you notice your skin becomes extremely itchy or irritated, that’s a good sign to see a doctor too. “Cracking, bleeding, or painful peeling are signs that something more serious could be going on,” adds Dr. Ostad.

Really, it boils down to a few things. Most importantly, in my mind, are durability, power, and video storage, but things like video quality, smart home compatibility, and motion detection also play a role.

Video quality - 1080p on a doorbell is fine. 2K on a security camera gets the job done. 2.5k or 3K video is where you want to be. 4k video is nice to have, but honestly it’s a bit overkill.

Motion detection - Most cameras are just OK at this. Brands claim their cameras are smart and can tell the difference between you and the UPS guy, but often that's a lie. A ponytailed man in head-to-toe denim orbits me a third, then a fourth time in quick succession, like his eyes are twin asteroids and my waist a powerful gravitational force. When he stops, suddenly, he calls out, “Thirty-two!” and asks, “Button or zipper?”

I should’ve expected this sort of wizardry when I entered Momotaro Jeans Aoyama, a Tokyo outlet of the highly esteemed Japanese denim purveyor. Though it’s housed on the second floor of a mall just a three-quarter-mile walk from overstimulating, people-packed Shibuya, this tiny shop feels tranquil, purposeful, reverent. Dark indigo pants are folded and piled neatly in wooden cubbies along the walls; thick jackets hang gently from floating racks. Such a scene differs greatly from my earliest jeans-shopping memory.

In my early teens, on an Easter Sunday trip into New York, my mother shooed me into the Times Square Levi’s to buy my first big-boy pair. I never wore jeans. I attended Catholic school for much of my youth, so most mornings I was stuffed into stiff, ugly khakis. In rebellion against forced formality, I’d sport sweatpants or basketball shorts outside school hours.

Naturally, this appalled my fashionable mother, who couldn’t have lived with herself if she dropped her teenage son off at coed social outings wearing swishy Adidas track pants. But why was her remedy to hit a retail store in the busiest neighborhood in America on a holiday weekend? Trying on fur coats in hell would be comparably pleasant.

We dodged shoppers yanking slim cuts off floor displays and dug through unkempt mounds of unfolded denim until Mom found a few sizes in a few washes and forced me into the back changing room to try ’em all on. Of course, I huffed and puffed. Of course, I whinged and complained. But I left with a pair of medium-wash straight legs that at least wouldn’t look embarrassing on a first date.

In the nearly two decades since, as my interest in menswear has graduated from begrudging to professional, mass-market clothing has plunged in quality but spiked in price. Consumers are faced with a harsh reality when buying new: Either replace your decaying polyester-blend garments every few years or buy pricier, sturdier stuff from reputable manufacturers. I diverted toward the latter path a few years ago when I purchased a $150 heavyweight cotton hoodie from American Giant. (Though that may not seem like much to an Esquire reader, it was quite the jump for a J. Crew Factory sales-rack goblin like me.)

I learned of raw, selvedge denim a year later, when a buddy who’s into menswear sent me a Reddit post depicting a pair of jeans standing up all on their own. This was the beauty of raw Japanese denim, my friend told me, which is manufactured in the spirit of heritage American workwear from a time when durability was paramount. Heavyweight. Premium dye. Selvedge, meaning it’s woven on old-school looms from high-quality cotton. (You can also buy cheaper, significantly less impressive Japanese denim, but we’re talking about the reputable specialists here.) I was intrigued until he mentioned how many hundreds of dollars they cost thanks to import fees and general U.S. financial fuckery. But if I were ever on a trip to Japan at a time when the U.S. dollar was stronger than the yen? Maybe I could swing a pair.

In September, I was invited on a work trip to Tokyo, and finally my chance presented itself. When I landed at Narita International Airport, all I could think about was trying on stiff, uncomfortable jeans—talk about a different mindset from that of teenage me in Times Square.

After a week of tight itineraries, I finally sneaked away on my very last day and made my way past the stanchions lining the perimeter of the Momotaro shop. I read online that some folks wait up to 30 minutes to enter; luckily, there is no queue when I arrive.

A handful of employees stand silently behind a broad counter, one of them the man with the ponytail, who approaches me when he sees me checking out the brand’s #100 Standard Straight jeans in 14.7-ounce denim, which is a measurement of weight per square yard of fabric. Instead of asking for my size, he does the circling bit described earlier. I reply to his follow-up question with “zipper,” and he shows me to a narrow changing room toward the back of the space and asks me to remove my shoes. Japanese etiquette. He arrives with two pairs of the very jeans I was checking out. I try them both on, but they don’t feel quite right.

“Anything thicker?” I ask, trying to overcome the language barrier between us. My Japanese is rudimentary; his English is solid in bursts but not completely fluent.

He returns with a straight-cut pair from Momotaro’s classic line that’s 15.7 ounces, but it has a button fly, which I struggle mightily to close. Alas, there are no zippered pairs of this kind left. These classic button flies are a 32, but when I return to the standard zippers, I notice he has accidentally brought me a 31—way, way too tight in the crotch—and a 33, a hair too loose.

And just like that, I’m ready to walk out a satisfied customer. This is how I’m used to buying jeans, after all. I shouldn’t wash them too often, but when I do, I should fasten everything and turn them inside out. Oh, and keep them separate from other clothes, because the indigo will definitely run. That’s part of the fun; the jeans are designed to fade handsomely over time based on how and when I wear them. A personalized pair, if you will. Don’t ever dry them in a machine, though. Take ’em out, smooth the creases, and hang to dry in a cool, dark place.

He hands me a little box with a lifetime-guarantee card and explains that if I’m ever back in Japan and need a repair, they’ve got me covered.

A second employee guides me across the hall to a second storefront, where payment and hemming take place. After I show my passport to receive the 10 percent tax reduction for tourists, I swipe my card. $211. That’s it?! By shopping here, I essentially chop a third off the price listed on the Momotaro U.S. website. I’m told to return in 15 or so minutes once the hemming is done, so I dash to a café downstairs for a matcha over which I reflect on my experience.

Whenever I buy anything over a Benjamin, I immediately get buyer’s remorse. That American Giant hoodie? I spent a good few days lamenting that the sleeves were slightly too long before I talked myself out of a return. And yet, when I pick up my Momotaro jeans once they’re ready—they’re bagged neatly and wrapped in plastic to protect them from the day’s ceaseless rain—I feel beyond cared for. When I finally return home and put them on, I don’t fear that I maybe should have purchased a smaller size in case they stretch—they’re not supposed to—because my jeans fit perfectly. They’re right in proportion with my body, sit higher than I’m used to but quite comfortably, and style well with a graphic tee and light jacket.

Maybe I’m shopping at the wrong stores at home. Maybe I need to raise my price floor to get that kind of service. Or maybe we’ve lost something here in the U.S. that’s now attainable only overseas. Maybe I need to get back on a plane to Tokyo ASAP and hit all the stores I missed—Hinoya, Japan Blue, the Flat Head—then take the shinkansen to Osaka and get myself some really thick boys from Iron Heart, where 25-ounce denim is the gold standard.

Or maybe I should just enjoy the jeans I have now, wear them in nicely over the next decade until they’re washed to sky blue and softer than a lamb’s ear. I can look forward to passing them down to whatever kids I have one day. It’ll save us a trip to the Times Square Levi’s.

#6904. New Industry Alliance to Work Toward Common Security Model for AI Agents

Okta has announced a coalition bringing together major cloud, security, data and application vendors to create a shared architecture for securing AI agents across enterprise environments.

The 12 founding members of the new Blueprint Alliance are AWS, CrowdStrike, Databricks, Docker, Google Cloud, Lovable, Okta, Proofpoint, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Wiz and Zscaler. GE Appliances and World Central Kitchen are participating as strategic advisors.

The effort expands an earlier Okta framework into an open, multi-vendor reference architecture intended to address a basic problem with enterprise AI agents: An agent can be created in one system, authenticated through another, access tools and data from several others, and be monitored by yet another security platform.

The Alliance is focused less on how agents are built and more on how organizations can govern them consistently across vendor boundaries.

The Blueprint Alliance architecture organizes agent security around four questions: Where are my agents? What can they do? What are they doing? How do I respond?

The reference architecture also covers development-related systems. Its whitepaper includes agent frameworks, code repositories, CI/CD environments, code assistants, tool registries, Model Context Protocol servers, and command-line tools among the components that may need to be discovered or governed.

That makes the initiative relevant to development teams deploying agents that interact with internal APIs, MCP tools, databases, SaaS applications, or other agents. The architecture calls for those connections and permissions to remain visible and controllable as agents move from development into production.

A major part of the Alliance's work will be testing how security information and controls can move between products from different vendors.

The initial architecture references several existing standards and protocols, including MCP for agent-to-tool interactions, the Open Cybersecurity Schema Framework for security telemetry, and the Shared Signals Framework and Continuous Access Evaluation Profile for exchanging risk information.

The goal is for one part of an enterprise stack to be able to detect a risk condition and pass that information to another control point that can act on it. Depending on the implementation, that could include revoking access or containing an agent whose behavior has changed.

The Alliance says members will build and test reference integrations and publish results as interoperability work progresses. Specific cross-vendor implementations and performance results were not documented in the launch announcement.

The Alliance's whitepaper recommends that organizations begin with foundational capabilities such as unified logging, telemetry, and agent discovery rather than attempting to deploy every control at once.

The architecture is available for community use, and the Alliance says it will continue evolving as members test integrations and additional participants become involved. Timelines for individual vendor integrations have not been published.

#6901. AI Platform Behind 12,000 E-mail Breaches Dismantled

The platform moved quickly. Since launching in February, EvilTokens had been tied to more than 12,000 compromised inboxes across over 10,000 organizations worldwide, according to Microsoft. Its victims included businesses and institutions in financial services, healthcare, construction, real estate, wholesale distribution, and higher education.

Microsoft and its partners seized 50 websites used to operate EvilTokens and disabled more than 150 additional domains supporting the service. The Metropolitan Police Service in the United Kingdom also arrested two men on Sept. 11 in connection with the alleged operation.

At the center of EvilTokens was an AI chatbot designed specifically for cybercrime. Once attackers gained access to an inbox, the chatbot could analyze messages, map relationships, identify employees involved in payments, and recommend ways to impersonate trusted contacts.

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

Business e-mail compromise has traditionally required attackers to manually comb through messages, learn how an organization handles payments, and find the right person to impersonate. However, EvilTokens simplified the entire process by automating much of that work.

The tools could quickly sort through a stolen inbox, summarize or translate messages, find invoices and wire-transfer discussions, and identify the people who moved money. EvilTokens could then draft messages that sounded like they came from the victim. It packaged all of this with mailbox access, phishing tools, management dashboards, and even customer support.

The service was sold through Telegram for a $1,500 startup fee, followed by a $500 monthly subscription. Microsoft's complaint also described a bulk e-mail tool that cost $600 a month and a more advanced e-mail delivery platform priced at $1,000 a month.

To break into a target, EvilTokens relied on device-code phishing. Victims were sent to a real Microsoft sign-in page and persuaded to enter an authentication code. The login process looked legitimate because it was, but the code quietly authorized a session controlled by the attacker.

That also made cleanup more complicated. The attackers stole OAuth tokens and authenticated sessions instead of passwords, so a password reset alone might not remove their access. Administrators also needed to revoke the stolen tokens and active sessions.

According to Microsoft's complaint,  EvilTokens used fake Microsoft 365 security notices and other branded templates to direct users into the device authentication flow. Once inside, attackers could use Microsoft Graph and other APIs to search and monitor a mailbox.

The company also found that AI played a role in creating EvilTokens itself. Investigators said much of the platform appeared to have been "vibe coded," with its developers using AI to quickly build and expand the service. EvilTokens reportedly drew on multiple AI models, including improperly integrated services from Groq and OpenAI.

The court authorized Microsoft to take control of or disable domains connected to the operation. Microsoft worked with Cloudflare, Coinbase, OpenAI, Railway, SpyCloud, the Shadowserver Foundation, and TRM Labs on the disruption. Health-ISAC joined the case because healthcare organizations were among the targets.

This marks the Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit's 40th court-authorized disruption and its first targeting an end-to-end, AI-enabled cybercrime service.

EvilTokens' infrastructure may be offline, but the model behind it is unlikely to disappear. The operation showed how AI can turn a stolen inbox into an immediately useful fraud playbook, allowing less-experienced criminals to launch attacks that once required considerable time and skill.

#6896. Redesigned Microsoft Copilot Will Build Apps and Work on Its Own

Micorosft has announced that it's redesigning Copilot, the company's all-encompassing AI assistant, so users can hand off complex tasks, build tools by describing what they need, and assign work to an agent that keeps going after they log off.

The new Copilot experience has three main parts: Home, Code, and Autopilot. It also brings Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into the Copilot app. For users, the pitch is simple: Start a task in one place and stay there as it grows from a quick question into a document, an app, or a longer-running project. Most of the new capabilities are still rolling out or in preview.

Home puts Chat and Copilot Cowork together. Chat remains the place for quick answers, research, and drafts. Cowork handles work a user wants to delegate, such as preparing a briefing or pulling together a response to a request for proposals. Microsoft said Copilot eventually will route requests to Chat, Cowork, or Code on its own.

Office in Copilot will allow users to create a Word document, Excel workbook, or PowerPoint deck without leaving that experience. The files remain editable in the regular Office apps, and colleagues can work on them at the same time. Someone building a presentation, for example, could draw on a workbook, draft the slides, and keep refining both files from within Copilot.

Code is aimed at people who need a tool but might never open a development environment. A user can describe a tracker, dashboard, app, or workflow in plain language and have Copilot build it. Microsoft calls these "small, purpose-built solutions." The feature uses the same underlying technology as GitHub Copilot and runs in a sandboxed environment. A separate Copilot Managed Runtime, now in preview, lets organizations host and share the resulting apps within Microsoft 365.

Then there is Autopilot, previously known as Scout. It is designed for work that does not end with a single response: following a discussion, checking in with stakeholders, or keeping a project moving over several days. "Give it a name, a role, and a goal," Microsoft said. Autopilot runs in the cloud and has its own identity, memory, and workspace in the customer's tenant. Microsoft said its actions are subject to permissions, auditing, and organizational controls.

Those longer-running tasks also change how customers pay for Copilot. In a separate pricing post, Microsoft said everyday work in Chat and Microsoft 365 apps will remain covered by a per-user subscription. Cowork, Code, and Autopilot will be billed separately through usage-based Copilot Credits. A Copilot user subscription will still be needed to access those features.

For enterprise customers, the features that charge by usage will stay off until an admin sets up a spending policy in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Microsoft said there will be no charges before then. Admins can set budgets for the whole organization or for specific groups, limit how much individual users can spend, and get alerts when costs approach a set amount. Reports will show who is using the features and which tasks are driving costs.

Microsoft is bringing Code and Copilot Managed Runtime under those cost controls alongside Cowork. Copilot Studio agents are expected to follow in October. Before turning the features on for more employees, IT departments will need to decide who can use them, how much they can spend and which tasks are worth the extra cost.

Home and Code will begin rolling out through Microsoft's Frontier early access program in the coming weeks. Code's Frontier rollout starts at the end of September, when Autopilot also expands to private preview.

For more information, visit the Microsoft site.
