Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Borrowers will be taxed on student debt canceled under the program in 2026 and could owe up to $12,000 more in April, per a new analysis.

Under the pandemic-era American Rescue Plan Act, discharged student loan debt did not count as income on federal taxes from Dec. 31, 2020, to Dec. 31, 2025. But since Congress allowed that provision to expire, IDR loans discharged in 2026 and beyond will appear on borrowers’ tax bills.

Protect Borrowers called on federal lawmakers to intervene and permanently exempt discharged IDR loans, as well as discharged loans through the Repayment Assistance Plan, from federal taxes in the next budget reconciliation process.

The average discharged IDR loan is worth about $50,000, according to the nonproft. Its analysis said that sum often outstrips the value of the initial loan, due to borrowers’ low income-based monthly payments not covering the cost of ballooning interest.

Single borrowers without children with discharged IDR loans would pay over $10,000 in federal taxes if they earned $40,000 in 2026 and nearly $20,000 if they earned $80,000.

After payroll and state taxes, these borrowers “could effectively see their take-home income cut nearly in half,” the report said.

Families with children and a lower household income could see the highest share of their annual earnings absorbed by the tax increase. A household of four with $40,000 in annual income can expect to pay an additional $10,558 on top of their base expected tax bill — $1,704 in new taxes and $8,854 in lost tax credits.

“Treating such cancelled debt as taxable income will hit many borrowers with an unprecedented tax bomb and force them to replace their debt to the U.S. Department of Education with debt to the Internal Revenue Service,” the analysis said.

The change is also expected to have a disproportionate impact on borrowers in the South, as they typically have larger loan balances and lower incomes than the U.S. average.

The massive expected increase in taxes will put most borrowers in an impossible financial position, after they’ve already “endured significant financial hardship for a majority of their adult lives,” Protect Borrowers said.

As of 2022, half of borrowers who had their IDR student debt canceled didn’t have a bank account or nonretirement savings, the nonprofit said, citing survey data from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Like Protect Borrowers, a coalition of Democratic Senators last November called on the Trump administration to exempt canceled IDR loans from income taxation.

Following Tuesday’s midterms, Democrats are set to gain control of both chambers of Congress and such a change could stand a better chance of passing. President Donald Trump still holds veto power.

#7213. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding. Rather than designing for a single type of learner, UDL recognizes that flexibility benefits all students and should be incorporated into learning experiences from the beginning.

For district leaders, accessibility is also a strong indicator of a curriculum provider's overall commitment to quality. Providers that prioritize accessible design are often demonstrating a broader investment in learner-centered development, thoughtful user experience and continuous improvement.

#7195. Want to improve the preschool-to-kindergarten handoff? Strengthen local connections

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

#7154. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

#7130. AI Platform Behind 12,000 E-mail Breaches Dismantled

The platform moved quickly. Since launching in February, EvilTokens had been tied to more than 12,000 compromised inboxes across over 10,000 organizations worldwide, according to Microsoft. Its victims included businesses and institutions in financial services, healthcare, construction, real estate, wholesale distribution, and higher education.

Microsoft and its partners seized 50 websites used to operate EvilTokens and disabled more than 150 additional domains supporting the service. The Metropolitan Police Service in the United Kingdom also arrested two men on Sept. 11 in connection with the alleged operation.

At the center of EvilTokens was an AI chatbot designed specifically for cybercrime. Once attackers gained access to an inbox, the chatbot could analyze messages, map relationships, identify employees involved in payments, and recommend ways to impersonate trusted contacts.

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

Business e-mail compromise has traditionally required attackers to manually comb through messages, learn how an organization handles payments, and find the right person to impersonate. However, EvilTokens simplified the entire process by automating much of that work.

The tools could quickly sort through a stolen inbox, summarize or translate messages, find invoices and wire-transfer discussions, and identify the people who moved money. EvilTokens could then draft messages that sounded like they came from the victim. It packaged all of this with mailbox access, phishing tools, management dashboards, and even customer support.

The service was sold through Telegram for a $1,500 startup fee, followed by a $500 monthly subscription. Microsoft's complaint also described a bulk e-mail tool that cost $600 a month and a more advanced e-mail delivery platform priced at $1,000 a month.

To break into a target, EvilTokens relied on device-code phishing. Victims were sent to a real Microsoft sign-in page and persuaded to enter an authentication code. The login process looked legitimate because it was, but the code quietly authorized a session controlled by the attacker.

That also made cleanup more complicated. The attackers stole OAuth tokens and authenticated sessions instead of passwords, so a password reset alone might not remove their access. Administrators also needed to revoke the stolen tokens and active sessions.

According to Microsoft's complaint,  EvilTokens used fake Microsoft 365 security notices and other branded templates to direct users into the device authentication flow. Once inside, attackers could use Microsoft Graph and other APIs to search and monitor a mailbox.

The company also found that AI played a role in creating EvilTokens itself. Investigators said much of the platform appeared to have been "vibe coded," with its developers using AI to quickly build and expand the service. EvilTokens reportedly drew on multiple AI models, including improperly integrated services from Groq and OpenAI.

The court authorized Microsoft to take control of or disable domains connected to the operation. Microsoft worked with Cloudflare, Coinbase, OpenAI, Railway, SpyCloud, the Shadowserver Foundation, and TRM Labs on the disruption. Health-ISAC joined the case because healthcare organizations were among the targets.

This marks the Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit's 40th court-authorized disruption and its first targeting an end-to-end, AI-enabled cybercrime service.

EvilTokens' infrastructure may be offline, but the model behind it is unlikely to disappear. The operation showed how AI can turn a stolen inbox into an immediately useful fraud playbook, allowing less-experienced criminals to launch attacks that once required considerable time and skill.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

#7094. AI Ambitions Are Running Into a Network Infrastructure Trust Problem

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

The research found that concerns about network providers have caused organizations to rethink strategic initiatives over the past two years. Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#7072. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#7060. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.
