#7094. COVID learning recovery falls on states’ shoulders, CRPE report says

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Only about 1 in 10 students are in districts that are back to prepandemic achievement levels in math and reading, per the Center on Reinventing Public Education.

Pandemic-related learning recovery has "failed" for most students, and now states need to take the lead in accelerating academic achievement and driving improved student outcomes, according to a new report from the Center on Reinvening Public Education.

Only 10.5% of students are in districts that have returned to prepandemic achievement levels in both math and literacy. No states have returned to prepandemic attendance levels, according to the report. And with a downsized U.S. Department of Education shifting more responsibilities to the states, "all eyes [are] on states at a very pivotal moment," said Robin Lake, director of CRPE and co-author of the report. Lake was speaking at a Nov. 9 webinar on the report's findings, along with co-author Chelsea Waite, a research principal at CRPE. The report was released last month jointly with Arizona State University's Mary Lou Fulton Teachers College, where CRPE is housed.

To improve student outcomes, state leaders should investigate gaps between their policies and how they are implemented — including systems for measuring their progress and accountability performance. Then, they could prioritize plugging those gaps by paying special attention to the students furthest behind, including those with disabilities and multilingual learners, the report said. While academic performances varies widely among states, CRPE's analysis shows that every state has room for improvement. The analysis found that only 13 states have clear, specific, statewide goals for student learning that is messaged across multiple documents and webpages, said Waite.

South Carolina, for instance, has set a goal for 75% of its students to perform at grade level by 2030. "A clear goal is the first piece of a clear strategy," Waite said. "So without a clear goal, there's no way to tell whether a state is doing something that adds up to anything, and nobody can hold the state to that goal if it's not made public."

The CRPE report explores how state education policies, initiatives and reforms break down along political lines. The center found partisan divides in state-led instructional reform for math and literacy. For example, eight of the 12 states with “strong” policies for science of reading teacher preparation programs were categorized as red states, and 13 of 18 states with math laws were Republican-led.

On the other hand, student well-being policies — such as initiatives that touch on counseling, suicide prevention and school safety — are "led almost entirely by Democrats," the report said. Of the 17 states that have policies giving mental health days that can count as an excused absence from school, three are red states, eight are blue and six are purple.

The report found that states' academic achievement spanned the political spectrum, even though a "popular narrative" is that blue states outperform the others.

As far as how midterm elections will influence state education policy, that's a "big unknown," said Waite. The analysis was conducted before the November elections.

Eliminating this eligibility "may result in some level of increased administrative burden on states, a reduction in SNAP benefit spending, and associated effects on school meals eligibility," the USDA acknowledged in its proposal.

In addition to impacting school meal eligibility, the department estimates that approximately 42,000 children could potentially no longer receive the Summer Electronic Benefit Transfer beginning in summer 2029. The USDA’s new proposed rule would further exacerbate challenges for school meals, as well as students' benefits for summer meals, according to the Food Research & Action Center.

"Eliminating BBCE would reduce access to free school meals, making children hungry at home and at school," said FRAC in a Nov. 10 statement. "Children who lose SNAP are no longer automatically eligible for free school meals."

It would also increase administrative work for schools, such as those who were previously eligible for Community Eligibility Provision, the organization said. CEP helps high-poverty schools provide universal access to free school meals. The organization said it is concerned the impact could be “far worse than projected."

#7059. Teacher prep programs probed in Education Department’s latest anti-DEI push

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The agency said diversification efforts disregard “merit,” but critics say the programs level the playing field for historically excluded candidates.

The U.S. Department of Education opened a string of investigations into teacher preparation programs on Nov. 13, alleging that they discriminate on the basis of race under Title VI or sex under Title IX.

The investigations mark one of the first times the department's Office for Civil Rights has used civil rights protections to challenge diversification efforts in teacher scholarships, hiring, training and recruitment practices.

The programs at the center of the investigations are aimed at recruiting historically underrepresented groups in the teaching profession, such as men and teachers of color.

“Race, national origin, or sex should not decide who will shape America’s next generation, yet many states and universities seem to prioritize recruiting and training ‘minority’ candidates rather than evaluating candidates based solely on merit,” said Assistant Secretary for Civil Rights Kimberly Richey in a Nov. 13 statement.

“Education agencies and institutions that consider race, national origin, or sex as a means to withhold opportunities in selecting, supporting, and preparing teaching candidates violate federal law and compromise education excellence."

Richey concluded that students should be "judged on their hard work and merit, not prejudged by the color of their skin or their sex.”

EdTrust, a nonprofit that advocates for racial and economic equity in American education, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that the Trump administration is using civil rights protections meant to "dismantle discrimination and expand opportunity" to instead "undermine pathways into teaching for people historically excluded from the profession betrays that purpose."

“The administration’s false divide between ‘merit’ and diversity perpetuates the damaging suggestion that teachers of color are less qualified or less deserving," said Eric Duncan, director of Pre-K-12 policy of EdTrust, in the Nov. 16 statement. "It discounts their skills, expertise, and contributions while ignoring the systemic barriers that have denied talented people opportunities for generations. Scholarships that address those barriers help ensure that family wealth does not determine who can become a teacher."

The Education Department's investigations, Duncan said, "risk turning civil rights enforcement into a tool for preserving the very inequities it should confront.”

CUNY, which houses the NYC Men Teach Program being investigated by the department, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that it was "proud of its role educating the teachers of tomorrow and our programs are open to all students regardless of race, gender or national origin."

According to the city's website, the program was created around 2015 in collaboration with the federal Education Department and then-Mayor Bill de Blasio with the goal of putting an additional 1,000 men of color into the city’s teacher pipeline.

A March 2026 evaluation of the program conducted for the NYC mayor's office by Westat, which provides research services for government agencies, shows that the program contributed over 3,100 new male teachers of color to New York City Public Schools between school years 2015–16 and 2023–24.

The American Association of Colleges for Teacher Education said in a Nov. 16 email to K-12 Dive, “Strengthening and diversifying the educator pipeline is essential to addressing persistent teacher shortages and ensuring every PK-12 student has access to well-prepared, effective teachers. Decades of research confirm that a diverse educator workforce benefits all students academically, socially, and emotionally." AACTE added that it does not comment on active federal inquiries involving specific institutions or state agencies.

While the investigations are some of the first of their kind by OCR, they build on previous efforts by the Trump administration to weed out DEI in teacher preparation programs — especially in the wake of the U.S. Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard.

Some of the earliest federal grants terminated in 2025 by the administration were under the Supporting Effective Educator Development Grant Program, the Teacher Quality Partnership Program and the Teacher and School Leader Incentive Program, due to what it said was the promotion of "divisive ideologies". The department admitted in a March court filing that it had cancelled 104 out of 109 active TQP and SEED grants.

The loss of over 100 federal teacher training grants last year caused at least 127 teacher candidates to drop out of their programs and another 259 accepted candidates to not enroll after all, according to survey findings released in October.

Those cuts were preceded by an anti-DEI directive that has since been heavily litigated and was most recently blocked in court in September.

That anti-DEI letter specifically called for agency personnel to review all new grant awards and issued grants to ensure those federal dollars “do not fund discriminatory practices — including in the form of DEI.” The directive added that “Illegal DEI policies and practices can violate both the letter and purpose of Federal civil rights law and conflict with the Department’s policy of prioritizing merit, fairness, and excellence in education.”

The Justice Department in August 2025 also warned districts they could be exposed to legal liability by asking job applicants how their “cultural background informs their teaching,” using recruitment strategies targeting candidates from specific geographic areas or racial backgrounds, and asking job candidates to describe how they overcame obstacles.

Since the Supreme Court’s decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, civil rights groups have raised concerns that it would set back educational equity efforts even outside of race-conscious admissions. That decision undid race-conscious policies in college admissions, and the Education Department under Trump has used it to justify scaling back other diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives in education.

Scholarship availability, teacher pipelines and student affinity groups were among the top areas beyond college access that advocates were concerned could be impacted in the wake of the ruling.

#7034. Is This a ‘Gut Feeling’ About My Relationship... or Just My Anxiety?

We may earn commission from links on this page, but we only recommend products we love. Promise.

We’re often told that we should trust our gut in relationships—that when something is wrong, we’ll get a distinct feeling in our bodies. But what if that feeling isn’t distinct at all? What if these feelings of dread and alarm are constant? What if you can’t really trust your gut?

I’ve had anxiety for as long as I can remember. And when I get into relationships, I feel the symptoms tenfold. I create worst-case scenarios in my head, over-analyze every little reaction, and go into a panic when a partner has the slightest shift in attitude. A lot of this anxiety has to do with my past (surprise, surprise). I was in an emotionally abusive relationship, riddled with infidelity and manipulation, for six years. And although it’s been years since my breakup and I’ve moved on to an entirely new relationship that feels healthy and fulfilling, I don’t think my body and brain have quite gotten the memo.

“A person who has experienced neglect, abandonment, unpredictability or inconsistencies in past relationships may be unknowingly conditioned to scan for threat, even in a healthy, stable relationship,” Jessica N. Gordon, LCSW tells Cosmopolitan.

So if you come equipped with intense relational trauma, how do you know the difference between when something is actually wrong in your relationship—and when it’s just your anxiety acting up again?

When every little thing feels like the end of the world, it becomes hard to discern when there’s a real concern in your relationship. This is typically what Relationship Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (ROCD) looks like.

"Anxiety driven by ROCD tends to feel very intense and urgent,” Cameron Murphey, LMFT says. “It feels like you have to address it right now.” He explains that people with ROCD typically feel like they’re unable to focus on anything else until they've addressed what's making them feel anxious.

But the problem is what’s making them anxious isn’t exactly rooted in something concrete. People with ROCD constantly ruminate on thoughts like “How do I know I’m truly in love?,” “There have been no signs, but I think they’re cheating on me,” or “Is my partner doing enough?” There might not even be a specific action that triggers these thoughts.

“An [ROCD] thought tends to jump from topic to topic... and feels spontaneous rather than a consistent subtle thought,” Gordon says. A true concern in the relationship, on the other hand, is tied to real, observable behavior that shows up in a pattern: “every time we talk about my feelings, he picks up his phone and I feel ignored.”

But what if your gut is just screaming at you that something is wrong?

“I think we sometimes give our gut feelings a lot of authority without stopping to consider what's informing them,” Sonni Williams, LPC says. “Our intuition can be shaped by our experiences, values, and even the things we're afraid of repeating.”

She adds that we sometimes tend to oversimplify intuition as calm and anxiety as panic. The problem with this is that both of these things can feel convincing, and neither feeling alone tells us whether something is true.

Rather than torturing yourself with these thoughts as they come, Williams says it’s more effective and helpful to look at what happens afterward.

“Can you reflect on the concern and decide whether something needs attention?” she says to ask yourself. “Or are you repeatedly analyzing conversations and feelings, trying to find certainty?”

Now this doesn’t mean you need an immediate answer in order to take your feelings seriously. Williams explains that when it comes to concerns involving abuse, coercion, or controlling behaviors, these things deserve attention regardless of whether anxiety or ROCD are present.

It’s absolutely a good thing to reflect on your relationship and assess if it’s working for you. But Williams explains that what’s most important is knowing the difference between evaluating your relationship and feeling like you have to constantly prove that it’s right or perfect.

And you may not be able to trust your gut, specifically, but that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t trust yourself—you just have to learn what that looks like in the context of your anxiety.

"Self-trust is not about believing that you'll always make the right choice,” Murphey says. “It's about trusting that you can handle whatever happens, good or bad.”

He explains that we tend to feel fear in our stomachs, so it’s easy to confuse those very physical feelings with something grander like intuition. And guess what? That’s totally fine—so long as you don’t use those feelings to solely dictate your actions in your relationship. If you get those feelings, think about why or where they’re coming from.

You’re going to have second thoughts about your partner from time-to-time (or if you’re me, every three business days). Seeing a therapist for lingering, uncontrollable thoughts can help you work through whatever anxieties you may have. Otherwise, there are going to be times where you just inevitably question your relationship.

Gordon says, “The goal is to feel less controlled by doubt, not to never have it.”

The reference architecture also covers development-related systems. “This was the first one that had the bones that Katherine wanted,” recalls Gauthier, who runs the firm GAuthier Architects. “It follows the classic real estate rule of buying the worst house on the best block.”

The strategy paid off: Once their offer was accepted, the couple embarked on an 18-month-long odyssey that saw them jump through a series of hoops (preservationists; concerned neighbors; skeptical engineers) to completely transform the moldering pile into a creative haven for them to live, work, and raise their daughter.

A true joint-effort, Gauthier oversaw a modest two-story rear expansion and structural alterations, while Keltner took charge of spatial arrangements as well as color, pattern, texture, and artwork—much of it her own.

Once deteriorating, the interiors now have an easy elegance, with plaster walls, arched passageways, and curved corners that give the soaring ceilings an intimate feel. Pink takes an unexpected starring role, appearing throughout the parlor floor and also in the zellige-clad primary bath, but other adventurous hues like mint green and crimson also feature prominently. Capping it off is a plywood-sheathed office for Gauthier’s practice, allowing the family retreat to double as a place of creative inspiration.

Below, Keltner and Gauthier share their biggest lessons (and some “before” pictures) from the painstaking passion project.

When the townhouse was converted to an SRO, it had been chopped up into at least 13 units and two kitchens, and few of the original decorative elements were still intact. A couple had bought it in the 1960s and made it a family home again and added an art gallery space. But by 2021, when Keltner and Gauthier first saw it, the house was in a state of severe neglect, with peeling paint, crumbling floors, ancient appliances—not to mention a 1,500-pound printing press in the studio (that the couple later preserved). Yet, Keltner and Gauthier saw an opportunity, not to recreate the past, but rather forge their own set of rules.

“The house wasn’t burdened with pristine historic details—our moldings were trashed,” says Gauthier. “There were a few things that we kept and celebrated, but to me, it was like a tabula rasa. We can just go in and make it ours.” Katherine agreed: “I feel like sometimes having to preserve things is more expensive than just starting clean,” she says. “And so I thought that was an advantage for us.”

Not having to be too precious with ornamentation allowed the couple to make a bold statement about crown moldings, eschewing tradition and installing curving corners and coved ceilings instead. Nowhere does the effect have more impact than in the parlor, where Gauthier devised a ceiling light in the shape of a giant oval that casts light upward rather than down. “I really like indirect lighting,” he says.

Crafted out of laser-cut plywood, the elliptical fixture is an abstracted homage to the ceiling medallions found in neighboring historic brownstones. “Some of them are incredibly ornate and beautiful, but they don't really have any use—they’re just decoration,” says Gauthier. “Adding ambient lighting to the living space takes that historical reference in pocket.”

Once they were through the process of changing the structure’s zoning from an SRO to a single-family home, Gauthier and Keltner had to contend with its landmarked status, meaning a series of approvals were required for any additions or exterior modifications.

The couple originally planned to construct a three-story extension in the back, but they ended up compromising on two floors. This expansion includes a bump-out for the parlor floor’s kitchen, leading to a rear deck that steps down to the garden. On top, a private terrace connects to the primary bath. “Instead of having that third story, we now have a deck off of our primary suite,” says Keltner. “It’s kind of amazing,” adds Gauthier.

One decision the couple wrestled with mightily was whether to replace the original, 150-year-old pine flooring with ebony-stained white oak. “The floors were part of the reason we liked the house so much,” says Gauthier. But their contractor talked them out of keeping them. “It’s a lot of extra work for them,” says Keltner. “They have to take it up, save it, put something else down, and then put it back. I think we reached a point in time and money where we were like, Okay, fine.”

Another flooring regret was not adding underfoot heating. “I was pushing the mechanical engineers to do radiant heating and radiant cooling,” says Gauthier. “Radiant heat is really lovely—you have warmth under your feet all the time.” But the mechanics behind the system proved complicated and technically redundant, as the home was already configured with a forced-air HVAC system.

“In doing research and other projects, it’s clear that the cost-to-pleasure ratio would have been worth it,” he says now. “We regret not putting the warm floors in.”

While windows might sometimes be an afterthought, Keltner and Gauthier have advice: Go with triple-glazed. “Windows provide heat and cool insulation as well as security,” explains Keltner. “And they can define both the outside and the inside of a space.” The couple went with triple-glazed windows from Austrian-based firm Bewiso that come pre-wired for easy connection to alarm systems. “They’re almost like hurricane windows,” she says.

“You can't break through three panes of glass,” says Gauthier. Due to the building’s landmark status, they had to use historic-style windows on the front facade. “We were able to get these old-fashioned type of windows that when you close them, they feel like you're closing a Mercedes,” says Gauthier. “They completely silence the outdoors.”

The final piece of advice is perhaps the most crucial: “Hire the best contractor you can afford,” says Gauthier.

“The typical advice is to get a few bids and throw out the lowest and the highest,” says Keltner. “People usually take the second lowest.” However, that could be the determining factor between an on-time project and one that drags. “You’ll end up paying the difference both in money and time,” she says. “It’s often hard for people to bite the bullet and go with the slightly higher price, but it really pays off.”

AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6954. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6948. Here’s What It Takes to Keep a Pool Running During the Winter, According to Experts

Before you dive in, it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved.

When most people think of backyard swimming pools, relaxing outdoors in the summertime immediately comes to mind. But some homeowners don’t want the swimming season to end when temperatures drop. Fans of cold-water swimming are starting to consider ways to enjoy their pools throughout the winter as a part of an at-home wellness experience.

“Ten years ago, most people didn’t think winter swims were possible, especially in colder climates,” says Karen Larson, cofounder of Soake Pools. “Times have changed because these days, they’re seeing neighbors, friends, and others do it and post the proof on social media.”

The increasing interest in cold-water experiences is part of a broader wellness trend. In American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, cold therapies, including cold plunges, cryotherapy, and cold showers, earned the 17th spot among fitness trends for the year, up from number 20 in its 2025 report.

If you’ve previously closed your swimming pool around Labor Day but are interested in making cold-water swimming a part of your daily wellness routine, there’s a lot to keep in mind. Not every existing pool is a good candidate for keeping open for winter swimming, and it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved. But if you’re curious, here’s what to consider about keeping a pool open year-round.

Why is cold-water swimming becoming more common? There are numerous purported mental and wellness benefits, from athletic recovery to stress relief and better sleep. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS ONE found that cold-water immersion led to a reduction in stress for 12 hours afterward as well as improvements in sleep quality and overall quality of life.

“Winter swimming often creates a time where friends and family can come together without distractions,” adds Larson.

That said, while swimming during the colder months has its perks, there are also several risks to keep in mind. A 2020 review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that cold-water immersion can trigger an immediate shock response, leading to involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, and a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure. These reactions can make it difficult to control your breathing and increase your risk of drowning. Cold temps can also impair your muscle strength and nerve function, making it challenging to swim safely.

It’s a good idea to acclimate to the cold water gradually and avoid prolonged exposure. You’ll also want to make sure there’s someone else home while you take your cold-water plunge in the pool.

In addition, if you have underlying conditions like heart disease, consult your doctor before all else, as the cold water may place additional strain on your heart.

If you’re trying to consider whether your pool is a good candidate to keep open during the colder months, these are the things to look into.

The cost of keeping a swimming pool open for winter swimming will depend on your location, size, desired water temperature, and the type of heater you use.

“Routine pool maintenance generally remains about the same year-round, although chemical consumption may decrease depending on how frequently the pool is used,” explains Jim Eisch, Jr., vice president of operations at Pinch A Penny Pool Patio Spa. “The biggest expense to consider in the winter is heating the pool.”

For this reason, prospective winter swimmers may want to consider an upgrade to their heating system. Instead of using pricey natural gas heating or a traditional heat pump, Eisch says that inverter technology is one of the most affordable ways to maintain a swimmable pool temperature year-round.

For example, if you live in Florida and have a 10,000-gallon swimming pool, Eisch estimates your annual heating costs will be $2,467 with a natural gas heater, $741 with a traditional heat pump, and $434 for a heat/cool pump that uses inverter technology.

As aforementioned, the goal with wintertime swimming is not to be soaking in bathwater-like temperatures while it’s freezing outside. For safety purposes, winter swims are quick, and the heating system is to keep your chlorinated pool water well above the 19-degree Fahrenheit freezing point.

Unsurprisingly, a standard 14-by-28-foot swimming pool is going to be one of the hardest to use effectively year-round, unless you live in a region with a mild wintertime climate.

“You can technically use a large pool year-round, but it’s difficult to cover all its surface area well enough to hold in the heat, so heating bills can climb fast,” says Larson.

Therefore, smaller sizes like a 10-by-16-foot cocktail pool or plunge pool are better candidates for keeping open. Dedicated winter swimmers should also invest in an easy on-and-off cover—or a motorized auto cover—for added protection.

Regular pool cleaning and maintenance are essential, and this upkeep over the winter is no exception.

“Check the filtration system regularly to make sure it’s circulating smoothly, monitor the water chemistry, and plan for power outages—because there’s a good chance they will happen,” Larson explains. For this reason, you should also invest in a generator that can keep the pump running if the power goes out.

Safe pool access is just as important as any other aspect of the pool’s design. That’s why a nonslip surface, excellent drainage, and ample lighting are key.

“Your design should also provide a clear path from the house to the water,” says Neha Gupta, interior designer and founder at NU Curations.

Especially in the wintertime when ice and snow are a factor in your region, make sure you have a safe and smooth exit plan for getting out of the water, bundled up, and into your warm home without any obstacles. And then, of course, remember to cover the pool back up to keep it protected and ready for your next plunge.

#6918. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.
