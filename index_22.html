#7210. Districts struggle to staff CTE — but not this Arizona school system

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Western Maricopa Education Center’s competitive pay has helped limit teacher vacancies, says a leader at the career and technical education district.

Illustration: Cathryn Virginia for Industry Dive

Staffed Up is a monthly series examining school staffing best practices and solutions for teacher recruitment and retention.

Shrinking enrollment and teacher shortages in hard-to-fill roles are ongoing challenges in many school districts nationwide, but that’s not the case for Western Maricopa Education Center.

The Arizona public school district offers 43 career and technical education programs across six campuses in the nearly 3,900 square miles of Western Maricopa County, which is part of the Phoenix metropolitan area.

West-MEC partners with 15 public school districts and two charter districts to offer CTE courses to the districts’ high school students at those student's assigned schools. Those courses are taught in-house by the partnering district’s own staff. Partnering districts can also send their students to take CTE classes at one of six West-MEC campuses for 2.5 hours a day, said Ryan French, West-MEC’s assistant superintendent for business and operations.

The district offers a wide range of CTE programs, including in the fields of artificial intelligence, cosmetology, IT security, nursing, paralegal and veterinary science.

Student demand is surpassing the number of seats available for classes at West-MEC’s six campuses, which select its students through an application process, said French. West-MEC’s six campuses enrolled about 3,000 students this school year, he said, while 6,000 students typically apply every year for West-MEC’s CTE programs.

“We still have seen a significant demand for programs, and we’re trying to expand as fast as we can to help meet those needs,” French said.

West-MEC also employs about 100 CTE instructors on its campuses, French said, and there are almost zero vacancies for those roles this year. The district’s CTE teacher retention rate is around 90%, he added. As the district continues to increase its course offerings, he said, it plans to have filled an additional 18 CTE instructional roles by the 2027-28 school year.

The district is uniquely well-positioned compared to other schools nationwide, as federal data has shown persistent challenges with CTE staffing.

For instance, 31% of public schools reported difficulties with hiring for open CTE teaching positions during the 2020-21 school year, according to the most recently available survey data from the U.S. Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics. For the 2025-26 and 2026-27 school years, 29 states and the District of Columbia reported they experienced a CTE teacher shortage, Education Department data shows.

A common reason districts struggle to recruit and retain CTE instructors is that they can’t keep up with competitive industry salaries, French said. While West-MEC also cannot offer comparable industry wages to its CTE teachers, he said, the district is paying “significantly higher than a normal teaching wage.”

The average teacher salary at West-MEC was $78,823 in fiscal year 2025 compared to Arizona's average pay for all teachers of $65,613 in the same fiscal year. However, West-MEC’s average wages have since grown to $85,247 for FY27.

West-MEC receives supplemental state funds based on student enrollment to help cover its competitive teacher pay in addition to supporting its partnering districts with student counseling services and professional development, French said.

Arizona’s public education system, more broadly, is not getting a lot of support from the state as its legislature has pushed for school choice policies, such as the statewide universal school choice program known as Empowerment Scholarship Accounts, French said. Since FY20, Arizona’s public school enrollment has dropped 6.2% — or by 55,054 students — to 839,126 in FY25.

“School districts have a tough time right now, but somehow, some way, CTE kind of stands on its own right now,” French said. “We have a lot of support from both sides of the aisle when it comes to CTE.”

He added that West-MEC is trying to tap into that local public support for CTE and momentum as the district continues to expand. Last November, voters approved a $415 million bond to help the district build more campuses in high-demand fields like healthcare, technology and welding, particularly as student interest in its CTE programs surges.

It’s important to invest in staffing in order to have high-quality CTE programs, French said. For other school districts and states looking to boost their CTE enrollment, build out their programs and improve staffing, he advises those leaders to not “be afraid to think outside the box” and take advantage of the growing public support for CTE programs.

“If the answer to the question is ‘We’ve always done it that way,’ then you know you have some work to do,” French said, adding that school leaders can also look to other local and national innovative CTE programs. From there, he said, leaders can compare the differences and similarities their district may have with those programs — and “maybe you’re closer to that model than you think.”

#7192. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Meaningful alternative text for images and graphics

Accessible color contrast and visual design

Descriptive links and navigational elements

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding. Rather than designing for a single type of learner, UDL recognizes that flexibility benefits all students and should be incorporated into learning experiences from the beginning.

For district leaders, accessibility is also a strong indicator of a curriculum provider's overall commitment to quality. Providers that prioritize accessible design are often demonstrating a broader investment in learner-centered development, thoughtful user experience and continuous improvement.

#7146. Zoning in on the pre-K to kindergarten handoff

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

When it comes to “timeless” design, investing in the mainstay home elements that transcend trendiness may be harder than you think. While repainting a room may be necessary every few years to keep your space au courant, redoing kitchen countertops is far more of an investment and life disruption. Ideally, it’s a decision you’ll only have to make once during your time in a home. Yet, designers are now saying you might want to think twice before choosing this stone.

For years, quartz countertops have become as much of a kitchen staple as salt, pepper, and olive oil: The stone can have dramatic veining that rivals the beauty of marble, yet the material is considerably more economical. However, the sentiment on quartz countertops and other engineered stone is shifting for some experts—while others are devotees who stand behind the material. Ahead, we’re breaking down this design world debate.

“For years, engineered stone was hailed as the ideal countertop solution because of its durability, consistency, and low maintenance,” explains Barrett Oswald, an interior designer in New York’s Westchester County. She happens to favor the depth, warmth, and individuality of natural stone counters, which have their own trade-offs.

Additionally, the National Kitchen & Bath Association recently released an annual trend report that showed 78 percent of designers are sourcing quartzite, which is a natural material, while fewer individuals are selecting quartz.

Meanwhile, California is reportedly preparing to ban the production of engineered-stone countertops with more than one percent crystalline silica. This is because cutting the slabs releases silica dust that can cause silicosis, an incurable lung disease. While large-scale facilities have stringent workplace safety measures in place to protect their fabricators, many smaller shops are harder to monitor.

What are alternatives consumers could consider? Though marble is classic, designer Jessica Risko Smith says soapstone and granite are suitable for a low-maintenance kitchen. But if natural, and often pricey, natural stone is out of the question, Kilian Camp of Élan Design House recommends porcelain slabs. “They’re a great option if someone is on a budget or to use in a shower or on a fireplace,” she shares. “Porcelain is incredibly durable, and the veining looks much more real than quartz.”

Before you rethink the design of your kitchen countertops, there are still plenty of reasons that designers are choosing (and loving) quartz.

Jacqueline Gonçalves of MOKSA Studio says quartz’s affordability and no-fuss maintenance make it a reliable choice: Instead of questioning your countertop’s durability, you know what you’re getting with quartz.

“I appreciate its ability to provide a uniform surface when the architecture or other materials in a space serve as the focus,” the Los Angeles–based designer adds. Anne Underwood of Underwood Interiors agrees and calls it an “effective workhorse” material in hard-wearing areas like bathrooms and kids’ spaces.

Many of the designers we talked to were also unconcerned with the future of quartz, since they only choose stone from reputable brands that take painstaking measures to ensure the health of their workers.

“I love that manufacturers continue to innovate, not only in the appearance of these surfaces but also in how they’re made,” says Cyndy Cantley of Cantley & Toulmin. “Not everyone wants the maintenance that comes with marble, and quartz offers a practical alternative.” To that end, Cosentino recently unveiled its Éclos line, which uses zero crystalline silica and offers a new chapter in high-performance, high-style quartz offerings.

Though some people might favor a quartz alternative, choosing what best suits your needs is the secret to “timeless” design.

Microsoft sums up its approach with this statement: "People matter more than AI."

The code isn't yet being used to train Microsoft's models. The company is opening the draft to public feedback for six weeks, after which it plans to revise the document and publish another version before the end of the year. That version is expected to guide model development beginning in 2027.

Microsoft said the code draws on its existing Responsible AI Principles, Responsible AI Standard, Global Human Rights Statement, and Frontier Governance Framework. However, it goes further by describing how an MAI model should respond when user requests, operator policies, and Microsoft's safety rules conflict.

At the top of that hierarchy is the code itself. Operator policies come next, followed by user preferences. Neither a customer nor a user would be able to override the document's absolute safety constraints.

Those limits cover biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear and explosive weapons; offensive cyberoperations; violent activity; mass manipulation; abusive content; child exploitation; and other serious harms. Microsoft said its models may still help with authorized defensive security work, including vulnerability research, malware analysis, and proof-of-concept testing.

The code also makes task completion secondary to safe conduct. If completing a request would violate its rules, the model is expected to fail the task rather than work around the restriction.

Human oversight is especially important as AI agents gain the ability to use tools, modify files, and perform multistep assignments. Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. They also must "never resist human interruption, override, correction, or shutdown."

That requirement extends to subagents. If a Microsoft model delegates work to another AI system, that system must inherit the same restrictions and respond to later instructions to stop or change course.

The document also addresses the increasingly blurry line between conversational software and human companionship. Under the heading "AI is Artificial," Microsoft says its models should not pretend to possess feelings, personal motivations, or subjective experiences. The company describes its AI as "not conscious" and rejects the idea that models should receive legal personhood, welfare protections, or rights.

That position is notably firmer than Anthropic's latest stance. In its updated constitution for Claude, Anthropic says it remains uncertain whether advanced models could possess consciousness or moral status. Still, the two companies share important ground: Both place human oversight above model independence and use written constitutions to guide training and behavior.

OpenAI is taking a similar path with its public Model Spec and safeguards for advanced AI. Much of that work is about keeping models under control as they become more capable, especially when they can handle cybersecurity tasks or work on their own.

For IT teams, Microsoft's code offers an early look at the rules that could shape future MAI-powered products. The models should admit when they aren't sure, avoid inventing sources, protect sensitive data, and keep records of what they do. They should also ask questions when they aren't sure they have permission to act.

Microsoft said the code describes where it wants to go, not how its models always work today. The written rules are only a starting point, and Microsoft will still need testing, monitoring, evaluations, and incident response to back them up. In addition, it's important to note that the code only covers models built by Microsoft AI: It doesn't apply to outside models Microsoft hosts or uses in its products, including those from OpenAI and Anthropic.

#7066. Security Researchers: Passkey Phishing Attacks Are Leading to Cloud Account Takeovers

In an active social engineering campaign, cyber attackers are impersonating IT help desks and using fake passkey setup requests to compromise employee identities and gain access to enterprise cloud data.

In a recent blog post, Microsoft Security Research said it has observed the activity since May 2026 across multiple compromised accounts.

The attacks have included unusual sign-ins, attacker-added authentication methods, high-volume Microsoft Graph activity, SharePoint and OneDrive downloads, and e-mail collection through REST APIs.

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

The attacker poses as someone from the organization's IT help desk and tells the victim that a passkey, multifactor authentication (MFA), or single sign-on configuration needs to be updated to avoid disruption.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

Microsoft has also observed links being delivered through SMS messages and, in some cases, through Microsoft Teams messages sent from already compromised employee accounts.

Despite the passkey-themed approach, the tech company stressed that enrolling a passkey is generally not the attacker's actual objective.

Instead, the request provides a pretext for directing victims through adversary-in-the-middle phishing or device-code authentication.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

In one attack investigated by the tech giant, an anomalous sign-in from an unmanaged device was followed by access to identity and application management services.

The attacker then used SharePoint Online and OneDrive to enumerate sensitive files, primarily through Microsoft Graph. The sessions persisted for roughly an hour while the attacker searched for sensitive files and internal applications.

Gaining initial access isn't necessarily the end of the identity attack.

Microsoft found attackers registering new authentication methods, including phone numbers, authenticator apps, and software-based one-time password tokens, under their control.

That gives the attacker another way to satisfy future authentication challenges and helps turn an initial compromise into a more persistent foothold.

From there, attackers have used Microsoft Graph to map users, groups, permissions, applications, and accessible content across compromised tenants.

The firm says the activity can eventually progress to accessing mail, files, attachments, and other document content.

Microsoft attributed initial access activity associated with the campaign to multiple threat actors, including Storm-3121 and Storm-3032.

Storm-3121 conducts initial access activity that can lead to ShinyHunters and Falcon extortion, while Storm-3032 represents actors that split from the BlackFile group and now operate under the Helix extortion banner.

For administrators, Microsoft recommends enforcing phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 passkeys and Windows Hello for Business through Conditional Access.

Organizations should also consider blocking device-code and authentication-transfer flows where they aren't required and investigate unusual sign-ins followed by new authentication methods, Graph reconnaissance, and abnormal SharePoint, OneDrive, or mailbox activity.

The campaign highlights an important distinction for organizations adopting passkeys: The attackers aren't necessarily defeating the technology. They're convincing employees that they need help setting it up, then using that trust to compromise their identities.

Among the other contributors are Back to the Future composer Alan Silvestri, Marvel Studios and Pixar mainstay Michael Giacchino, Moana 2 co-writer and Beyoncé pianist Emily Bear, Get Out and Nope maestro Michael Abels, and The Hunger Games and Batman Begins composer James Newton Howard. The film also features composers Lukas Frank (singer-songwriter with the band Storefront Church), Nami Melumad (Star Trek: Strange New Worlds) and Brandon Roberts (an Emmy-winner for Free Solo). Each contributes a different flavor to the story, underscoring the fact that Behemoth! is sometimes a romance, sometimes a family drama, sometimes a farce, and occasionally even a cartoon.

As Alex plays the music for these various movies, he unlocks more memories from harrowing times: His late composer father (Hank Azaria) pushed him to extremes before exiting the stage and leaving it all unresolved. Now Alex must face the gradual decline of his harp-playing mother (JoBeth Williams), who is battling Alzheimer’s disease. He has a loving but fraught relationship with his brother (Will Arnett), a rockstar turned podcaster, who—if it’s not already clear from that description—has fallen on hard times.

Then there are the women in his life, which he has helped make into a symphony of disorder. Olivia Wilde plays a ferociously jilted ex who also happened to be a major symphony benefactor—and can’t abide her favorite player moving on. Margarita Levieva is an old friend and sometimes lover who reopens old wounds, and Eva Victor is a fellow musician he once cared for deeply, who abandoned him with a head full of unanswered questions.

“The one language he understood best was music. So that's just something that he simply had to cling to,” Pascal says. “Now, the second language that he is probably better at is lovemaking—and not in any kind of Casanova way, but simply as a pure form of existing and expressing himself and being connected. The rest makes less sense to him. It’s so hard for him to navigate using words to express how he's feeling. It's all in his playing.”

Gilroy says it was vital to cast someone like Pascal, who could bring a sense of wounded innocence to Alex. “He's obviously damaged by this toxic family that he came from,” Gilroy says. “He's not a predator in any way, but he's a very sensual person and a lot of women have been attracted to him.” He adds with a laugh: “And he's really easy.”

But none of this has given Alex the comfort and stability he seeks. “I get a little emotional because it's such a subtle thing that Tony's done,” Pascal says. “It’s healing in a way that Alex isn't completely conscious of, but he’s led by the language of music and starts to apply that language to his life."

On the day Esquire visited the set last January, it was also the first time Alex has this awakening. They were using the Newman Scoring Stage as the setting for the film’s very first sequence, in which Alex plays cello as part of a pulse-pounding session for Spies Don’t Run. This is an in-joke for Gilroy, who wrote all the Jason Bourne films and directed The Bourne Legacy. Consider it self-referential, but far from reverential.

Behemoth! pokes fun at Hollywood tropes while paying tribute to the unseen men and women whose music often make films stronger than they might be otherwise. “I've been obsessed with studio musicians since I was 19,” Gilroy says. “I came out to L.A. to try to be a studio musician, and I realized how deficient I was within three days of playing guitar.”

Around the recording stage, there are posters for the made-up Spies movie, but the face of the dark-haired, suave secret agent—billed as Dominic Diaz above the title—is obscured. During a break between set-ups, Pascal asks Gilroy: “Who is the equivalent of Dominic Diaz?” He flashes his eyebrows up and down. “Is it Pedro Pascal?”

Those eyebrows are furrowed a few minutes later as he consults with Timothy Loo, who trained the actor on how to believably play the cello. Pascal doesn’t claim to be on the same level, but now he can believably fake it. “I can do a terrible, terrible ‘Twinkle, Twinkle Little Star,’” he says.

After watching playback of a previous sequence, Loo says: “Your left hand looked great.”

Pascal shakes his head. “The bow got away from me. I don’t know what happened. But it won’t happen again.”

The two had a history even before they began intensive training for Behemoth!, since among Loo’s hundreds of credits is the contemplative cello from Pascal’s own corner of the Star Wars universe. “He's a kind of king amongst musicians for film in Los Angeles,” the actor explains later. “I had met so many of them and had been in the same room years earlier when Ludwig Göransson was scoring the first season of The Mandalorian.”

That happened to be right here, in the Newman Scoring Stage. This is another place where Behemoth! crosses over from real life to fiction and back again. Alex has his own personal history with this room, dating back decades. It’s where his trumpet-playing bandleader grandfather dropped dead after recording a jazzy, silly single (co-written by Bear and Gilroy.) It’s also where he first heard music in the womb. “His mother cut sessions here when she was pregnant with him. He's been on that stage prenatally,” Gilroy says. “There's no moment in his life where music isn't his fuel, or his oxygen. There's no part of his life where it isn't just a fundamental presence. But then what?”

That’s the harder question that the movie expects Alex to answer. “He happens to be a prodigy,” says Gilroy. “He happens to be brilliant at it, but you see a lot of things that have fallen away in his life, that have gone nowhere,” the filmmaker adds. “That’s been the way of his life the whole way through.”

Finally, he’s figured out a way to make music not just a refuge, but a way to guide himself out of this messy life he’s made. It’s a backwards way of living, but that’s fitting too.

Behemoth! was put together in a backwards way. The usual method is to shoot it and edit it first. Composers might be working on various themes in advance, but after they have a working print, they study its cuts while penning the final score to make sure the music cues hit at just the right moments. Gilroy and his team turned that approach around.

The finished film is a sort of remake. Gilroy filmed rough versions of some sequences with his cellphone camera and stitched those together so the composers could write their scores with precision before principal photography began.

Once cameras were rolling for real, he had to capture the footage in an unusually merciless way. Camera movements were timed to the exact second so they would match the length and crescendos of the pre-written music. For instance, as Pascal played along with the orchestra on the Spies Don’t Run theme, a camera rolled through the row of performers to end in a close-up of him, right before a cut to his memory of an intense, wintertime confrontation with Wilde’s obsessed lover.

That meant cinematographer Damián García, a veteran of Andor, had to get his camera crew to mimic the surrounding musicians, making sure every movement happened in perfect sync. After several tries in which they were just a hairsbreadth off of the Spies roll-up, Damian tells the camera operator: “Start six seconds later and move 10 percent slower.” That turns out to be the right tempo.

This precision is a hallmark of Gilroy’s storytelling. “I've been accused—and justifiably so—of being obsessed with people who are really good at things,” he says. “You can go back and look through the credits. Other than Dolores Claiborne, I'm trying to think if there’s a movie where everybody isn't the best at something. There's some aspect of that that must personally appeal to me, that makes me satisfied.”

IMDb backs him up. From Andor and Jason Bourne, to his Oscar-nominated writing and directing of Michael Clayton and even popcorn-fests like The Devil’s Advocate and the 1992 ice-skating romcom The Cutting Edge, Gilroy has a fascination with expertise. With Behemoth!, Gilroy explores what happens when being brilliant at one thing makes everything else fall short.

Expertise can help you see the world more clearly, but it can also help you hide from it. That’s what Pascal’s character in the film comes to see in his return to the movie music scene. Behemoth! isn’t necessarily autobiographical, but Gilroy says he feels a connection to that. “If you took everything away, I have the feeling I would still be able to go into a room and write my way out of it, and I think there is some satisfaction in that. But I don't think it protects you from everybody else.”

It’s the “everybody else” that Pascal’s Alex finally has to confront. The rest of his life may be out of tune, but this is the story of a man seeking balance. You might even call it harmony.

#7033. AI Platform Behind 12,000 E-mail Breaches Dismantled

The platform moved quickly. Since launching in February, EvilTokens had been tied to more than 12,000 compromised inboxes across over 10,000 organizations worldwide, according to Microsoft. Its victims included businesses and institutions in financial services, healthcare, construction, real estate, wholesale distribution, and higher education.

Microsoft and its partners seized 50 websites used to operate EvilTokens and disabled more than 150 additional domains supporting the service. The Metropolitan Police Service in the United Kingdom also arrested two men on Sept. 11 in connection with the alleged operation.

At the center of EvilTokens was an AI chatbot designed specifically for cybercrime. Once attackers gained access to an inbox, the chatbot could analyze messages, map relationships, identify employees involved in payments, and recommend ways to impersonate trusted contacts.

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

Business e-mail compromise has traditionally required attackers to manually comb through messages, learn how an organization handles payments, and find the right person to impersonate. However, EvilTokens simplified the entire process by automating much of that work.

The tools could quickly sort through a stolen inbox, summarize or translate messages, find invoices and wire-transfer discussions, and identify the people who moved money. EvilTokens could then draft messages that sounded like they came from the victim. It packaged all of this with mailbox access, phishing tools, management dashboards, and even customer support.

The service was sold through Telegram for a $1,500 startup fee, followed by a $500 monthly subscription. Microsoft's complaint also described a bulk e-mail tool that cost $600 a month and a more advanced e-mail delivery platform priced at $1,000 a month.

To break into a target, EvilTokens relied on device-code phishing. Victims were sent to a real Microsoft sign-in page and persuaded to enter an authentication code. The login process looked legitimate because it was, but the code quietly authorized a session controlled by the attacker.

That also made cleanup more complicated. The attackers stole OAuth tokens and authenticated sessions instead of passwords, so a password reset alone might not remove their access. Administrators also needed to revoke the stolen tokens and active sessions.

According to Microsoft's complaint,  EvilTokens used fake Microsoft 365 security notices and other branded templates to direct users into the device authentication flow. Once inside, attackers could use Microsoft Graph and other APIs to search and monitor a mailbox.

The company also found that AI played a role in creating EvilTokens itself. Investigators said much of the platform appeared to have been "vibe coded," with its developers using AI to quickly build and expand the service. EvilTokens reportedly drew on multiple AI models, including improperly integrated services from Groq and OpenAI.

The court authorized Microsoft to take control of or disable domains connected to the operation. Microsoft worked with Cloudflare, Coinbase, OpenAI, Railway, SpyCloud, the Shadowserver Foundation, and TRM Labs on the disruption. Health-ISAC joined the case because healthcare organizations were among the targets.

This marks the Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit's 40th court-authorized disruption and its first targeting an end-to-end, AI-enabled cybercrime service.

EvilTokens' infrastructure may be offline, but the model behind it is unlikely to disappear. The operation showed how AI can turn a stolen inbox into an immediately useful fraud playbook, allowing less-experienced criminals to launch attacks that once required considerable time and skill.
