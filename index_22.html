“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

A lower court said institutions may not know when accepting federal funds that they are accepting liability under Title IX, when Title VII typically covers employment claims.

School districts could get a hint of how the U.S. Supreme Court will rule on a case that could change how teachers and other school employees bring sex discrimination claims when the justices hear oral arguments next week.

In Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, the 11th U.S. Court of Appeals ruled in 2024 that Title IX’s right to sue doesn’t apply to employees, diverging from at least eight other appeals courts. At odds is whether school employees have the right to sue privately under Title IX outside of protections under Title VII.

Title IX protects anyone who interacts with a school — including students, employees and parents — from sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs and is often invoked by students who are harassed, assaulted or bullied due to their sex. Title IX protections for school employees include, for instance, prohibiting discrimination against teachers due to pregnancy or pregnancy-related conditions, among other things.

Meanwhile, Title VII prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex and national origin.

In Crowther, two University System of Georgia employees — a basketball coach at Georgia Tech and an art professor at Augusta University — said the university system discriminated against them on the basis of sex when it fired them.

Georgia Tech women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph was fired in 2019 shortly after she filed an internal complaint that alleged the university was discriminating against her and her program by not providing the same resources as the men’s program.

At Augusta University, art professor Thomas Crowther was told his contract would not be renewed in 2021 after several students reported allegedly inappropriate conduct in his class — including sexual harassment — which triggered a Title IX investigation. That investigation process and ensuing negative performance reviews, he said, discriminated against him on the basis of him being male.

Joseph and Crowther both reported the alleged discrimination to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, but also sued in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia under Title IX.

The two cases were combined before the 11th Circuit, which said in its 2024 decision that schools may not understand when accepting federal funds that they accept potential liability for damages of Title IX claims by employees, when those types of claims are typically filed under Title VII.

The Association of Title IX Administrators called the Supreme Court case "one of the most consequential decisions impacting the intersection of Title IX and employment law in decades."

Multiple major education and legal organizations have filed briefs in the case, including the National Education Association and the National Women's Law Center.

"The American education system faces a severe and worsening crisis in educator recruitment and retention," said NEA's brief that was filed earlier this year in favor of the former University System of Georgia employees. "Anti-discrimination protections affect whether talented professionals enter, remain in, or depart from careers."

The NEA said that a court decision implicates "a nationwide educator workforce crisis, the professional environment in which recruitment and retention decisions are made, and ultimately the quality of education available to students across the country."

"When sex discrimination drives talented educators from the profession, the consequences extend beyond individual career harm and cause systemic degradation of educational quality," the teachers union said.

Granting teachers the right to sue privately under Title IX would help improve an environment where sex discrimination "has remained pervasive," the NWLC said in its own brief. The center cited teachers' high rates of sexual harassment from their colleagues and students, unequal pay for female teachers despite their overrepresentation in the profession, a lack of leadership opportunities, and pregnancy discrimination, such as a lack of accommodations and supportive school policies.

Such a decision would also help address lower investments in girls' school sports and barriers to STEM in education programs, NWLC said.

Title IX "can only be meaningful for women who work in education if they can enforce their right to be free from sex discrimination, including in court," the organization argued.

However, the Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia maintained in its opposing brief filed in October 2025 that Title IX does not include a private right to sue and that petitioners "had every chance to prove their claims for employment-based sex discrimination under Title VII."

"Had Congress desired that employees of educational institutions have a bonus remedy for employment discrimination under Title IX, unburdened by Title VII’s exhaustion requirements and damage caps, it could have provided as much," the respondents said. "It has not."

That case was brought by Roderick Jackson, a high school girls’ basketball coach who spoke out to his supervisors after he noticed that his team was not receiving equal funding and equal access to athletic equipment and facilities. Following his complaints, he received negative work reviews and was ultimately removed from his position.

His lawsuit reached the high court, which decided that Title IX's private right to sue included retaliation claims for complaining about sex discrimination.

"Reporting incidents of discrimination is integral to Title IX enforcement and would be discouraged if retaliation against those who report went unpunished," the Supreme Court said in its decision. "Indeed, if retaliation were not prohibited, Title IX’s enforcement scheme would unravel.”

Read on for Post’s refreshingly down-to-earth tips.

No one can keep up with the incredible party décor shown on influencers’ posts, and you don’t have to try. Elegance can be found in any space, so host within the realities of your home. “You are not meant to be entertaining at the level of someone with a million-dollar budget and a gigantic home. You get to entertain at exactly your level, whatever that is,” Post says. “All the little touches and the things you do to make people feel comfortable and welcome in your home are what make for a great host.”

Bringing the wrong item can be an error on the part of the guest, but hosts should always make sure to minimize discomfort. “You want to take the etiquette high road and let it not be a problem. If [someone brings] an extra guest, quickly enlist someone from your household or a trusted guest to help you zhuzh the arrangements in a discreet way so that the extra person does not feel like they were not meant to be there… [We have] the nearest-and-dearest to the host take smaller portions, so that everyone can have a decent portion.”

Part of smartphone etiquette is letting others make their own choices. “You don’t overly control someone’s phone usage. Their rudeness will show on its own and will be noticed by more than just you… [Saying] ‘Could you make sure your phone is off, so you’re not tempted to use it throughout the evening?’ That is not a polite thing, even though it sounds gentle and so reasonable. That’s micromanagement.”

Bringing a thoughtful gift is always appreciated, but truly engaging with the event is what really matters. “There is no burden for hosting you. You’ve been invited and should feel welcome and like you belong in this space. You are not there to repay the efforts of the host. We are greeting our host. We are engaging with the other people. We are helping ourselves to the refreshments that are offered. If there are games or entertainment, we’re participating in that. If there’s a theme, we’ve showed up in the theme. That’s really how we’re a good guest.”

Always say goodbye. “At the end of the night, say thank you and goodbye and seek out the host, whether it’s a big open house party or it’s a small dinner party to make that connection and express gratitude before we go.”

Serving an item that a guest brought is at the host’s discretion. “A hostess gift is a hostess gift. It is not meant to be used that night with the people who brought it. It can be, but it does not have to be.”

Whether you mail a card or not, make sure to share your appreciation with your host. “Thank-you notes are a wonderful idea and they allow you an extra level of expression, a little more thought. It doesn’t mean that a text message the next day would not also be appreciated. Any version of thanking your host after a party is a great version.”

Interior designer Marie Flanigan likes to add a fresh twist to her seasonal decor with dried oranges and pops of cranberry red.

If there’s one classic Christmas element Grace hopes makes a comeback, it's multicolored lights. Inherently nostalgic, these bold-hued bulbs were a ’90s staple.

The classic red-and-green combination may take the title for the most iconic holiday palette, but this year, color trends are going complementary. While base colors stay warm and slightly rusted, patterned pieces are taking on cooler tones. This year, we’re also trading traditional plaid for Scottish tartan.

“Warm crimsons, rich browns, tactile fabrics, and blue-green plaids make a beautiful holiday palette,” says Elizabeth Bennett, cofounder of the interior design firm Kobel + Co.

The style has even coined a new holiday aesthetic: #Kitschmas, and, according to Lyle, it’s here to stay. “As AI continues to homogenize design, things made by humans will feel more special and important. Handmade and crafted items create stories around decor that feel meaningful and can be treasured year after year,” she says.

Moody browns are steadily emerging as the new neutral as we inch closer to the new year, and lucky for our winter design schemes, the hue really is as versatile as it seems. Paired with powdery blues, bold crimsons, and even minty greens, chocolate brown grounds nearly any palette and matches every metallic hue.

“I’m particularly drawn to chocolate brown as an unexpected foundation for holiday color palettes. It's incredibly rich and versatile. I love the idea of moving beyond traditional red and green and allowing the existing colors of a room to inform the holiday decor. You can create something incredibly festive through tonal layering, subtle metallic accents, and variations in material,” Flanigan explains.
