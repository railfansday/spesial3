With three bedrooms and two bathrooms, there was technically enough room for their family of four, and the architectural bones could easily meld with the relaxed, “summer camp in Maine” aesthetic Maggio chases in every project. “We needed to get smart about space planning and bring back a significant amount of character that had been stripped away,” she adds. In other words, the layout had to be revamped, and nearly every wall needed a dose of paneling, paint, or wallpaper—or preferably, some combination of the three.

The kitchen was first on the chopping block. Sandwiched between the laundry room and the dining room, the space felt cut off from the rest of the house in a way that wasn’t conducive to their family-focused, indoor-outdoor lifestyle. Maggio’s solution was to remove the laundry room wall, giving the kitchen access to the backyard, and finish the space with a painted beadboard ceiling to lend it a rustic, timeworn feel.

Mixed patterns, layered textures, and natural materials became Maggio’s way of capturing that summer camp nostalgia without creating a space that felt too formal for everyday life. The narrow living room lent itself well to a circular seating arrangement; when paired with a canopy-inspired wallcovering, the layout mirrored the act of sitting around a campfire to tell stories. Throughout, vintage furniture mingles with custom pieces, and personal touches abound, from the butcher block Maggio found on Facebook Marketplace to a chair left behind by a previous owner.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

On the other hand, Nicholas Reaves, who represented the petitioners, said Catholic families and schools "just want the same treatment" as others in being able to participate in the universal preschool program.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

#7150. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

#7122. DOL final rule revamps antidiscrimination requirements for apprenticeships

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the time, then-Acting Secretary of Labor Julie Su said in a press release, “Equity and job quality have marked the most successful Registered Apprenticeship programs for workers and employers alike. This proposed rule codifies the Department of Labor’s strong commitment to these principles.”

Martha Stewart has a reliable dinner-party gift: “Fresh eggs from my chickens,” she told ELLE Decor. It helps to have a flock, of course, but the rest of us can borrow the principle. Bring something your host will enjoy having around tomorrow, when the guests have gone and there’s no dinner to get on the table.

That’s especially good advice when the invitation comes with a request to bring nothing. There’s no need to arrive with another course or flowers that require immediate attention. If you’d like to bring a small thank-you, the designers and entertaining experts we spoke to have suggestions—and a few reservations about the things guests tend to grab on the way over.

“When a host says ‘don’t bring anything,’ I think they are fairly certain they have enough food and beverage, and probably don’t want excess,” says Amber Mayfield Hewett, founder of event production company To Be Hosted and author of Your Turn to Host. “I wouldn’t bring anything that needs to be served or pressures the host to make accommodations.”

An unexpected dish can put the host in an awkward position, particularly if they’ve planned around guests’ dietary restrictions. Interior designer Christina Nielsen generally avoids bringing food she expects to be served, though she makes exceptions for close friends whose tastes she knows. For them, she might arrive with a freshly baked baguette or pastries. Unless you have that level of familiarity, ask before bringing something for the table.

Nielsen’s suggestions include chocolates, good olive oil, and a bottle of wine intended for another night. Event designer and etiquette expert Hovik Harutyunyan adds coffee, tea, specialty honey, and cocktail olives, as these can all go into the pantry without becoming part of the evening’s preparations.

Choose according to what your host enjoys. Wine is appropriate for someone whose preferences you know, but it needn’t be the automatic purchase for every invitation. Andria Mitsakos, founder and creative director of the brand Anthologist, suggests gifts with a personal connection. She often brings friends things she has found on her travels.

“A gift doesn’t need to be expensive, and it certainly doesn’t need to have anything to do with dinner,” she says. Her other suggestions also include a book the recipient would enjoy or fingerless gloves for a friend who is always cold. “I love gifts that demonstrate you’ve paid attention to someone.”

For a friend whose interiors you know well, Nielsen likes a small vintage object, cocktail napkins, or candleholders. But there’s no need to choose something for the house if you’re less familiar with the owner’s taste—chocolates may be the better bet, she emphasizes.

Flowers seem easy until you’re the one answering the door with a bouquet in your arms and a saucepan on the stove. Nielsen points out that loose stems require a vase, trimming, and arranging while guests are arriving.

Harutyunyan would save them for afterward. Even an arrangement that needs no attention may not suit the room or table the host has prepared. He recommends sending flowers with a handwritten thank-you card one to three days after the dinner.

Arriving empty-handed doesn’t mean you’ve missed your chance. Hewett suggests a handwritten note or a gift card for coffee the following morning. A few specific lines about the evening give your host something they can enjoy without having to unwrap, refrigerate, or arrange it.

While you’re there, be interested in the other guests, enjoy the meal, and offer a helping hand. Hewett says assisting in the kitchen or staying to load the dishwasher can mean a great deal. Take your cue from the host, though—someone who declines help may genuinely prefer you to stay at the table.

“Being a guest is more important than the gift itself,” Nielsen says. “Be engaged and fun!” You can send flowers tomorrow. Tonight, they’ve invited you for your company.

#7086. This Colorful California Mountain Home Gives Classic Ski House Style a Fresh New Look

Using the surrounding landscape as palette inspiration, designer Alden Fletcher brought 70s retro flair to this family retreat.

When most people think of a classic ski chalet, they picture log-lined walls, dark corners, a raging hearth, plenty of plaid, and maybe even a taxidermied deer or two. But a certain kind of magic strikes when high-altitude architecture hits the right notes, and this Truckee, California, retreat is a masterclass in making the mountains sing.

Built almost 20 years ago, this family holiday home boasted all the 90s mountain classics: an abundance of knotty pine, traditional slate floors, and a minimalist beige palette. Undoubtedly stylish for the time, the retreat now felt out of step with the Bay Area family looking to make it into a personalized winter escape. The owners knew updates were imminent (necessary, even), but they feared losing the home’s classic California soul. Fighting modernism’s white-box aesthetic, they called in Alden Fletcher of the eponymous San Francisco-based design firm to meld the home’s retro architecture with a fresh, forest-inspired brief.

Noting the owner’s concerns and dedication to sustainability, the project’s goal was not to remodel for grandeur, but to restore the home’s charm. Fletcher’s task was to design around storied memories, the family’s obvious love for winter sports, and their passion for entertaining—all while combining the crisp aesthetic of the alpine wilderness with cozy textiles and natural details to create the ultimate mountain sanctuary. By pulling saturated hues from the surrounding landscape and sourcing perky patterns inspired by the local culture, the redesign breathes new life into the slope-side abode without sacrificing sentiment.

FAST FACTSDesigner: Alden Fletcher Interiors Stylist: Beth Protass Location: Truckee, CAThe Space: A five-bedroom, four-bathroom home across 3,000 square feet

The home's hallways set the tone for the entire design scheme. On the first floor, just beyond the main door, wall-mounted ski runners double as decor. Upstairs, Fletcher redefined the boundaries of the primary suite to carve out a nook for a vignette displaying the home’s most bespoke decor piece: a vintage cherry-red chairlift. “The home’s location is unique,” adds Fletcher. “It sits on a ski slope, and you can view chairlifts out the back windows!”

A true gathering space, the living room was designed around the original stone-faced fireplace, though Fletcher did replace the former pine mantel with white oak, connecting the floor details with the exposed oak beams running along the ceiling.

Swiveling burnt orange armchairs accompany an inky indigo sectional. Both gather around a button-tufted ottoman, which doubles as a coffee perch or game table. Shaded sconces flank the fireplace to give the room a glow, even when the hearth is unlit, while a pair of vintage ski shoes hang on the wall, crowning the design.

In the playroom, the deep green hue of the wallpaper paired with the natural texture of knotty pine borrows inspiration from the forest-like landscape just beyond the window. Vertical lines carried through both the wallpaper and the wainscoting create the illusion of height, while playful ski motifs bring the local sport’s culture into the play space.

“The clients are sustainably minded and didn't want to get rid of what was working,” says Fletcher. With conservation in mind, she opted to keep the original perimeter cabinets, refreshing them with paint, new hardware, and Virginia Black Satin Granite countertops, a sturdier alternative to show-stopping soapstone.

To optimize the space, she nixed the two existing islands, reimagining the room to fit a larger iteration that boasts a sink and easily seats five. The design team then added upper cabinets, transforming the space into a storage haven. Later, she placed a library ladder and golden hardware for easy access and a touch of whimsy. Then, she turned her attention to making sure the lighting was up to par. Fletcher firmly believes a beautiful space should be equally as functional. To ensure no detail fell short, she installed discreet recessed lights alongside under-cabinet lighting, and two large glass pendants to adorn the space. All dimmable for ambiance—of course. “There’s nothing worse than a home kitchen that is just flooded with bright light everywhere. A kitchen should still feel cozy and warm, and good lighting is the key to accomplishing that,” says Fletcher.

Fletcher compensated for this former powder room’s lack of windows with vibrant wallpaper to energize the space and make the most of interior lighting. Borrowing space from an adjacent closet, she converted this former two-piece bath into a full retreat with a shower, vanity, and storage, finishing the space with gold hardware for an additional touch of luxury.

Perhaps the most customized room in the home, the mudroom boasts bold color and smart storage solutions for a space as functional as it is chic. The custom cabinet is an ode to smart design, featuring bench seating, pegs, an overhead shelf, and under-seat shoe storage—but the real showstopper is its bold red hue.

“My favorite element of the built-in is the color. The brick color has just the right amount of earthiness,” says Fletcher.

The family loves to entertain, often hosting large groups of friends and family. To accommodate their social lifestyle, Fletcher designed a hospitality hub. Removing a bathroom door, she altered the room’s layout, allowing space for this guest suite to come to life.

With the new dimensions secured, she covered the walls in printed wallpaper inspired by the great local outdoors. The bold pattern set the tone for the rest of the design as she pulled colors, patterns, and themes to complement it.

For the primary bedroom, the theme was warmth: a warm bed, warm home, warm soul, all channeled through muted terracotta walls. But before it could truly transform, the team had to iron out a few oddball details.

“This room used to have a few odd details, including transom windows to the adjoining bathroom and an exterior door to a deck. We removed those elements, which allowed for a better furniture layout and a cozier feeling,” says Fletcher.

The room features a traditional four-poster bed in a nearly black finish, which carries over to the lighting details and furniture hardware, bringing out the warmth and depth of the surrounding rich red hues.

For the primary bath, the team capitalized on the luxury of the sloped-side views, framing a classic 400-pound slipper tub with a nearly floor-to-ceiling window featuring vintage pine trim. Truly the focal point of the design, Fletcher finished the window treatment with woven shades to tie in the natural wood details of the home and stand up to moisture. Natural grain runs through the room’s doors and vanity, topped with soft white Calacatta marble, channeling the classic ski chalet aesthetic. Gold fixtures extend from the vanity to the lighting, towel rack, and tub, for a touch of glamour.

Boasting the project’s biggest splurge, a custom bunk-trundle bed, the kid’s room combines playful hues with forest-inspired details and maximum sleepover potential. “We wanted to maximize sleeping spots in this home,” Fletcher explains. “The Casa Kids bunk bed in the kid's room was a splurge, but it can sleep 5!”

Alden Fletcher first fell in love with design while working as an art teacher and refurbishing furniture in her downtime. In 2020, she grew that passion into a full-service design firm focused on bringing functionality to storied designs and creating ultra-personalized interiors.

#7081. 9 Curb Appeal Trends That Will Take Over in 2027, According to Designers

These are the exterior elements that are getting a major refresh.

Curb appeal is more than having a pretty house.

“It's not just about how a home looks, but how it lives,” says Danielle Faustini, landscape designer and owner of Helix Green Designs. Homeowners and designers alike are stepping up their game when it comes to creating an appealing facade—in some cases, the shift is quite literal, as stairs are becoming an “it” feature.

The latest U.S. Houzz Outdoor Trends study is seeing a shift toward natural elements like wood cladding, beige exteriors, and native plants.

"Professionals on Houzz have shared that homeowners are looking for exteriors that feel grounded and connected to their surroundings," says Annie Thornton, Houzz outdoor trends expert. “Warm beiges and wood siding blend naturally with stone, native plantings and other organic landscape materials, and they suit a wide range of architectural styles. Our studies show that people are moving away from the stark white and cool gray palettes of recent years toward something that feels more timeless, a look that adds a sense of history without tipping into nostalgia.”

Simple upgrades, like a fresh coat of paint on the front door, are also pivoting into colorways anchored in the past. Designer Alexis Warren is seeing a shift toward darker, moodier front doors in shades of green, deep purple, and near-black, like Farrow & Ball’s Paean Black.

Here are 10 curb-appeal-boosting design trends for the year ahead to make your house the envy of the neighborhood.

Introducing a sculptural element—like an outdoor artwork or creatively placed flora—sends a message that your house has a point of view.

“It elevates the sense of arrival and separates your home from other homes in the vicinity,” says designer Lisa Erdmann. To ensure your home truly stands out, look for one-of-a-kind pieces.

For the home pictured above, Robert J. Bell, founder of landscape architecture firm Bell Design, commissioned artisans to create a copper light inspired by a feather. “More than the end of minimalism, the new focus on sculptural shapes brings back a sense of craft, and the expressive and sensory nature that a simple black metal and glass fixture cannot possess,” he says.

To contrast against the patina, Bell chose purple bougainvillea and a sunny yellow paint on the door.

It’s all about adding warmth and texture, and that’s one of the reasons that beige exterior colors have more than doubled in popularity in the last two years, jumping from 10 to 24 percent, according to Houzz.

Think of a palette that extends into the landscape—warm neutrals such as tan, soft cream, khaki, sand, and greige, says Thornton, who also notes that natural wood and wood-look cladding, sandy stucco, and stone are a welcome change.

Adding paneling can mimic the warmth of wood without worrying about upkeep, points out Francisca Alonso, cofounder and CEO of AV Architects + Builders. This trick also helps soften the front elevation. “We like to combine minimalistic modern details with warm tones to make it more inviting and less commercial-looking,” Alonso adds.

A picket fence may be just what your house needs, but that doesn’t mean you have to resort to the predictable white paint.

“Adding a low-profile picket fence is one of the best ways to gently delineate a property line without in any way compromising sightlines of the home,” says Joelle Nesen, principal designer at Maison Inc. “The picket fence has the kind of classical lines that work well with an historic home like this Craftsman in Portland. By not defaulting to the expected white and leaving the fence a natural wood, the picket fence, with its carefully spaced slats that are almost see-through, allows it to blend in with the landscaping and trees that surround the home.”

When you choose gravel over grass or pavers, it’s all about an understated but classy look.

“Gravel and other permeable aggregates like crushed shells are some of my favorite materials for creating more informal outdoor living spaces,” says Faustini. “It is a beautifully tactile and sensory material. Who doesn't love the soft crunch underfoot? And it really softens a space, helping blur the line between outdoor living areas and the surrounding landscape.”

The look works especially well with decking and patinated light fixtures.

Adding native plants around your house feels cohesive, relaxed, and rooted in place, Thornton says.

Rachael Grochowski, founder and principal of RHG Architecture + Design, adds that she expects to see an increase in native landscapes, meadow-type gardens, as well as an integration of clover into lawns.

“The trend to embrace natural landscapes is not only about supporting environment regeneration, but it also supports our wellbeing by instigating wonder, beauty, and randomness,” she says.

Curb appeal is all about first impressions, and homeowners are not taking this feature lightly. Houzz reports that searches for “front walkway” have more than quadrupled (up 357 percent) in the first three quarters of 2026, compared with the same period last year.

Step design, in particular, is one feature that is growing in popularity.

When Sarah Crawford, associate designer with Courtney B. Smith Design, renovated her home, she added steps to her 1920s bungalow to literally and figuratively elevate the experience. The home initially had just one step from the sidewalk to the front door. By adding a series of stairs to gently guide the way to the door, she added a quiet sense of elegance in keeping with the wood-shingled exterior. Key to the transformation was selecting materials that reflect the character and style of the original home, in this case reclaimed granite. Scale and balance were essential to adding a series of stairs to gently guide visitors to the door.

“Steps should never be narrower than the doorway,” Crawford says. “In fact, the wider the steps, the more inviting the approach.”

For a different approach to the front entrance, consider stone pathways, especially those with a casual look, like grass pavers.

#7075. Here’s What It Takes to Keep a Pool Running During the Winter, According to Experts

Before you dive in, it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved.

When most people think of backyard swimming pools, relaxing outdoors in the summertime immediately comes to mind. But some homeowners don’t want the swimming season to end when temperatures drop. Fans of cold-water swimming are starting to consider ways to enjoy their pools throughout the winter as a part of an at-home wellness experience.

“Ten years ago, most people didn’t think winter swims were possible, especially in colder climates,” says Karen Larson, cofounder of Soake Pools. “Times have changed because these days, they’re seeing neighbors, friends, and others do it and post the proof on social media.”

The increasing interest in cold-water experiences is part of a broader wellness trend. In American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, cold therapies, including cold plunges, cryotherapy, and cold showers, earned the 17th spot among fitness trends for the year, up from number 20 in its 2025 report.

If you’ve previously closed your swimming pool around Labor Day but are interested in making cold-water swimming a part of your daily wellness routine, there’s a lot to keep in mind. Not every existing pool is a good candidate for keeping open for winter swimming, and it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved. But if you’re curious, here’s what to consider about keeping a pool open year-round.

Why is cold-water swimming becoming more common? There are numerous purported mental and wellness benefits, from athletic recovery to stress relief and better sleep. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS ONE found that cold-water immersion led to a reduction in stress for 12 hours afterward as well as improvements in sleep quality and overall quality of life.

“Winter swimming often creates a time where friends and family can come together without distractions,” adds Larson.

That said, while swimming during the colder months has its perks, there are also several risks to keep in mind. A 2020 review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that cold-water immersion can trigger an immediate shock response, leading to involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, and a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure. These reactions can make it difficult to control your breathing and increase your risk of drowning. Cold temps can also impair your muscle strength and nerve function, making it challenging to swim safely.

It’s a good idea to acclimate to the cold water gradually and avoid prolonged exposure. You’ll also want to make sure there’s someone else home while you take your cold-water plunge in the pool.

In addition, if you have underlying conditions like heart disease, consult your doctor before all else, as the cold water may place additional strain on your heart.

If you’re trying to consider whether your pool is a good candidate to keep open during the colder months, these are the things to look into.

The cost of keeping a swimming pool open for winter swimming will depend on your location, size, desired water temperature, and the type of heater you use.

“Routine pool maintenance generally remains about the same year-round, although chemical consumption may decrease depending on how frequently the pool is used,” explains Jim Eisch, Jr., vice president of operations at Pinch A Penny Pool Patio Spa. “The biggest expense to consider in the winter is heating the pool.”

For this reason, prospective winter swimmers may want to consider an upgrade to their heating system. Instead of using pricey natural gas heating or a traditional heat pump, Eisch says that inverter technology is one of the most affordable ways to maintain a swimmable pool temperature year-round.

For example, if you live in Florida and have a 10,000-gallon swimming pool, Eisch estimates your annual heating costs will be $2,467 with a natural gas heater, $741 with a traditional heat pump, and $434 for a heat/cool pump that uses inverter technology.

As aforementioned, the goal with wintertime swimming is not to be soaking in bathwater-like temperatures while it’s freezing outside. For safety purposes, winter swims are quick, and the heating system is to keep your chlorinated pool water well above the 19-degree Fahrenheit freezing point.

Unsurprisingly, a standard 14-by-28-foot swimming pool is going to be one of the hardest to use effectively year-round, unless you live in a region with a mild wintertime climate.

“You can technically use a large pool year-round, but it’s difficult to cover all its surface area well enough to hold in the heat, so heating bills can climb fast,” says Larson.

Therefore, smaller sizes like a 10-by-16-foot cocktail pool or plunge pool are better candidates for keeping open. Dedicated winter swimmers should also invest in an easy on-and-off cover—or a motorized auto cover—for added protection.

Regular pool cleaning and maintenance are essential, and this upkeep over the winter is no exception.

“Check the filtration system regularly to make sure it’s circulating smoothly, monitor the water chemistry, and plan for power outages—because there’s a good chance they will happen,” Larson explains. For this reason, you should also invest in a generator that can keep the pump running if the power goes out.

Safe pool access is just as important as any other aspect of the pool’s design. That’s why a nonslip surface, excellent drainage, and ample lighting are key.

“Your design should also provide a clear path from the house to the water,” says Neha Gupta, interior designer and founder at NU Curations.

Especially in the wintertime when ice and snow are a factor in your region, make sure you have a safe and smooth exit plan for getting out of the water, bundled up, and into your warm home without any obstacles. And then, of course, remember to cover the pool back up to keep it protected and ready for your next plunge.

The artificial intelligence industry is expected to generate $2.67 trillion in worldwide spending this year, according to a new forecast from Gartner. But relatively little of that money will be spent on the generative AI models dominating the industry's headlines.

The much bigger business is everything required to make them run.

Worldwide spending on AI infrastructure will reach nearly $1.5 trillion in 2026, accounting for about 56% of all AI spending. Gartner expects spending on generative AI models themselves to total just $28.3 billion. Put another way, for every dollar Gartner expects to be spent on generative AI models this year, more than $52 will be spent on AI infrastructure.

It is a striking measure of how the economics of the AI boom are changing. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other models may be the most visible products of the generative AI era, but much of the money is going to the enormous buildout of servers, semiconductors, networks, cloud capacity, and other infrastructure required to support them.

"The buildout of AI data center capacity is the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken," John-David Lovelock, distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, said in announcing the forecast.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to increase 49.5% this year, from about $1.79 trillion in 2025 to $2.67 trillion in 2026. Infrastructure represents $1.484 trillion of that total. The next-largest categories are AI services, at $576.5 billion, and AI software, at $461.6 billion. Spending on AI agents and assistants is expected to reach $29.2 billion, only slightly more than the $28.3 billion Gartner forecasts for generative AI models.

The numbers also show just how quickly expectations are changing. In January, Gartner forecast $2.53 trillion in total AI spending for 2026, including $1.37 trillion for infrastructure. By May, those forecasts had risen to $2.60 trillion and $1.43 trillion, respectively. The latest forecast raises them again, to $2.67 trillion overall and $1.48 trillion for infrastructure.

Since January, Gartner has added roughly $143 billion to its 2026 AI spending estimate. About $118 billion of that increase, or roughly 83%, comes from a higher infrastructure forecast.

That is significant because infrastructure was already, by far, the largest component of Gartner's AI spending estimate. As the firm's expectations for the AI market have risen during 2026, most of the additional money has accumulated there.

The infrastructure category is broad. Gartner includes AI-optimized cloud infrastructure, servers, networking, AI processors, and devices. Demand for those systems remains strong despite rising memory prices, Lovelock said.

"The capacity growth from hyperscalers and service providers purchasing AI-optimized servers will continue to be the largest single area of spending," he said.

#7047. Report: AI's Productivity Gains in Science Tempered by Time Spent Validating Outputs

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. The authors describe this as a verification tax associated with the high value science places on reliable and correct results.

#7043. More Oversight: California Governor Proposes Independent Watchdogs Within AI Safety System

California Governor Gavin Newsom is proposing a new model of artificial intelligence regulation that would give independent organizations a formal role in evaluating the safety claims made by AI giants.

The effort could eventually include placing outside evaluators inside frontier AI laboratories and requiring companies to maintain an independently tested emergency shutdown mechanism, commonly described as a "kill switch."

Those requirements are not yet California law.

Newsom's Sept. 18 executive order directs state officials to study the proposals and recommend possible changes to existing law by Nov. 16. It does not immediately require OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or other developers to install a shutdown mechanism.

The distinction matters because the phrase "AI kill switch" suggests a technical safeguard California has already imposed. For now, it is one element of a broader oversight system the state is assembling around the developers of its most powerful AI models.

Under the order, the California Government Operations Agency and the Governor's Office of Emergency Services must consult outside experts about whether the state should require large frontier developers to embed independent verification organizations in their laboratories.

The evaluators could conduct periodic audits and determine whether company safety frameworks and risk assessments meet adequate standards. They could also test whether an emergency shutdown mechanism would work as intended.

The order asks officials to consider expanding the definition of a reportable critical safety incident to include cases in which an AI system appears to evade controls or operate beyond its intended boundaries.

Newsom said the action was prompted by recent incidents involving AI agents defeating security restrictions, as well as the absence of comprehensive federal legislation.

"We're not waiting to act," Newsom said in the state's announcement of the order. "We're going to speed up our work on substantial and responsible AI oversight before it's too late."

The executive order builds on two laws Newsom signed on Sept. 9. Together, Senate Bill 813 and Assembly Bill 1405 begin regulating the organizations hired to inspect AI systems.

California's earlier frontier AI law, SB 53, requires large developers to publish safety frameworks and report specified critical incidents. The new laws begin addressing a separate question: Who checks whether those disclosures and assessments are reliable?

SB 813 requires the Government Operations Agency to develop standards for designating independent verification organizations, or IVOs. Those organizations must demonstrate that they have the expertise to assess AI risks and explain the measurements and methods supporting their conclusions.

The Alliance is focused less on how agents are built and more on how organizations can govern them consistently across vendor boundaries.

#7023. AI Leaders Warn of Risks at United Nations Security Council Briefing

The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

#7017. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

It was a shock, then, to talk to my older coworkers, most of whom had been involved with or studied the Black radical tradition, and hear their cautious indifference to what felt like a coming revolution. “We’ll see,” most of them said—a pragmatism about this country that I didn’t fully understand back then. Back then I hoped, deep down, that what those older coworkers were experiencing was merely sour grapes.

In 2026, of course, we are all living through the political backlash that they were sure was coming and the missteps of moderation during the Obama years that set the stage for Trump. I don’t really hope about politics anymore. Yes, I know hope is a discipline. Hope is something that, by definition, you have to have when things are dire. But the way hope lives in my body—a frantic, desperate thing that plummets when it meets catastrophe—means national politics aren’t a good place for that emotion. When I talk about politics now with friends, it’s mostly to see who can make the darkest joke about our current timeline.

But after last night, things are different. Like a lot of people, I’m allowing myself one calendar day to actually feel excited about the state of the world. I’m still saying “Let’s see,” but for right now, every time someone tries to bring up why we’re still doomed, I keep scrolling. I can do bad all by myself tomorrow.

Since Trump entered the scene 10 years ago, there’s been an active disdain for sincerity in politics. The reasons for this are many. Trump and MAGA ran on the belief that becoming great could only be measured by how badly the people you hated were suffering. Those who would oppose Trump grew cynical about the national Democratic party’s dance of fearmongering about the state of the country and steadfastly refusing to do anything meaningful about it when given the power to do so. Between these two extremes, it felt foolish to care. It felt smarter and safer to view politics the way a consultant or a pundit might, with the distance of someone who keeps telling you he has no skin in the game. As if that position was a superior one. As if that distance could save you any heartbreak or keep the threat of a layoff or a medical bill or an eviction at bay.

To care is to be cringe, and in the last few years, being cringe has been one of the greatest cultural sins, a devastating social faux pas. But last night was such a seismic shift in American politics that the idea of cringe is currently blissfully irrelevant. Being cringe enough to care brought millions to the polls and a historic midterm turnout. Being cringe enough to care meant that people like James Talarico, Abdul El-Sayed, and Angie Nixon launched campaigns even as everyone told their potential base that voting was irrelevant, that nothing could ever change about their states.

I’m not the same person I was in 2008; hopefully none of us are. And this is not the same country. But to make sure this country is one where I want to be present, I have to believe on some level that it’s possible. To change anything in this world, you first have to care enough to want to do it.
