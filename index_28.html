#5986. Outer Spaces takes solar beyond the rooftop with integrated backyard energy system

Residential solar is finding a new place to call home: the backyard.

Outer Spaces, a new company founded by the entrepreneurs behind outdoor living brand Outer, is introducing a solar-powered pavilion that combines photovoltaic generation, battery storage and usable outdoor living space. The company’s first product, OS1, can be designed for multiple functions, including an outdoor kitchen, office, gym or gathering space, while generating electricity and providing backup power for the home.

Outer Spaces is planning to produce outdoor structures that integrate solar PV and energy storage. Credit: Outer Spaces

The California-based company is positioning OS1 as a new category of residential energy infrastructure that goes beyond conventional rooftop arrays and garage-mounted batteries. It builds on the growing interest in solar structures that also serve a secondary purpose.

OS1 combines a solar pavilion with a finished outdoor power deck. Solar panels integrated into the overhead structure generate electricity, while a thin battery storage system is housed beneath the deck. According to Outer Spaces, the combination allows the structure to produce energy throughout the day and provide backup power during outages.

Rather than competing exclusively with traditional rooftop solar, OS1 offers homeowners an additional way to use available property for energy production, particularly when roof aesthetics, space limitations, or homeowner preferences become obstacles to conventional installations.

Outer Spaces describes OS1 as a modular system designed for rapid on-site assembly, with minimal to no excavation required. The company says installation avoids traditional construction steps such as concrete pouring and lumber cutting. Once the structure is assembled, a licensed electrician then connects its energy system to the customer’s home.

The product’s first availability is scheduled for 2027, beginning in Southern California and select additional markets; pricing is expected to be announced in early 2027.

Solar canopies and pergolas have increasingly incorporated features such as EV charging, lighting, fans and weather protection, giving homeowners additional reasons to invest in solar outside the traditional rooftop model.

Outer Spaces represents another potential avenue for expanding residential solar adoption through design-driven products. The opportunity will ultimately depend on pricing, installation economics, permitting requirements and the system’s measurable energy performance.

Outer Spaces plans to showcase OS1 publicly for the first time at RE+ 2026, Nov. 16 to 19, in Las Vegas.

The string of outages has fueled speculation about whether the incidents are connected, though Trump administration officials have ruled out a coordinated cyberattack.

National security experts caution that while the outages do not establish malicious activity, they underscore the stakes of protecting systems that underpin everything from financial transactions to military operations.

#5949. Solar lease holdouts still see value in privately-owned residential projects

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act drastically reduced the lifespan of federal subsidies available to certain segments of the solar market, and among the first to go was the investment tax credit for residential projects. The ITC is a historic renewable energy subsidy that provides a 30% tax credit against the total cost of an eligible project, and it is currently available to the commercial and utility-scale solar segments.

A residential solar project in New York installed by Nickels Energy Solutions. Credit: Nickels Energy Solutions

After 2025, homeowners hoping to install solar on their house couldn’t subsidize the project’s cost with this federal subsidy if it was privately owned. However, a loophole in the tax credit language has allowed leased or “third party-owned” (TPO) solar projects to still receive the ITC (25D) on residential solar projects. In this scenario, it matters who owns the solar project, not necessarily where it’s built. The project can be subsidized because it is owned by a commercial company, and not the homeowner.

Some residential solar installers are still strictly pursuing and installing privately-owned solar projects. Nickels Energy Solutions (NES), a residential solar contractor working in Central New York believes a privately-owned PV system is still the better deal for the customer.

“We don’t have the same offers as some of the bigger companies,” said Kevin Nickels, VP of sales with NES. “If you can offer leases, it’s like the ‘haves and have-nots.’ I don’t have that loophole or that trick up my sleeve, so it forces me to get the project sold, while I’m as sharp as I can be with margins to make it somewhat competitive.”

TPO solar transactions are typically arranged at a lower up-front cost than a wholly owned solar project. Lease contract lengths vary by provider, and financiers have surety in these transactions because the projects will be built with products from an approved vendor list (AVL). Products on an AVL can qualify for a domestic content subsidy, another tax credit in addition to the ITC, because they are manufactured using a certain percentage of domestic components.

These are premium products vetted for AVLs. But it can also mean that installers are paying premium prices for components in building TPO solar projects.

Nickels believes that, while a privately owned residential solar project will cost more at the start, the combination of expensive components and interest rates for financing over a lease term — with factors like rate increases — can cost a customer more in the long run.

“I don’t think all leases are bad,” he said. “I just don’t think it’s what’s best for the industry or the homeowner.”

Rudy Wright, owner of Wright-Way Solar of Tyler, Texas, has shifted the company’s focus from building new projects to servicing existing systems. As the ITC exited the residential solar market, so did many of the lenders financing these projects. Without lending options, Wright-Way’s installation output has slowed down, and those clients who can afford solar are paying with cash or using a home equity loan to cover the cost.

Despite that, Wright said he isn’t very interested in building leased solar projects.

Wright-Way Solar built a solar plus storage project at a Ranch in Texas featuring arrays that are facing east, west and south. Credit: Wright-Way Solar

“I do think it cost me some of those entry level clients, but every time I analyzed the financing end of it, I felt like I was benefitting my company and the industry more than the customer,” Wright said. “I haven’t seen the angle where bringing a pre-purchase agreement or bringing a lease agreement has enough ironclad return for the customer.”

Tyler is a unique market for solar because many of Wright-Way’s customers are trying to make their properties more resilient, and less reliant on the grid, by installing solar and storage, and not just seeking cost savings for their energy use. Wright has installed solar plus storage systems on Texan ranches at a scale close a small commercial project.

That promise of resilience is still enough reason for some people to commission a solar project in Texas, where the state’s independent electrical grid operator has faltered and experienced dangerous outages in recent years.

“Folks realize money in the bank doesn’t do you any good if the power goes out,” Wright said.

NES is still building a steady pipeline of residential solar projects in New York. Nickels said that has been possible in part because many of its clients are upper-middle class; but New York also maintains an incentive program for solar projects, NY-Sun, which includes a 25% state tax credit for residential systems.

“The OBBBA caught a lot of states flat-footed,” Nickels said. “Without federal support, it puts a lot of pressure on what the state is offering.”

Some states’ solar subsidy programs have stepped in where federal support has diminished since the OBBBA was passed. The North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center maintains the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency, which analyzes which states currently have policies and incentives supporting clean energy developments. Every state has policy enabling solar projects, but not necessarily cost incentives for individual projects.

While traditional residential solar projects have lost a significant subsidy, the reality is that energy costs have risen in response to new grid demands from hyperscale data centers and oil scarcity from the United States’ war in Iran. Sure, a privately owned residential solar project won’t receive the ITC, but it can still stabilize energy costs without them.

“We lost a big incentive, but the rate increase is proportional,” Nickels said.

#5940. Three Routes to VPP Revenue for Installers, EPCs, and Developers Utility programs, wholesale markets, and bilateral contracts each come with different eligibility rules, payment structures, and commercial responsibilities.

By Intersolar & Energy Storage North America

In June 2026, Google and Voltus announced a three-year agreement to unlock up to 100 megawatts (MW) of distributed capacity annually in PJM. Under Voltus’s Bring Your Own Capacity model, Google funds procurement while Voltus aggregates qualifying distributed energy resources (DERs) and compensates participating customers.1

The deal is one of three distinct paths to earn revenue from DERs. Across the U.S., utility programs, wholesale electricity markets, and bilateral contracts each have their own eligibility requirements, payment structures and commercial responsibilities.

Utility programs offer an established pathway for compensating customers and asset owners who reduce demand, shift consumption or discharge stored energy during periods of grid need. Payments may be tied to enrollment, committed capacity, measured performance or a combination of these factors. Massachusetts, for example, offers residential battery customers $275 per kW of average summer performance through ConnectedSolutions2.

Beyond customer incentive programs, utilities also procure distributed flexibility through contracts to address local grid constraints or broader capacity needs. Unlike standardized incentives, these arrangements can establish specific delivery requirements and compensation terms, creating opportunities for developers and service providers to assemble and manage DER portfolios. During a July 2026 heat event, Green Mountain Power dispatched 90 MW of stored energy capacity, helping customers avoid an estimated $6 million in peak-related costs during one peak hour3.

Aggregated DERs can earn revenue from energy, capacity, and ancillary services in wholesale electricity markets, subject to regional market rules and resource qualification. Unlike utility-specific programs, wholesale markets can provide access to broader regional revenue opportunities and multiple grid services. For developers and installers, participation typically occurs through an aggregator or other qualified market participant rather than through direct market registration.

While Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 2222 established a broader framework for DER participation, uneven implementation across regional markets continues to constrain commercial opportunities for aggregated resources. Market access does not automatically translate into revenue, as individual resources must still meet qualification requirements and comply with market-specific rules. New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), for example, requires DER aggregations to meet minimum capacity and real-time telemetry requirements, which can increase participation costs and limit the economic viability of smaller resources.

Wholesale participation can also expose VPP portfolios to fluctuating market prices, performance obligations and restrictions on receiving compensation for overlapping services. In Texas, ERCOT’s Aggregate Distributed Energy Resource (ADER) pilot allows aggregated DERs to participate in wholesale energy and ancillary services markets, subject to qualification, telemetry, and dispatch requirements.

Bilateral contracts allow utilities and large electricity consumers to procure distributed flexibility directly from aggregators and service providers. Growing interest from data center operators and other large energy buyers is expanding the potential market for VPP capacity beyond traditional utility programs. Unlike standardized programs, bilateral agreements can be tailored to a buyer’s specific needs, with negotiated terms for capacity, delivery periods, compensation, and performance obligations.

The appropriate route depends on existing customer relationships, market access, and the responsibilities each partner assumes. Developers and EPCs working on commercial storage projects may find opportunities through utility procurement or capacity contracts, while residential installers with established battery customer bases may be well positioned to incorporate VPP enrollment into utility programs. Wholesale markets can expand revenue opportunities through aggregation partners, and bilateral contracts can create opportunities to develop resources around specific buyer needs.

#5902. BMW In South Carolina: A Free Trade Success Story

I recently attended BMW’s “Beyond The Machine” event in South Carolina. The largest facility of the German company globally is Plant Spartanburg in Greer, South Carolina. As part of the event, I saw the opening of BMW’s new battery plant in nearby Woodruff. We stayed in the nearby city of Greenville, which was transformed by BMW and the businesses that followed.

Beyond the vehicles and manufacturing, which I plan to cover in more detail in future articles, one of the most striking aspects was the human impact. From the “Woodruffian” Mayor fighting back tears at the opening of the battery plant to the plant employees to the people I met exploring the town, they clearly appreciate what BMW has done for the community. Throughout the presentations and discussions, there was support for free trade along with the local benefits. People in the area pride themselves in their resilience. They have reason to be proud. And they were optimistic about the growth of the increasingly clean manufacturing and electrified products from this global company.

The area around Greenville, South Carolina the rose to prominence in the 1870s on the textile industry. Cotton from throughout the South that had previously been shipped to other areas for processing was processed in Greenville and Spartanburg counties. However, as global competition increased in the 20th century, the mills became less competitive. The last textile mill closed in Greenville in 1977, although a few textile operations continued in the region into the 1980’s. As that industry left, the local economy declined. If the story ended there, many might use it as an argument against free trade.

When BMW came to South Carolina in 1992, they saw a lot of potential. Michelin had already started making tires there in 1978. Local leaders were enthusiastic to work with BMW. The people were also enthusiastic to receive training for the new jobs. The deep-water port of Charleston on the other side of the state facilitated shipping, while nearby rail links connected to much of the US.

Since 1992, BMW has invested over $15.8 Billion. The new Woodruff battery plant is their most recent major investment. In the process, an area that could have been considered a casualty of global trade became one of its greatest beneficiaries.

The first model, an E36 3 series, rolled off the line in 1994. BMW also produced the Z3 in the plant, including the first “German” car driven by James Bond on camera. However, the big breakthrough came with the introduction of the X5 in 1999. At the time, relatively few SUVs were being sold in Europe. BMW Spartanburg as the center of SUV production was intending to largely for the US. However, global markets shifted.

Now, Spartanburg is the “Home of X.” It currently produces the X3, X5, X6, X7 and XM, including electrified versions. It is the sole manufacturing plant for a number of those SUV models globally. A little over half of their production is exported, which also makes it consistently the top US overseas automotive exporter. Export volume topped $9 Billion in 2025 over roughly 200,000 X vehicles.

BMW designed those vehicles to be able to use ICE, PHEV and BEV powertrains. While some may want them to make just pure BEV, the approach gives BMW the ability to shift rapidly with the market. The different models also give the plant a level of market resilience. However, to fuel that resilience they need a very flexible supply chain that can respond quickly. Suppliers being close to the plant creates greater resilience.

BMW is designing its vehicles to be globally competitive. While 400+ miles of EPA range and charging speeds approaching 500 kW may be at the top of the US market, they are also competitive in global markets. While BMW developed the cells and packs, they use multiple suppliers to manufacture the cylindrical cells to their specifications. Packs at the plant currently use AESC cells, but they will shift to LG in the future and use other suppliers in other markets.

BMW also worked with the state of South Carolina to develop people. Nearby Clemson University provides a source of engineering talent, and BMW partners with the university on the Clemson University International Center for Automotive Research, where both students and BMW benefit from the development of new innovations. They are also developing potential future suppliers with the BMW Venture funds. One such company will be supplying flax-based composites to future M models.

A heavy emphasis is placed on technical training at the factory. In addition, they collaborated with community colleges to provide technical training. But the development programs start much earlier, with a heavy emphasis in middle school and sponsoring the First Robotics Competition for elementary school students.

BMW also worked with the South Carolina Department of Transportation to build infrastructure to support the plant and its suppliers. Moving forward, that infrastructure will increasingly include electrification and EV charging. BMW also worked with the SC Ports Authority to develop an “inland port,” which is essentially a direct rail link to the Port of Charleston. By moving freight by rail, emissions are greatly reduced. In addition, a lot of emphasis is going into transferring freight quickly, as every minute a truck sits idling is also wasted emissions.

While BMW employs 11,000 workers, over four times as many jobs are supported by the plant, according to a Clemson economist. That multiplier is much higher than most companies due to how BMW operates. Part of the impact on the local economy beyond employee count comes from the strategy behind the manufacturing. Overall, more than 70% of the value of the vehicles comes from outside suppliers.

Plant Spartanburg receives 3.3 million parts per day delivered by 1200 truckloads and 50 sea containers, 6 days per week. All those parts then go into over 1500 vehicles produced per day. The plant is laid out a bit different than some other manufacturing plants to facilitate the supply chain. Multiple fingers go up off the main line, along which parts are delivered to be assembled nearby. There isn’t much in the way of warehousing. Trucks come to the plant each day and the parts can end up on vehicles driving off the line the same day.

In the initial stages of manufacturing, more of the supply base came from overseas. However, as the plant established itself, more suppliers localized. That growing supply chain then attracted additional companies to the region, like Mercedes, Volvo and Scout. Those companies then create greater scale for the suppliers, who can reduce costs and expand operations.

Overall, for heavy, bulky components, localization will tend to  happen naturally. For a company that relies on suppliers to consistently and almost constantly deliver parts, that localization tends to increase. The more localized the supply chain, the more capable it becomes to respond to market dynamics. Once scale is attained for heavy, bulky components like batteries, that production will naturally become more localized.

BMW has a growing supply chain footprint across the Americas and especially across the Southeastern US. There will be over 850 Tier 1 supplier locations in the Americas by 2027, with multiple times as many Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers providing materials, parts and components. BMW focuses on value creation, competitiveness and collaboration in their supply chain. In addition, while they are bringing economic benefit, they are also focused on decarbonization.

Of course, all this economic activity has brought “green” in terms of money to the local communities. As I was stranded in Greenville due to flight interruptions, I had time to walk around the town and speak to some of the locals. Streets that were once desolate and unsafe are now vibrant with shops and restaurants. An area that people once wanted to leave is now growing rapidly and attracting people from around the world. I started conversations  with several friendly people in town about trade. I heard stories of transformation from past struggles to current successes.

Even though it was clear that they were benefitting from the economic impact, I was still surprised how strongly and openly they supported free trade and realized the benefits. A local guide pointed out major buildings and businesses that were new. Downtown is now pleasant and walkable. Part of that is due to the restoration of a river that runs through the city, surrounded by shops and artwork, where I saw some locals fishing in the clear water. This gets to the other kind of “green.”

While there are a lot of trucks transporting materials and components, shortened travel distances and efforts to streamline intermodal transfers reduce emissions. The proximity enables packaging to typically be sent back to the supplier to be reused. BMW is also putting a lot of effort into reduced CO2 emissions steel produced locally, which I plan to follow up on. Over half the steel in their vehicles is reduced CO2. Solar panels and landfill gas provide power for their facilities.

As the company relies heavily on its supply chain, the supply chain is a major focus of its decarbonization strategy. While EVs reduce emissions overall, the proportion of emissions from the manufacturing phase tends to be higher than for ICE vehicles, adding emphasis to decarbonization of manufacturing. At the same time, they strive to create regional strength, resilience and value creation while reducing emissions. Close collaboration is the key to meeting these goals. BMW plans to have net zero CO2 emissions throughout their supply chain by 2050 at the latest.

The new Woodruff plant will produce their sixth-generation battery. BMW develops the batteries first and then outsources the cell manufacturing, but they will now be manufacturing the packs locally. Initially, this will be for the iX5, to be delivered domestically and to global markets. However, additional EV models may benefit in the future.

Overall, politicians in the 1970’s could have tried to block global competition to protect the textile mills. That might have extended the life of those plants for a few years. However, in the process, those plants would become less globally competitive and less globally relevant. Protectionism would have only extended a slow, painful decline.

However, by keeping the market open and staying open to new business opportunities, Greenville, Greer, Spartanburg and Woodruff created the circumstances for their current success. People were open to new companies from other countries. They were open to industries that were new to the area. And they were open to investing in retraining to better take advantage of the new opportunities.

In the initial site selection, South Carolina could have imposed strict regulations and tried to force localization before the factory was built, production was ramped up and scale was attained to support that localization. If that happened, the plant would have undoubtedly been built elsewhere, and the economic benefits would have gone to someone else. By taking a more collaborative approach, rather than being confrontational, both BMW and local suppliers benefitted greatly. Building on that approach, more companies came to the region, building scale and amplifying the benefits.

BMW is not being hypocritical in their support of free trade. They are not promoting free trade just when it benefits them and then putting up barriers for other competitors. They are not shying away from global competition. They are seeking to make globally competitive vehicles for global markets. While global competition is fierce, by facing that competition, rather than hiding from it, their products become more competitive.

In the process, one of the brightest spots in US automotive trade globally, as well as one of the leaders in electrified product and supply chain decarbonization, comes from a German company.

Change and globalization can be scary things. Especially in the short term. The transition to EVs can also be scary, especially for those tied to the past. However, hiding behind political and trade walls rarely works out in the long run. A willingness to be open and collaborative with people who might come from within different lines on a map or sound different than you can have long term benefits. And the courage to engage with the technology transition and compete globally can put you ahead of many established competitors. That cannot only transform how we power our vehicles, but also transform the lives of people in communities for generations to come. As Woodruff Mayor Kenneth Gist put it at the battery plant opening:

BMW and the City of Woodruff wanted the same thing. Success in business, success in our community, and success in building the next generation of BMW electric vehicles to be the best in the world…

…When I think about this plant, I think about our children, our grandchildren. Educational partnership that will open the doors for them, the career that will be waiting for them, and a Woodruff they would be proud to call home for decades to come. BMW, you have been a true partner to me personally, to every person in this community.

#5880. When Less Is More: Rewilding as a Strategy for Nature Recovery

Can we bring nature back from the brink of collapse? That may sound melodramatic, but the evidence is sobering: the Planetary Health Check 2026 shows that we have crossed 7 of the 9 science-based planetary boundaries;[1] the Living Planet Report 2026 finds that wildlife populations have declined 73% on average since 1970;[2] and around 1 million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, with 75% of the land surface having been significantly altered by human activity, according to the IPBES, the leading intergovernmental body for assessing the state of biodiversity.[3]

In fact, with vertebrate extinction rates up to 100 times the natural background rate, some scientists believe we are at the onset of a sixth mass extinction.[4] But perhaps we should not be surprised at this precipitous decline in the health of our living planet. After all, around $7.3 trillion is spent every year on activities that harm nature ($4.9 trillion by companies and $2.4 trillion in government subsidies), while only $220 billion goes towards protecting or restoring ecosystems (2023 figures).[5] In other words, for every $1 we spend on taking care of nature, $33 is spent on activities that degrade the environment.

The drivers of nature loss are many, including climate change, overexploitation of resources, pollution and invasive alien species, but the largest is land-use change, primarily for agriculture.[6] Research by UNEP and Chatham House goes so far as to identify agriculture as a threat to 86% of species at risk of extinction.[7] So, is regenerative agriculture the answer? That is certainly one approach that would help, as I explored in a previous CleanTechnica article. But there is another, more direct—and some would say more radical—solution that is gaining popularity: rewilding.

As with regenerative agriculture, rewilding is more a collection of ideas and practices than a well-defined science. Nevertheless, experts convened by the IUCN define rewilding as the process of rebuilding a natural ecosystem, following major human disturbance, by restoring natural processes and food webs.[8] The Global Rewilding Alliance puts it even more simply, as “helping nature heal”.[9] In practice, this can mean anything from leaving areas of nature undisturbed to actively introducing keystone species such as beavers, bison and wild boar.

Perhaps the most cited rewilding experiment is the reintroduction of wolves into Yellowstone National Park in 1995–96, after they had been eradicated there by 1926. This resulted in a so-called trophic cascade, whereby elk numbers fell, scavengers such as eagles, ravens and bears gained a year-round food supply, and willow and aspen began to recover in places.[10] The strength of that recovery, and the extent to which the wolves were the primary driver, has been contested,[11] but few dispute the overall positive effect on biodiversity and the ecosystem.

The Yellowstone case is rewilding on a large scale, but it is still dwarfed by the rewilding work of Tompkins Conservation, created by the late Doug Tompkins, co-founder of The North Face and Esprit, and his wife Kristine McDivitt Tompkins, former CEO of Patagonia. Working primarily in Chile and Argentina, they have helped create or expand 16 parks protecting over 45 million acres of land and sea, and reintroduced 14 species, including around 50 wild jaguars.[12] Besides the ecological benefits, ecotourism is boosting local economies.[13] In Chilean Patagonia, every dollar the state invested in the national parks along Chile’s Route of Parks in 2024 returned US$6 to local communities,[14] while visitors to Argentina’s Iberá Park, partly degraded by cattle ranching in the past, have doubled in a decade to 60,000 a year.[15]

At a more local scale, the Knepp Estate in the UK has become another well documented case of rewilding. In her book Wilding: The Return of Nature to a British Farm, Isabella Tree tells the story of how she and her husband, Charlie Burrell, converted their 1,400-hectare farm from intensive dairy and crop agriculture, which was losing money, into a thriving “wild farm,” with free-roaming herbivores such as cattle, ponies, pigs, and deer.[16] A 20-year review shows an incredible rejuvenation of biodiversity, including a 916% increase in breeding bird abundance in the southern block, a 132% rise in bird species richness, a near-ninefold increase in dragonflies and damselflies, and a doubling of butterfly species in some areas.[17] There are climate benefits too, with 5.2 tCO2e/ha/yr sequestration, comparable to new native woodland.[18]

Critics of rewilding often frame it as a trade-off: more nature means less food; more wildlife means fewer jobs; more biodiversity means lower economic growth. These arguments can (and should) be tested, for in many (but not all) cases they will prove to be a false choice. My view is that it should be about balance. I saw this pragmatic approach at a rewilding site in Norfolk, England, called Wild Ken Hill, where they have designated separate areas for regenerative farming, coastal conservation, and rewilding—including 30 or so wild Exmoor ponies, 6 very inquisitive and vocal Tamworth pigs, and a small herd of Red Poll cattle.

My conclusion on rewilding is that it can certainly help us to reach the global goal of reversing biodiversity loss and becoming nature positive. It is by no means the only strategy, but it is one of the most direct and impactful. As I noted in the chapter on nature restoration in my book Thriving: “We know that nature is on the ropes, bruised and battered by dumb agriculture and dumber economics. But another thing we know is that, given half a chance, nature bounces back. Ecosystems are resilient and designed to flourish. The most powerful and effective action we can take is to step back and give nature space and time to recover.”[19]

My full TFIE Strategy Briefing traces China’s heavy-truck sales from 2015 through 2026, examines real freight costs and charging choices, and assesses the consequences for manufacturers, logistics companies and fuel demand.

The operating economics explain much of the appeal. Trucks earn money by carrying cargo, so annual kilometres, payload, electricity prices, charging stops and vehicle availability matter as much as the price on the invoice. High-mileage routes make electricity and maintenance savings more valuable. A costly grid connection or charging queue can eat into those savings. Fleet operators need to evaluate the truck and its route together.

Recent ICCT case studies document how those economics play out. A Guangdong warehouse fleet of 28 electric trucks had estimated five-year ownership costs 16% below comparable diesel trucks. In Yangtze River Delta express delivery, 50 electric tractor-trailers working routes of 300–500 km achieved a narrower 3% advantage over six years without purchase incentives. Another operation covering 900 km a day with strategically placed high-power charging showed estimated ownership costs 19% below diesel. The differences reflect route, utilization and charging arrangements.

Those three operations offer a more useful procurement lesson than a single quoted range figure. The operator starts with cargo, schedules and energy access, then selects equipment. The narrow 3% margin in express delivery also shows why financing and charging costs can determine whether a viable electric route makes money.

Battery swapping has carved out a sizable part of this market. In September, 20.09% of newly registered battery-electric heavy trucks were classified as swap-capable. That compares with 22.92% across January–September 2026 and 30.39% for all of 2025. The number of swap-classified trucks continued to rise, while conventional charging gained a larger share of the expanding electric market. China’s registration classification also includes some trucks with leased batteries that are recharged rather than exchanged.

Swapping fits dense, repeatable freight operations such as steelworks, ports and bulk-material haulage. Fleet operators and station owners can standardize vehicles and use stations often enough to pay for batteries held in inventory. Conventional charging serves a wider range of routes and can fit scheduled stops, overnight parking and depot operations. Both approaches depend on predictable truck traffic and reliable electricity connections.

China is building infrastructure around those freight patterns. Yunnan commissioned 3,350 km of heavy-truck charging corridors in 2025, with 113 stations and 869 connectors. Fujian opened a 649 km coastal corridor in October 2026, including 15 charging stations and eight swapping stations. The IEA’s 2026 charging review describes the concentration of heavy-vehicle charging around freight corridors and industrial centres. Long-haul coverage still has gaps, but the network is expanding where trucks already have work to do.

Hydrogen has found little traction alongside this expansion. China registered 689 fuel-cell heavy trucks in the first nine months of 2026, compared with more than 223,000 battery-electric trucks. LNG has a much larger installed customer base, although its advantage over diesel has narrowed as gas prices change. Battery trucks offer a different cost structure built around drivetrain efficiency and lower maintenance needs. That makes the competition increasingly difficult for equipment and fuel suppliers dependent on combustion engines.

Sales changes take years to work through the operating fleet. Kpler projects that new-energy trucks, predominantly battery electric, will account for about 8% of Chinese heavy trucks in service at the end of 2026. It also estimates they will displace about 230,000 barrels of diesel per day compared with moving the same freight using diesel trucks. Freight vehicles with high daily utilization can have a large effect on fuel demand before they become a majority of vehicles on the road.

China’s June 2026 heavy-truck programme joins purchasing incentives, grid access, charging corridors, financing and logistics planning. Its 2030 targets include a 40% share of new-energy heavy-truck sales, a 20% share of the operating fleet and 30,000 km of zero-carbon freight corridors. Monthly new sales have already surpassed the sales-share target. Building and replacing enough vehicles to reach the fleet target will take longer.

Other countries have different electricity tariffs, road networks and freight routes, but the procurement method travels. Start with the actual jobs trucks perform. Identify routes with strong electric economics, plan the charging and grid connections those routes require, and finance vehicles and infrastructure as one programme. Other drivetrains can compete where their operating costs or performance are better. The evidence from China’s largest commercial freight applications increasingly favours batteries.

Notably, each module retains its own independent maximum power point tracker (MPPT), which preserves the module-level performance benefits associated with the traditional one-to-one optimizer configurations.

For developers, EPCs and installers managing large-scale rooftop and distributed generation projects, the value here lies in fewer components to purchase, wire and install, with no trade-off in module-level optimization.

Simplifying installation without sacrificing performance

SolarEdge’s optimization tech addresses the challenge of conventional string inverter systems, which is that an underperforming module can affect the output of an entire string. But by allowing each module to operate independently, the system limits the impact of shading, degradation or performance differences between panels.

The Duo brings demonstrates that capability in a two-module configuration. By reducing the number of optimizers by 50% compared with one-to-one designs, SolarEdge is targeting an opportunity to streamline balance-of-system requirements and reduce the installation labor.

Rather than relying exclusively on detection at the inverter, the system distributes monitoring throughout the array, allowing potential arc faults to be identified closer to their source.

SolarEdge says the technology provides coverage across strings with up to 3,280 feet of total cable length between the inverter and optimizer. It can also help pinpoint problems to the optimizer area, potentially making troubleshooting and maintenance more efficient.

Manufactured at SolarEdge’s Florida facility, the Duo is designed to meet U.S. Domestic Content and Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) requirements, according to the company. #5850. The Harm From Plastic Is Happening In Plain Sight

It is known. Plastic is a major cause of the climate crisis, yet governments continue to ignore it.

The Environmental Justice Foundation (EJF) has issued a new briefing published to coincide with the UN Climate Change Conference, COP31, in Antalya, Türkiye. The briefing, “Reducing plastic production in a post-1.5°C world: A test of resolve for the international community,” calls on governments to reduce global plastic production.

Plastic already emits more than two billion tons of CO₂e a year, about twice as much as aviation, and under current policies this would more than double as the fossil fuel industry turns to plastic as its hedge against electrification.

“There’s only so much carbon the world can still emit if we want to avoid blasting past the Paris Agreement targets. Plastic’s harm to our climate is happening in plain sight, and governments are choosing to look away. Every new plastics plant locks in decades of emissions the planet simply cannot afford. As leaders gather at COP31, they must recognise plastic as a major cause of carbon pollution that is rapidly heating our world.” — Steve Trent, CEO, Environmental Justice Foundation

Over 90% of plastic emissions come before a plastic product is even used, during extraction, conversion and production. If current policies remain unchanged, global plastic production is projected to almost triple by 2060, and annual emissions from fossil-based plastics to more than double, to 4.3 billion tons of CO2e.

On this trajectory, plastics would emit 67 billion tons of CO2e between 2026 and 2050. That is just over half of the remaining carbon budget for a 50% chance of meeting the 1.5°C target, and 27% of the budget for a very good chance of limiting the overshoot to 1.7°C. None of this is hidden; emissions are quantified, the projections are published and the industry’s expansion plans are public, yet plastic remains on the margins of climate negotiations.

EJF is calling on governments at COP31 to recognize plastic for what it is: a major driver of climate change that must be tackled at its source, by cutting production.

The petrochemical industry’s bet on plastics

As the world electrifies transport and power, the fossil fuel industry is using plastic as its hedge against electrification. With oil demand for transport and energy expected to decline, petrochemical companies are pivoting to plastics to keep oil and gas flowing. The plastics sector could account for 20% of global oil consumption by 2050, up from around 12% today. The top fifteen petrochemical companies received an estimated US$590.8 billion in financing between 2019 and 2025, and the sector was propped up by state subsidies estimated at over $80 billion in 2024 alone, almost half of which came from Saudi Arabia.

A test of resolve for the plastics treaty

A total of 145 countries from every continent have expressed support for provisions to reduce plastic production during the global plastics treaty negotiations. Rwanda and Peru have proposed a global target to cut plastic production by 40% by 2040 against a 2025 baseline. Modelling cited in the briefing shows that a 40% cut in high-ambition countries, joined by China and emerging producers, could reduce global emissions from plastics by as much as 21% compared with business as usual.

But negotiations have reached a deadlock, driven by a small group of oil- and plastic-producing states, including Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iran and China, with the United States recently aligning with their position. At least 234 fossil fuel and chemical industry lobbyists attended the latest round of talks in Geneva — more than the combined delegations of the EU and its 27 Member States.

Final Thoughts about Plastic and our Leaders

The truth about plastics is perverse. Plastics are widely used around the world due to ease of manufacturing, low cost, stable chemical properties, and good water resistance. They have infiltrated every part of the environment and human life cycle. More than 16,000 chemicals are used in manufacturing plastics, and at least 4,200 of these have been identified as hazardous to human health and the environment. Thousands more chemicals used in plastic production have never been tested. Many of these chemicals migrate out of plastic packaging into our foods, drinks, household products, and bodies.

Researchers have come up with many possible solutions to plastic pollution, as well as ways to mitigate microplastics in the environment. But will governments have the wherewithal to stand up to the powerful fossil fuel lobby that is behind plastic propaganda?

#5849. The grid will fall short of AI’s power needs. These 6 resources can close the gap.

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

No single option is fast, firm and large enough on its own, writes Simon Irish, CEO of Terrestrial Energy.

Simon Irish is CEO of Terrestrial Energy, a developer of small modular nuclear reactors.

Most planning for artificial intelligence’s electricity demand starts with a forecast and asks whether it is right.

I would start somewhere else: Assume the grid will fall short, then ask how to close the gap — and how fast.

The evidence supports that starting point. McKinsey & Company estimates the U.S. will need 30 GW to 55 GW of additional capacity by 2030 as data center demand grows and aging plants retire. PJM Interconnection, the largest U.S. grid operator, has already seen the impacts of this trend. Its latest capacity auction fell about 6.6 GW short of its reliability requirement and cleared at the price cap.

Forecasts can be wrong, and requested load can be speculative or duplicative. But the PJM result is a market outcome, not a forecast. The useful question is which tools can close the gap, by when and at what cost.

The first four options below address serving data centers through the grid. The final two consider generation at or near the data center, with an optional grid connection. These categories overlap; on-site generation is a delivery strategy, not a generation technology, and can use multiple technologies.

Flexible demand. Duke University’s Nicholas Institute estimates the grid could absorb 76 GW of new load if that load accepted curtailment averaging 0.25% of annual consumption, in events of about 1.7 hours. It is the fastest option because it needs no new generation. It makes better use of capacity already on the grid.

But that capacity is finite, and beyond it, further load growth requires new supply. There is also a practical hurdle; to date, many data centers have shown little willingness to curtail AI workloads, because the value of computing far exceeds the cost of power.

Existing nuclear plants. Increasing output at operating plants through uprates and restarting closed plants can add reliable electricity supply, while extending operating licenses keeps existing plants running. A U.S. Department of Energy initiative targets 5 GW of added capacity at existing nuclear plants by 2029, and DOE has closed a $1.9 billion loan to restart Iowa’s Duane Arnold plant, returning 615 MW to service.

The limit is opportunity: Few operating plants remain candidates for significant uprates, and restarts are underway at nearly every plant where one is practical.

Natural gas. Gas offers firm power and a familiar path, but gas turbine supply has become the constraint. GE Vernova’s gas turbine backlog and slot reservations reached 116 GW at midyear 2026, and the company has said a heavy-duty turbine ordered today will not arrive until 2031. Buyers also take on fuel-price exposure and decades of emissions.

Wind, solar and storage. These resources can be deployed quickly and at scale. Their limit is that data centers require dependable power around the clock, so matching that requirement takes substantial overbuilding, energy storage and transmission infrastructure to deliver the same capacity as a firm resource.

On-site generation. Building a power plant at a data center can bring electricity online sooner by avoiding some grid upgrades and connection delays, where permitted.

However, it does not speed up equipment deliveries. An on-site gas turbine plant faces the same turbine shortages as one supplying the grid. Reliable operation also requires sufficient on-site backup capability or access to grid power during outages and maintenance. If a project relies on the grid, developers need clear terms defining that access and its cost.

Advanced nuclear energy. Advanced reactors offer firm, clean power and can be built as a fleet. They can serve data centers directly, with an optional grid connection. They will not close the gap this decade, and their real test is cost at fleet scale.

No single option is fast, firm and large enough on its own. Three principles follow.

Flexible demand, uprates at existing nuclear plants, and new wind, solar and storage can help meet near-term electricity needs.

Natural gas and on-site generation can help fill the gap while longer-term resources are developed, but only if equipment deliveries, permits and grid interconnection align with the data center’s schedule.

Advanced nuclear energy can contribute in the 2030s and beyond.

Utilities must plan now for both natural gas and advanced nuclear energy. DOE’s long-term modeling indicates 550 to 770 GW of additional firm, clean generating capacity could be needed by 2050, with advanced nuclear energy providing about 200 GW.

Infrastructure built to meet an immediate shortfall should also have a useful role later. A gas plant could provide firming capacity or operate during periods of peak demand as longer-term supply becomes available.

Make flexibility the price of speed. Utilities can offer earlier interconnection to customers accepting curtailment under defined, metered and enforceable terms. For data center operators, that is a choice between some power sooner and firm power later. Customers that can curtail load may get power sooner; those that cannot must wait for firm supply. This converts flexibility from an assumption into a contract and rewards developers whose flexibility is real.

Secure the long-lead items for firm, clean power now. Buyers who reserved gas turbine production slots early secured earlier deliveries; those ordering now may have to wait until 2031.

Firm, clean power has its own long-lead items, including fuel supply and equipment manufacturing capacity. Utilities and data center operators can sign reservation or offtake agreements that give suppliers the demand signal to build capacity before it is needed.

These principles also address the forecast debate. Some investments — flexibility, uprates and transmission — remain valuable across demand scenarios. Others are options worth buying, such as reservations for firm supply in the 2030s and beyond.

And some are bets to avoid: irreversible commitments sized to a demand number that may not arrive. If forecasts prove too high, the cost is the price paid to preserve an option. If they prove right and no one bought the option, the cost is the kind of shortfall PJM has already seen.

Planning for a shortfall is not pessimism. Of all the inputs AI needs, reliable power may prove the scarcest. The race to scale AI is a race to deliver power. Utilities, regulators and developers need tools that arrive on time, hold their value if forecasts change, and keep electricity reliable and affordable for existing customers.

“U.S. wind turbine pricing is experiencing unprecedented uncertainty as conflicting market and regulatory forces interact,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Domestic manufacturing overcapacity relative to permitted project volumes, particularly after 2028, would normally place downward pressure on prices. Despite this, onshore wind costs are expected to continue rising due to tariff exposure on raw material inputs and subcomponents.”

Several presidential candidates have made reaching one million vehicles (or even more) one of the hallmarks of their campaigns¹. While this convergence is excellent news, the battle of figures says nothing about how to achieve this goal.

The current system cannot simply be scaled up twentyfold. Its cost per vehicle, intermittent financing, imperfect social targeting, lack of a guaranteed solution after three years, and the resale risks borne by dealerships are all structural limitations. The 2026 edition also revealed its dependence on Renault and Stellantis, which can at any time restrict volumes to favor more profitable traditional sales.

The proposed law changes its approach. Sponsored by MP Christine Arrighi, it draws inspiration from the proposals of T&E France and Bernard Julien, stemming from their study on the future of social leasing, published in June 2026.

In this new model, the government would directly purchase the vehicles at an average net cost of €15,000. These vehicles would be kept for twelve years in a public fleet and leased successively to one or more households for an average monthly rent of €150 before being resold. The day-to-day management of the fleet would be delegated to groups of public or private operators within the local areas.

“This will also allow us to direct our production towards the vehicles that households truly need: affordable, fuel-efficient, repairable electric cars, produced in France and Europe,” concludes Léo Larivière, automotive transition expert at T&E France. “This proposal must now open a substantive discussion among all stakeholders. #5840. Europe’s Inland Barges Need a Cargo Plan Before an Electric Fleet Plan

Coal throughput at Rotterdam rose 15.3% in the first nine months of 2026. Crude oil and petroleum-product volumes also increased. An inland vessel operator looking only at this year’s cargo market could see decades of work ahead. But an engine, battery conversion or replacement vessel bought now may still be operating in 2050, when the mix of cargo travelling through European ports and rivers will be different.

Rotterdam’s latest throughput release explains the coal rebound through recovering coking-coal movements, expensive natural gas and power-market conditions. Meanwhile, Eurostat reports that EU inland-waterway freight fell 3% in 2025 to 118 billion tonne-kilometres. Coal and crude-petroleum freight rose that year, while agricultural freight contracted. These changes are reshaping the services that ports, vessel owners and customers need to buy.

The Central Commission for the Navigation of the Rhine counted 6,224 dry-cargo vessels in Rhine countries in 2025, down from 6,410 in 2023. Smaller vessels account for much of the decline. Tankers moved the other way, rising from 1,400 in 2022 to 1,477 in 2025. The numbers describe a fleet already adapting to changing cargo and operating economics.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing on the inland barges Europe will need in 2050 uses a cargo-first model to work from the freight task to the future electric fleet. The underlying 98-page Inland Freight Electrification report is available to read.

A vessel owner has two connected capital decisions. The first is whether the freight service will still have customers through the useful life of the asset. The second is which propulsion and charging system can perform that service economically. Starting with equipment and then finding a market for it risks spending scarce investment capital on the wrong vessels.

I modelled a large European industrial port corridor from a 2025-style baseline to 2050. The central scenario assumes bulk cargo falls from 57.8 million tonnes to 34.4 million tonnes, a decline of about 40%. Wet bulk accounts for much of the loss, falling from 34.5 million to 15.0 million tonnes. Dry bulk declines from 23.3 million to 19.4 million tonnes. The model is a transparent scenario for investment decisions across this type of corridor, rather than a forecast of Rotterdam’s exact future cargo volumes.

The largest change is fossil-linked cargo. Fossil fuels and refinery streams fall from 19.0 million tonnes to 1.5 million tonnes, while coal and other solid fossil fuels fall from 2.5 million tonnes to zero. Combined, those reductions account for about 20 million tonnes of the scenario’s 23.4-million-tonne net decline. Any operator whose principal customers depend on moving coal or petroleum faces a different replacement decision from one carrying grain, construction minerals, chemicals or scrap.

Steel illustrates what cargo substitution means. The model reduces imported iron ore and other virgin iron units from 4.5 million tonnes to 3.0 million tonnes, while scrap and circular feedstocks rise from 1.8 million to 3.5 million tonnes. The industrial function continues as the materials moving to and from factories change. Existing barges and terminals may find new work, although parcel sizes, loading systems and routes will also change.

Biofuel feedstocks create a second trap. In the central scenario those movements peak near 5 million tonnes in 2030 before falling to 1.5 million tonnes in 2050. A temporary freight boom can support profitable operations without supporting a new vessel or terminal investment with a thirty-year life. The durability of the contract matters as much as the volume carried this year.

Cargo tonnage alone cannot size an electric fleet. The model translates commodities into powered vessel-equivalents using parcel sizes, routes, loaded calls and productivity. It starts with around 1,150 powered vessel-equivalents and ends with 664, a reduction of 42%. Dry cargo falls 17% in tonnes but 43% in powered vessel-equivalents because the modelled freight service changes. The starting 1,150 powered equivalents represent about 1,234 physical hulls after counting unpowered barges moved by pushers. Battery conversion decisions concern powered units; terminal and cargo-handling decisions also concern the additional hulls.

That distinction is particularly important for pushed formations. A pusher provides propulsion for several cargo barges. Converting one powered unit does not necessarily mean replacing every physical hull. Counting all hulls as propulsion conversions would inflate the equipment and financing requirement.

Low Rhine water levels bring a different constraint into the same calculation. A barge can remain on the register yet carry less freight per journey when draught is restricted. Cargo owners may have to buy more voyages, use different hulls or shift some freight to rail and road. Route depth, seasonal load factors and terminal access belong in the service model alongside cargo demand.

Ports should begin with a living cargo and route register showing which freight is declining, which remains, and which changes its material inputs. Vessel owners can connect that register to remaining hull life, annual utilization, cargo contracts and the cost of electric conversion. Utilities and terminal operators can then size grid connections, charging facilities and battery-container pools against vessels that will run on bankable routes. Infrastructure developed around dependable freight customers has a stronger prospect of earning a return.

Europe has good reasons to electrify established, high-utilization inland freight now. Battery-powered vessels are already serving container and bulk routes. The commercial opportunity is to invest in routes with surviving customers and electrify the services they will continue to buy. Expanding an electric fleet before measuring that freight task puts the order of decisions backward.

TFIE Strategy also undertakes scoped corridor and fleet assessments for ports, operators and cargo owners.

#5839. Grid operators need data center ride-through rules that protect reliability

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The first facilities built to Texas’ new standard are about to come online, proving that firm, predictable regulation can protect reliability without slowing development, says ON.energy’s Alan Cooper.

Alan Cooper is CEO of ON.energy, a developer of power infrastructure.

On July 22, a mechanical failure forced a 230-kV transmission line in Northern Virginia offline. Within milliseconds, grid protection systems cleared the fault, as they are designed to do. The event would not have been a particularly noteworthy story, had it stopped there. It’s what happened next, though, that pointed to a growing challenge for grid reliability.

In response to the fault, data centers that had been drawing nearly 4 GW of energy, close to 4% of PJM’s load, collectively disconnected and switched to their on-site backup generation. The move sent voltage and frequency spiking across the system, a technically wonky but threatening result that caused power flickers as far away as Chicago. Thankfully, the prevailing grid conditions were strong enough to absorb the shock of the fault, so the grid held. But if the same event had hit Texas or even Northern Virginia at a time of higher demand, it could have caused a widespread outage. In West Texas, the Electric Reliability Counil of Texas estimates that a loss of as little as 3.2 GW could threaten grid reliability.

The power sector is not unfamiliar with the need for things hooked up to the electricity grid to “ride through” disturbance events. In 2023, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission told the North American Electricity Reliability Corp. to develop requirements that inverter-based supply resources like wind and solar facilities must follow to ensure they “ride through,” i.e., stay online, during certain disturbances. And this time around, NERC, FERC and several grid regions have been quick to identify a similar need for large loads to adhere to analogous standards.

In an Oct. 14 presentation to ERCOT, Dominion Virginia confirmed that the disturbance fell within the ride-through parameters that have now been finalized or proposed by NERC and two grid operators (ERCOT and PJM Interconnection). They concluded that if those requirements had been in place, the event would have been avoided.

Clear ride-through standards protect the grid, and they protect data centers’ ability to keep connecting to it. The open question for grid operators and regulators isn’t whether to create them, but rather which facilities the rules should cover. Some proposals would exempt projects that aren’t likely to draw grid power for years. Texas took a firmer line and in doing so, gave other operators and regulators an effective blueprint to follow.

The Public Utility Commission of Texas approved ERCOT’s ride through rule and it took effect in August. ERCOT made three choices that are key to making it work.

It drew the applicability line at energization. Facilities that energize roughly 18 months or more after ERCOT’s first market notice must comply with the rule. That exempts projects that are nearly finished, with equipment already ordered or even installed, and it applies to projects that exist only on paper, which can still change their electrical design for a small share of what a retrofit would cost later.

It measured the risk in exempt capacity within a cluster. ERCOT studies ride-through risk in its quarterly stability assessments. Those studies identify the clusters of large load that could trip together and the rule limits the amount of exempted load — i.e., the amount that is not equipped to ride through disturbances within each cluster — to 3,200 MW per single fault. Its latest results flag North Texas and Central Texas as active risk areas, with far West Texas being monitored.

It made the risk transparent and predictable. ERCOT watches each load cluster’s combined consumption in real time. When a cluster’s actual demand reaches about 2,500 MW, a mitigation plan kicks in: each utility and scheduling entity gets a “not to exceed” limit based on its share of recent peak use, and ERCOT keeps the authority to curtail non-compliant load. Because ERCOT publishes the study results and updates them over time, developers can see how much headroom is left long before they hit the limit. This transparency gives them time to decide whether to install ride-through capability, change their plans, or accept the risk of curtailment.

The result is that existing data centers keep running, and projects under construction generally avoid a forced redesign or need to buy different equipment. Other grid users stay protected. The first facilities built to the new standard will connect in the coming months.

In general, regulators and regional grid operators across the country are right to be careful about borrowing from ERCOT. Its isolated grid and energy-only market make much of its experience hard to apply elsewhere.

This isn’t one of those cases. A data center’s response to a voltage dip is the same in Virginia, Ohio or Texas, and so is the physics of thousands of megawatts disconnecting at once. NERC, PJM and other grid operators should adopt the core tenants of the Texas approach:

A single large-scale event would change the conversation overnight. Instead of debating exemption criteria, policymakers would be questioning if data centers can remain connected. Clear standards now are the best way to ensure the industry has a path forward.

The impact of a major event wouldn’t stop at the fence line: a multistate outage would affect the communities where data centers are concentrated and working to become good grid citizens. Proposed exceptions to ride-through requirements leave the door open for exactly the kind of event that consumers and policymakers are concerned about.

None of this protection is theoretical or depends on a technology breakthrough. Equipment to keep data centers connected through grid faults is commercially available today and already running in the field. The first facilities built to Texas’s new standard are about to come online, proving that a firm, predictable regulation can protect reliability without slowing market development. All too often, reliability problems are left lingering until there’s an emergency.

Large load ride through concerns can be solved now. The only thing missing is clear, consistent rules that protect grid reliability while providing certainty to the industry.

#5823. Last Chance to Get It Right: The Industrial Accelerator Act Will Make or Break Europe’s Battery Industry

How the Franco-German auto deal, a wide Made-with-EU scope, and carmakers abandon Europe’s battery value chain to foreign competitors.

There is a growing consensus that Europe must reduce its reliance on Chinese cleantech, especially batteries

The IAA marks a major shift in EU industrial policy. It introduces Union content incentives to ensure scarce public subsidies and corporate tax incentives are used to support European manufacturing. This demand-side pull is crucial if the energy transition is to deliver high-value industrial jobs and lessen critical geopolitical dependencies. There is a growing consensus that Europe must reduce its reliance on Chinese cleantech, especially batteries. But several proposals currently before co-legislators risk severely undermining that overarching goal. Diluting battery criteria in the IAA means giving up on a strategic value chain for Europe, leaving it to China only.

T&E’s assessment shows that only an IAA with a calibrated timeline, robust battery requirements and a strict geographical scope will deliver substantial benefits for Europe. Without secured demand, announced industrial capacity will simply not materialise.

The Franco-German automotive deal from 16 October 2026 is a major blow for Europe’s battery industry as it severely dilutes incentives for batteries. It suggests battery sourcing requirements for eligible vehicles of 10% in 2028-2029, 20% in 2030-2031 and 70% from 2032 on. This is far below what the market already does: over 50% of cells in electric vehicles (EVs) sold in the EU are already Made-in-Europe. By 2030, the deal would only secure 13% (62 GWh) of Europe’s announced cell production: a further 206 GWh would likely find European buyers, though with no guarantee, while 220 GWh would find no demand at all. Even in 2032, the 70% would still not secure a substantial share of potential cell capacity (only 45%).

Another major risk is applying a wide Made-with-EU geographical scope to battery criteria. Compared to a strict EU-only scope, including free trade agreement (FTA) partners puts around 100 GWh of cell demand and 250 GWh of cathode active materials (CAM) demand at risk of leakage in 2030. Limiting the scope to trusted partners only helps to a limited extent. Since Europe can produce enough cells to meet demand under a Made-in-EU IAA, adding partner countries risks leaving EU production without offtakers: even with trusted partners only, a third of potential EU cell supply could be affected. For CAM, around 30% of expected EU supply could be left without a buyer under a trusted-partners scope, rising to 50% under an extended one.

The midstream, CAM and their precursors (pCAM), is the weakest link in Europe’s battery value chain. Boosting this key segment and diversifying away from China is essential for resilience as it shields European industry from supply disruptions and export controls. Under a strict Made-in-EU scope, reaching IAA CAM requirements is technically and commercially viable by 2030, requiring only limited sourcing from trusted partners (33 GWh cell-equivalent). From 2032, EU production can cover demand from EVs under support schemes. A Made-in-EU IAA would generate 323 GWh of extra EU CAM demand in 2032, around the output of six mid-sized EU CAM plants, bringing total EU-made CAM to 407 GWh (securing 94% of expected EU supply).

pCAM is just as vital for resilience and to establish local offtake for European refiners and recyclers. A 28% EU pCAM requirement in 2032 applied to a carmaker’s entire fleet sales (not only for vehicles eligible for public support) creates enough demand for expected EU supply to materialise and projects to reach Final Investment Decision (FID). Onshoring CAM and pCAM adds only a modest cost of $10.6/kWh for Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC) and $6.5/kWh for Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) batteries. This is a necessary sovereignty premium that will fall as the EU supply chain matures.

The car CO₂ standards are as critical to industrial delivery as the IAA. Even under a strict Made-in-EU scope, the European Parliament’s position as approved by the leading committee in November 2026 would sharply cut expected EV sales and reduce EU cell demand by 247 GWh between 2027 and 2032, which equals 3.7 million EU made EVs.

Finally, the proposed fleet booster mechanism fails to drive industrial onshoring and relocation if its compliance trigger is set below 85%. Carmakers’ efforts to lower that threshold aim at getting a stamp of approval on their sourcing strategy in China. A major risk for public subsidies ending up benefiting imported cars with Chinese batteries. Attempts to lower the Booster below a 85% threshold should be rejected, especially if combined with weaker battery criteria.

It’s key to reject the Franco-German deal proposal on batteries.

Battery criteria in this deal are too weak to create additional demand and undermine the IAA’s core purpose.

#5802. Impeaching RFK Jr. Won't Fix American Healthcare

We should be less interested in whether Congress can remove HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. than in what our senators and representatives intend to do about the healthcare system he would leave behind. Regardless of who is charge of HHS, patients still need care they can afford and providers still need to be able to deliver it. Removing Kennedy would not solve either problem.

After nearly three decades in private healthcare, military medicine, the Veterans Health Administration, and health insurance, I have little patience for defending the system as it stands. I am equally skeptical of reforms that promise savings without explaining how care will improve or what implementation will cost. Those are the questions I bring to Kennedy.

I agree with his emphasis on chronic disease prevention. Nutrition, food security, and environmental exposures deserve serious attention. My concern is what happens to people who need treatment while we wait for those investments to produce results. A patient who needs insulin cannot replace it with nutrition education. People with rare or complex conditions still need research, medications, and specialists.

Preventive services also must be available to people who cannot pay for them out of pocket. Expanding insurance coverage for effective prevention makes sense. Expecting patients to embrace a healthier lifestyle while making necessary care harder to obtain, or rolling back safety net programs that help them make healthy choices, does not. Any reform should be judged partly by whether it closes that access gap, especially for low-income patients and underserved communities.

The same practical scrutiny belongs in the debate over federal health agencies that fall under HHS. I support examining conflicts of interest at the FDA, CDC, and NIH. Public trust depends on knowing how decisions are made and whose interests they serve. I fail to see how weakening an agency's ability to do its work would resolve those concerns.

Changes to staffing, research priorities, or advisory processes need a clear account of what will improve and how essential work will continue. Any reorganization must preserve timely treatment reviews and dependable clinical guidance while disease surveillance continues. If it disrupts those functions, its proponents owe the public more than assurances that the old system was flawed.

Scientific assumptions should be challenged at times. But Kennedy's own claims deserve the same scrutiny. Challenging an established finding does not make the alternative correct; it creates an obligation to produce better evidence. In September 2025, Kennedy suggested a possible connection between acetaminophen (Tylenol) use during pregnancy and autism and, soon after, HHS issued new warnings against usage. Meanwhile, in a 2024 study in JAMA involving nearly 2.5 million Swedish children, researchers initially observed a small association, but it disappeared when they compared siblings, accounting for shared genetic and family factors. More recent studies have had similar results.

These findings do not close the door on further research. They do, however, underscore the obligation to address competing explanations before presenting a suspected cause as an answer. Families deserve that precision.

As a researcher, I am particularly concerned about preserving the data needed to make that judgment. Interrupted research and incomplete reporting can leave us unable to tell whether a policy helped or harmed people. New data systems should support existing research and reporting without interrupting them.

Cost deserves the same honesty. A reduction in federal spending may look like an achievement until someone examines where the expense went. States, employers, and families can end up paying more. Hospitals can be left with additional uncompensated care. That is a transfer of financial responsibility, not necessarily savings for the healthcare system.

Providers also have to pay for policy changes. New reimbursement rules can require software, staff training, and additional administrative work. I would give more weight to a reform that reduces billing and coding burdens than to a promise of savings that leaves those expenses uncounted. Payment changes should be phased in with enough attention to hospital and practice finances to avoid disrupting care during the transition.

I want to be clear: these are concerns to investigate, not proof that every change under Kennedy has failed. The incoming Congress should examine coverage and treatment access, preventable hospitalizations, patient costs, workforce stability, research capacity, and readiness for public health emergencies. Where performance deteriorated, Congress should determine why and use its oversight, budgetary, and legislative authority to correct the problem.

Meanwhile, impeachment is a constitutional remedy, not a healthcare performance improvement strategy. Allegations of impeachable misconduct deserve investigation, evidence, and due process. But policy disagreement alone does not establish impeachable misconduct. If the evidence supports a constitutional remedy, Congress should consider it. But our policymakers' responsibility for healthcare will remain regardless of who occupies the health secretary's office.

I want that responsibility taken seriously. Americans should be able to judge reform by the care they can obtain and the bills they can afford. Replacing Kennedy without addressing those problems would leave another generation with a healthcare system that costs more than it delivers. That is the accountability standard worth defending.

#5799. Evidence Grows for Link Between Imaging and Brain Tumor Risk in Kids

— However, findings "should not be used alone when counseling families," experts say

Medical imaging with ionizing radiation was associated with a dose-related increase in brain tumor risk among children and adolescents, a retrospective cohort study suggested.

Compared with no exposure, brain tumor risk increased with cumulative radiation dose, with hazard ratios of 1.12 (95% CI 1.01-1.26) for the lowest exposure category (>0 to <1 mGy) to 3.85 (95% CI 2.43-5.75) for the highest category (≥150 mGy).

Cumulative dose was associated with brain tumor risk overall, with an excess relative risk (ERR) per 100 mGy of 1.27 (95% CI 0.83-1.77, P<0.001), and with a stronger association for aggressive histologies (ERR per 100 mGy 1.51, 95% CI 1.00-2.11, P<0.001) reported Diana L. Miglioretti, PhD, of the University of California Davis School of Medicine, and colleagues.

"This study adds to the growing evidence that pediatric medical imaging with ionizing radiation is associated with increased cancer risk," they wrote in JAMA.

"These findings reinforce the importance of ensuring that pediatric imaging examinations that use ionizing radiation are ordered only when clinically justified and performed using optimized protocols that minimize dose," they concluded.

Miglioretti and team also noted that the cumulative incidence of brain tumors by age 21 increased from 5.4 per 10,000 children with no exposure to 22.1 per 10,000 children with a cumulative dose of 150 mGy or greater, corresponding to an excess risk of 16.7 brain tumors per 10,000 at that dose level.

For children with cumulative doses of 50 to less than 75 mGy -- a dose range comparable to the dose from one head CT -- the cumulative incidence of brain tumors was 13.3 per 10,000 children, corresponding to an excess risk of 7.9 brain tumors per 10,000 exposed children. The estimated population-attributable fraction of brain tumors associated with radiation exposure from medical imaging was 10.1% (95% CI 5.8-14.2).

In an editorial accompanying the study, Ruggero De Maria, MD, of the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore in Rome, and colleagues noted that the study "strengthens an existing epidemiologic signal and makes its potential population impact easier to understand."

However, while these findings "place a rare outcome in perspective," they "should not be used alone when counseling families," they cautioned. "For an individual child, the small potential long-term risk must be considered alongside the often much larger and immediate benefit of obtaining the correct diagnosis."

"For clinical practice, the standard remains straightforward: select the examination most likely to answer the clinical question, use ionizing radiation when its expected benefit justifies the exposure, and obtain the necessary diagnostic information with the lowest dose compatible with adequate image quality," they wrote.

The results from this study support those from other studies, including the European EPI-CT cohort study, which found a significant dose-response relationship, such that for every 10,000 people who received a single head CT scan, approximately one patient developed brain cancer attributable to radiation exposure in the 5 to 15 years following the examination.

However, "these studies were conducted outside North America and therefore reflect CT practices and radiation doses in those settings ... and all focused on radiation exposure from CT rather than cumulative exposure across the full range of medical imaging modalities using ionizing radiation," Miglioretti and colleagues pointed out.

The current study included children born in the U.S. or Ontario, Canada, from 1996 through 2016, and captures imaging histories from birth during a more contemporary era of pediatric imaging. The researchers estimated organ-specific radiation dose from CT, radiography, fluoroscopy, angiography, and nuclear medicine.

A previous analysis of this cohort evaluated hematologic cancer risk and found that up to 10% of blood cancers in children may result from radiation exposure associated with imaging.

The cohort included 3,704,860 children (51.3% male) with 45,671,641 person-years of follow-up (mean follow-up 12.3 years); 82.6% were followed until study end or age 21 years.

A total of 1,536 brain tumors were diagnosed. Of these cases, 55.7% were in males, and 64.5% were diagnosed between the ages of 2 and 10 years. Most tumors (82.5%) were classified as having aggressive histology.

Cumulative dose at the end of follow-up was 1 mGy or greater in 6.8% of all children and 7.9% of those who developed a brain tumor. Overall, 4.4% of all children and 5.9% of those who developed a brain tumor had a cumulative dose of 50 mGy or greater, and 1.1% and 2.3%, respectively, had a cumulative dose of 100 mGy or greater.

Before the end of follow-up, 4.3% of children had one head CT scan, 0.9% had two, and 0.4% had three or more. Among children with a brain tumor, 1.6% had two head CT scans and 1.2% had three or more.

Miglioretti and colleagues acknowledged their study had several limitations, including the fact that it could not account for cancer-predisposing syndromes, and that it did not capture radiation exposure from dental radiographs or cone beam CT.
