#6080. The True Meaning of Power

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

What the Godfather of Realism Can Teach Trump

“International politics, like all politics, is a struggle for power.” Hans Morgenthau wrote this sentence from his desk in Chicago at the dawn of the Cold War, as the United States and the Soviet Union embarked on an open-ended competition for global dominance. Politics Among Nations, the volume that Morgenthau ultimately produced, would quickly become the most widely adopted international relations textbook in the United States and provide an intellectual framework—realism—that would guide generations of scholars and policymakers.

Although much has changed in the nearly eight decades since Morgenthau’s most influential work was published, the fundamentals remain the same. Human nature, which for Morgenthau drove the struggle for power, is unmoved. States are still the most significant actors in the international system and compete for influence. Great powers continue to drive the course of history. Much of today’s world would seem to affirm Morgenthau’s conclusions: Russia’s attempts to establish a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe, China’s quest for hegemony in East Asia, and the United States’ revival of the Monroe Doctrine in the Western Hemisphere all suggest the enduring primacy of national interests over moral aspirations and international norms.

For many, Morgenthau’s contributions begin and end with his identification of power as the key determinant of state behavior. But this reading misses one of his core observations: that even if humans tend to be power-maximizing creatures, they are also moral beings who seek to limit their own power. Morgenthau’s writings reveal a dialectic between power and morality—two seemingly incompatible concepts that he spent his life attempting to reconcile. Far from romanticizing power, Morgenthau saw the need to tame it.

An immigrant who escaped Nazi Germany and owed his life to the United States, Morgenthau had specific guidance to offer his fellow Americans. Even as he urged the United States to protect its vital interests by maintaining a favorable balance of power in key regions, he implored the country to wield its power prudently. Morgenthau saw the United States as the only country with both the capacity and the will to fight on behalf of transcendent values that he believed power had to serve. He was committed to preserving American power as a check on the violence and bigotry he experienced. Yet he feared that U.S. overreach would hollow out the foundations of that power. This, for Morgenthau, would be a tragedy for humanity.

Perhaps no government has misread Morgenthau more than the second Trump administration. Its officials revel in the exercise of military force around the world, from Iran to Venezuela and the Caribbean, while dismissing concerns of alleged war crimes and threatening long-standing allies. White House Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller typified the Trumpian outlook when, in January, he invoked the “iron laws of the world,” one “governed by strength,” “governed by force,” and “governed by power.”

Some analysts have also erroneously rooted the Trump administration’s actions in realism. A lengthy essay in The New York Times Magazine, for instance, ran under the headline “The Theory That Gives Trump a Blank Check for Aggression.” This is understandable, as the Trump administration has repeatedly claimed its actions are underpinned by “flexible realism.”

But any reading of realism that does not consider the moral dimensions of Morgenthau’s theory is both mistaken and dangerous. When the United States limits its own power, abiding by international norms, consulting allies and partners, and wielding its strength in pursuit of transcendent values, countries find American influence more palatable, which helps sustain it. But if Washington’s actions become untethered from these restraints, others will no longer defer to American prerogatives, and the world will become a more dangerous place.

Returning to the foundations of realism—to the man who introduced this intellectual tradition to Americans—is critical. If Morgenthau were to witness this moment in U.S. history, he would conclude that the country had overlearned his lessons about the importance of power and erred in equating it with the use of force. He would see a need to elevate moral questions and rebuild normative constraints on the exercise of power. And he would warn against foreign entanglements that undermine American democracy, which remains the wellspring of the country’s power. These are the lessons that will help the United States navigate an era of intensified great-power competition, one in which the margin for error is thin and the cost of miscalculation could be catastrophic.

Morgenthau was born into an assimilated, middle-class Jewish family in southern Germany in the winter of 1904. His conservative and authoritarian father, Ludwig, raised his only child to revere the monarchy and take pride in their country’s achievements. Morgenthau’s childhood might have passed as that of a shy, precocious boy in a self-assured bourgeois community, but Germany’s defeat in World War I and the rise of anti-Semitism set his life on a drastically different course.

In 1922, as a top student in his high school, Morgenthau was chosen to address his classmates and neighbors in a public ceremony honoring the school’s namesake. When he began to speak, he noticed that some had turned their backs and others were holding their noses at this “stinking Jew.” Morgenthau finished his remarks, but as he left the square, his classmates hurled anti-Semitic insults and spat on him. Just months later, Hitler descended on Morgenthau’s hometown of Coburg with hundreds of storm troopers, who assaulted protesters while the police looked on. Coburg soon gained the ignominious distinction of being the first town to elect the Nazis to power.

Morgenthau left Coburg hoping to become a writer or poet but was forced to pivot to law after hyperinflation ruined his family’s finances. During his apprenticeship under Hugo Sinzheimer, a leading left-wing labor lawyer, he saw conservative judges undermine the Weimar Republic and aid those who sought its destruction. Morgenthau later reflected in his intellectual autobiography that this led him to conclude that “what was decisive was not the merits of different legal interpretations but the distribution of political power.”

As life for German Jews deteriorated, he fled to Geneva to teach law. Switzerland was unfortunately not far enough away; after Hitler took power in Germany, students began boycotting his classes because they did not want to learn German law from a Jew. He then found a teaching position in Madrid but was displaced by the Spanish Civil War and wandered through Europe before arriving in New York in 1937 as a penniless refugee. Having witnessed the rise of fascism up close, Morgenthau was shocked to find that so many Americans believed that human nature was fundamentally good, that peace was the normal condition of humanity, and that history was a story of progress.

In New York and then Kansas City, Morgenthau grew alarmed as the United States watched Nazi Germany rearm, annex Austria, and invade Czechoslovakia. He believed that a failure to understand human nature had inspired Western democracies’ blind faith in international law and the League of Nations, leading them down the path of least resistance with appeasement of Hitler, straight into the moral abyss.

Shaken by the fall of France in 1940, Morgenthau began writing his first English-language book, taking aim at Americans’ confidence that the methods of the natural sciences could make sense of the social world and that the sheer force of reason would eventually eliminate conflict. In Scientific Man vs. Power Politics, published in 1946, he charged that rationalism and liberalism had misunderstood the nature of man, the nature of the world, and the nature of reason itself. Humans, in addition to having rational needs, possessed irrational impulses that could not be reasoned away. Man’s “lust for power would be satisfied only … if he became like God.” Countries, like humans, sought to maximize their power over one another, making conflict inevitable.

Scientific Man received disastrous reviews, and Morgenthau later recalled that it “shocked people to no end.” He adjusted his approach, tailoring his message to Americans and packaging it in a textbook that would appeal to their pragmatism. He wanted this book to mark an evolution in American thinking about foreign policy, privately writing that it would differ from its predecessors “as an automobile differs from a horse and buggy.” Two years later, in Politics Among Nations, he achieved that goal. Morgenthau stressed that the struggle for power was inescapable. All states sought to keep, increase, or demonstrate their strength. Disarmament, collective security, and international law would not contain this struggle. Only a balance of power could prevent countries from attempting to overthrow the existing order. Power could be tamed only by countervailing power.

It is easy to see why many misread Morgenthau as a champion of realpolitik—an heir to the likes of Machiavelli or Bismarck. After all, he placed power at the center of his analysis. But he never romanticized it. As he wrote in a private letter, “I am an analyst of the nation state and of power and have emphasized time and again their negative moral connotations.” Morgenthau’s personal encounters with power had taught him that its unbridled pursuit led to calamity. He understood that normative and moral limitations were required to keep the struggle for power within tolerable bounds. As he wrote in another letter, “It is the task of civilized societies to temper the power drives and direct them into socially useful channels.”

Indeed, Morgenthau had less tolerance for those who failed to reconcile power with morality than for those who underestimated power’s importance. Although President Woodrow Wilson’s universalism and idealism made him one of Morgenthau’s favorite targets, he preferred him to a Machiavellian prince: “Wilson’s failure was at least the failure of greatness, of too great a vision. Nobody will say as much for the epigones of Machiavelli.” He similarly criticized the British historian and political theorist E. H. Carr, whose moral relativism had led him to advocate for appeasing Hitler. Carr, he wrote, had “no transcendent point of view from which to survey the political scene and to appraise the phenomenon of power.” While both men are seen as pivotal realist thinkers, Morgenthau witheringly concluded of Carr that “it is a dangerous thing to be a Machiavelli. It is a disastrous thing to be a Machiavelli without virtù”—that is, it is ruinous to practice power politics without skill and judgment.

In Politics Among Nations, Morgenthau took issue with leaders who both glorify the struggle for power and reject any limits on their own use of power. He argued that “philosophies and political systems which have made the lust and the struggle for power their mainstay have proved impotent and self-destructive.” And he drew attention to common moral standards that could sustain order and moderate the struggle for power. He observed that throughout history, humans insisted on subjecting political action to ethical scrutiny. When such restraints held, countries did less than their power enabled them to do; once commonplace practices, such as assassinating a rival country’s leaders or killing a large portion of its civilian population to reduce its strength, became stigmatized. Morgenthau concluded, “What has changed is the influence of civilization which makes what is desirable and feasible ethically reprehensible and, hence, normally impossible of execution.”

In the immediate aftermath of World War II, however, he believed that the limitations on the struggle for power had reached their weakest point since the advent of the modern state system. Hitler had attempted to extinguish a whole people, and both the Axis and the Allied powers had bombed cities indiscriminately. Although Morgenthau believed the Allies were morally justified in taking such actions to defeat a genocidal regime, he nonetheless feared that the struggle for power was on its way to becoming boundless. In the nuclear age, failing to reestablish these moral barriers could mean the end of humanity.

Morgenthau’s hard-nosed reputation has long obscured the moral core of his realism. He was unequivocal that power must be a means to an end—“an attempt to realize moral values through the medium of politics.” He recognized that maximizing power for its own sake was both destructive and pointless, arguing that “a nation must pursue its interests for the sake of a transcendent purpose which gives meaning to the day-to-day operations of its foreign policy.” In his view, ancient Greece and Rome are remembered because their power served a purpose that outlived their empires: Athenian democracy and philosophy, and Roman law and republican government.

As he assimilated and raised a family in Chicago, Morgenthau came to see the United States as the inheritor of this tradition. The country’s contribution to the world and the source of its power lay in its democracy and civic life. A realist approach—one that prudently wielded power abroad—was best suited to protect the American experiment at home, enabling Americans to live in freedom and security while offering an example for other countries to emulate. In other words, American power had to be bound to purpose—a purpose the country fulfilled “as a light to attract strangers, not as a flame to be spread throughout the world.” This was not merely the observation of a dispassionate scholar. The United States had accepted Morgenthau when no other country would, and it was a place where immigrants like him could rise to the top of their fields.

By the 1960s, Morgenthau saw that American power faced a threat of its own making. He began to develop deep misgivings about U.S. intervention in Vietnam, warning as early as 1961 that it could become a quagmire reminiscent of France’s war in Algeria. Two years later, as clashes between the South Vietnamese government and protesters intensified, Morgenthau urged the United States to get out. He built his opposition on realist grounds, arguing that a communist takeover of Vietnam would not affect the balance of power in Asia, thus rejecting the domino theory, which held that communism would spread inexorably outward once it gained a regional foothold. Morgenthau also recognized that communism was no longer monolithic and believed that Ho Chi Minh could lead a nonaligned country as Tito did in Yugoslavia. He saw the conflict as a civil war and worried that the United States was attempting to maintain a status quo that was itself the source of instability.

Successive administrations failed to heed Morgenthau’s warnings and instead plunged the United States deeper into the war. He came to resent American policymakers’ misappropriation of his lessons on power and entered public debate with new vigor. At a packed rally in New York’s Madison Square Garden in June 1965, he warned that a guerrilla war could be won only through the indiscriminate killing of the South Vietnamese people, which would tear at the moral fiber of the United States. Later that month, Morgenthau debated President Lyndon Johnson’s national security adviser, McGeorge Bundy, on national television. He led with his moral outrage: “I am opposed to our present policy in Vietnam on moral, military, political, and general intellectual grounds.”

The greatest risk of the war, Morgenthau discerned, was “the risk to ourselves, to our mission in the world, to our very existence as a distinct nation.” He lamented, “Here is the last best hope of the downtrodden and the enslaved, to which men of good will throughout the world have looked as a shining example, relieving its frustration in blind ideological fury and aimless destructiveness upon a helpless people.” Vietnam, in his telling, had corrupted the very purpose that gave American power its meaning.

The Vietnam War validated Morgenthau’s belief that power untethered from questions of morality would lead to overreach. It proved that the United States had an interest in constraining itself, if only to avoid disaster. More fundamentally, Vietnam showed that misadventures abroad could boomerang, weakening American democracy and societal cohesion and, ultimately, undermining the country’s international standing.

The second Trump administration’s foreign policy record has thrown these insights into sharp relief. Its officials view questions of morality as a costly sideshow, the remit of woolly-minded idealists who do not understand how the world works. The White House has time and again resorted to military force, striking boats in the Caribbean, kidnapping Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, blockading Cuba, and waging war on Iran. It has also threatened military action against Canada and Denmark, two of the country’s long-standing allies.

American democracy, far from serving as “a light to attract strangers,” is fast becoming a cautionary tale. The second Trump administration has undermined the foundations of American power through attacks on the courts, the press, and universities. It has gleefully bulldozed through any checks on its writ. Its assault on institutions and its aggressive foreign policy are intertwined; the administration not only failed to seek congressional authorization for the war in Iran, but it also refused to even make the case to the American people before striking.

President Donald Trump has justified his aggression on the basis of the “iron laws that have always determined global power.” Morgenthau would not have recognized this as realism. He contended that “superior power gives no right, either moral or legal, to do with that power all that it is physically capable of doing.” The Trump administration, by contrast, seems to believe that to not use the power at its disposal is to waste it. Morgenthau’s verdict on Carr applies equally to Trump: he is a “Machiavelli without virtù,” playing power politics without judgment and leading his country to disaster.

Morgenthau would argue that the Trump administration misunderstands the very concept of power, which should not be equated with military might and must instead be understood as a complex psychological relationship. Real power, Morgenthau insisted, lies in the ability to influence a country through consent, not coercion. In this view, the use of force signifies the abdication of political power rather than its culmination. The Trump administration’s belligerence betrays its weakness: its acts of aggression around the world are confessions of its inability to shape events through other means.

Although Morgenthau grew up on the statecraft of Bismarck, he came to admire Abraham Lincoln above all others, even attempting to write a book about him toward the end of his life. Morgenthau saw in Lincoln a rare leader who was never possessed by power, instead possessing it and, ultimately, subordinating it to transcendent values. Today, just as at the height of the Vietnam War, Morgenthau would see his task as restoring morality and ethics to their rightful place and highlighting the need for power to move the world toward justice.

The United States has lost its purpose, and unless it rediscovers it, discrete foreign policy successes will be irrelevant. As Morgenthau wrote, if the country loses its soul, any victories are merely “footnotes to the history of the decline and fall of a once great nation.”

#6077. Democrats Shouldn’t Squander Their Chance to Launch a Foreign Policy Revolution

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

MATTHEW DUSS is Executive Vice President of the Center for International Policy. From 2017 to 2022, he served as Chief Foreign Policy Adviser to Senator Bernie Sanders.

The Imperative of Ambition in a New Congress

Tuesday’s election made clear that endless wars, political corruption, and the high cost of living are three of the American public’s top concerns. They are deeply interrelated—and the new Congress must address all three. That will require a true foreign policy revolution that grapples not just with the outrages and failures of the Trump administration’s foreign policy but also with the ways in which past Democratic or Democrat-supported foreign policies have contributed to today’s crisis. There will be a temptation among some Democratic leaders, in Congress and beyond, to limit the scope or pace of change. But to find a way out of today’s crisis and move beyond the failures that have afflicted American foreign policy for the past several decades, Democrats will need to have the courage to face a hard truth: that President Donald Trump’s abuses of power and unilateral warmaking are not so much a break from Washington’s militarist mindset as they are a particularly aggressive, predatory expression of it.

The incoming Democratic majority will face a national security environment in which the most severe challenges to U.S. safety and prosperity come from within. That’s why Congress should shut down the Iran war immediately but also recognize that it can make the United States more secure only if it pairs a foreign policy shift with a frontal assault on spiraling costs and political corruption. Political polarization; government corruption and dysfunction; increasing dominance of political and economic life by a small handful of mercurial oligarchs; and a president who recklessly and unilaterally launches unauthorized military interventions while relentlessly seeking opportunities to enrich himself, his family, and his cronies—all pose more immediate danger to the safety and prosperity of the American people than does any foreign enemy.

When Democrats take control of Congress in January, they will have considerable power to challenge the corruption and malfeasance of the Trump administration. Control of committees and of the congressional calendar will provide the opportunity for sorely needed oversight. But although the Trump administration deserves aggressive investigation, Democrats must aim higher—and use the opportunity to articulate a genuinely compelling alternative for American foreign policy.

The first and most urgent task will be ending the Iran war, a reckless and unpopular conflict that has become even less popular as Americans have felt its effects on their wallets. Congress must respond to widespread bipartisan anger among voters at Trump’s war and abuse of war powers by using all the tools it has, especially its power to cut funding. It has been encouraging to see Democrats continuing to oppose any supplemental budget request by the administration for the war. It is an area where Democrats have gotten tripped up in the past when they’ve been afraid of accusations that they don’t “support the troops” if they oppose funding for foolish, counterproductive wars. With Democrats having held firm against any additional funding for the war, those days appear to be gone. That is good.

But Democrats need to go further. They should be prepared to oppose all funding for the military, including blocking votes on the National Defense Authorization Act, the annual bill setting out the broad scope and budget for the Defense Department, in order to put an end to hostilities in Iran. In fact, not only should they be willing to withhold funding from the Pentagon, but they should also be willing to withhold funding for the entire government, and threaten another government shutdown if the bill does not prohibit any funding for Trump’s war. December 11 is the deadline for a continuing resolution to keep the government funded and operating. If ever there was a reason for Democrats to threaten to withhold their votes, stopping a blatantly illegal and globally destructive war that American voters have now resoundingly rebuked is it.

Democrats must also be willing to take a hard look at the continuity between Trump’s use of military violence and past uses of force by administrations of both parties, and the bipartisan support for that violence. The groundwork for the Iran war was laid by the Biden administration’s unstinting support for Israel’s genocide in Gaza (as even some former administration officials have acknowledged), but also by years of U.S. refusal to impose any consequences on the Israeli government despite clear evidence of ongoing violations of human rights and international law. Trump’s strikes on alleged “narcoterrorist” drug boats in the Caribbean represent the logical evolution of long-standing treatment of the United States’ drug problem as a “war,” and of casually applying the label of “terrorist” to justify any and all measures, including execution without due process. Seeking accountability for these abuses will require acknowledging the past complicity of both Republicans and Democrats.

Democrats must also take on corruption, a key node connecting the country’s domestic and foreign policy crises. In doing so, they will be walking through an open door. Polls consistently show that political corruption is high on Americans’ list of concerns. According to a recent Harris poll conducted for The Guardian, “Nearly nine out of 10 Americans said that government corruption, as defined as elected officials putting their own or their donors’ financial interests ahead of the public’s, is a problem.” A mid-October CNBC All-America Economic Survey found that voters are more concerned about government corruption than any other issue, including high grocery and gas prices. In September, Gallup reported that “Americans’ perceptions of government corruption in the U.S. are at their highest level in 20 years, with 89% of U.S. adults saying it is widespread.”

To say that the Trump administration provides a target-rich environment for a strong anticorruption push is to commit felony understatement. Exposing foreign gifts to Trump and conflicts of interest by family members, contracts awarded to Trump’s oligarch donors and new tech buddies, and the selling of pardons, as well as restoring enforcement of the Corporate Transparency Act, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, and related task forces, should all be part of the plan. Democrats should drill down into Trump’s wildly corrupt “Board of Peace” for Gaza, which U.S. Representative Jamie Raskin, the Maryland Democrat set to chair the House Judiciary Committee, rightly referred to as a “global slush fund.” The obvious conflicts of interests of Trump’s diplomatic envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner also demand investigation.

The good news is that effective anticorruption does not necessarily require new legislation; it merely requires that existing legislation be enforced. An August 2025 essay in Foreign Affairs, co-authored with my colleagues Casey Michel and Trevor Sutton, laid out a more expansive case for Democrats putting anticorruption at the center of their foreign policy platform. (A recent essay by Casey Michel lays out a more detailed agenda for the new Congress.)

Ultimately, the most important step toward tackling corruption will be reforming the campaign finance system. The United States is an outlier among developed democracies in that its system of campaign finance allows for what is essentially legalized bribery. Legislators speak openly about campaign contributors expecting “returns on investment” for their political donations. This is an outrage. In recent years, foreign actors have increasingly begun bankrolling American legislators, taking advantage of massive loopholes in campaign finance laws and the Federal Election Commission’s lack of enforcement.

Confronting this will be no small task. At a minimum, Congress must move to legislate a response to the Supreme Court’s 2010 Citizens United decision, which effectively greenlighted bottomless campaign funding from American corporations. It must also reform the Federal Election Commission, providing the body with much-needed resources to investigate campaign finance crimes and restoring the practice of allowing congressional appointments to the commission, which has become little more than an appendage of the executive branch.

All of the above steps must be connected to the top concern among American voters at this moment—the high cost of living. Democrats have disagreements in several policy areas, particularly in foreign policy. Their coalition is diverse. But there is consensus around addressing Americans’ material needs urgently and aggressively. Such an approach would confront Trump’s reckless use of tariffs (while recognizing that the neoliberal taboo on tariffs is a thing of the past), rising gas prices because of the Iran war, the need to restore the global minimum tax, the threat of job losses to AI, and the increased power of tech oligarchs over Americans’ economic and political lives, including on foreign policy, as Elon Musk’s restriction of Ukraine’s Starlink connection demonstrated. The approach should involve a mix of tactics, including hearings, oversight investigations, and legislative proposals.

Many of these efforts may not produce new laws in the next two years. But they could become the backbone of a winning agenda for Congress and for a Democratic administration in 2029. Most important, they would represent a step toward the forging of a new American political consensus on which a new and durable foreign policy can be built.

The successful candidate for speaker requires an outright majority of the entire House — 218 votes of 435, if all Representatives-elect cast ballots — when the new Congress convenes Jan. 3.

Keep in mind that House Democrats may score more than 260 seats once all races are settled. So, he can afford dozens of defections.

For House Republicans, Johnson may have signaled his future last month when speaking to colleague Aishah Hasnie.

"If God moves on our hearts and says we should go back to my hometown of Shreveport and start a lawn business, I'll do that," said Johnson.

Now it’s far from certain that Johnson will soon channel Groundskeeper Willie from the Simpsons. But perhaps becoming "The Lawnmower Man" is a better option for Johnson than getting mowed down by your own colleagues in a bid for minority leader.

Maybe the grass IS greener back in Louisiana.

House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, R-La., and Rep. Jim Jordan, R-Ohio, are running to become the House’s top Republican. House Majority Whip Tom Emmer, R-Minn., wants to return as minority whip. He’s facing a challenge from Rep. August Pfluger, R-Texas.

But here’s the trick. How do these House Republicans win the leadership positions yet maintain their own coalitions? It’s unclear if Republicans think that Jordan and Scalise are right for the party — right now. Both are aligned with President Trump. And some Republicans hope to move past that.

Regardless, Democrats campaigned on two things: the cost of living and holding the Trump administration accountable. But what does the Democrats’ legislative agenda look like?

This isn’t like 1994, when former House Speaker Newt Gingrich, R-Ga., famously crafted the 10-point Contract with America as a pledge to voters as to what Republicans would do if they flipped the House for the first time in 40 years.

Democrats have a host of ideas on what they plan to vote on.

"We have to first get in there and really focus on rolling back the cuts from the one big ugly bill," Sen.-elect Juliana Stratton, D-Ill., promised on CNN.

Also expect votes on renewing expired Obamacare subsidies. Democrats demanded money to offset the costs of rising premiums in exchange for their votes to fund the government. The House finally passed a bill to do that. But the Senate didn’t. The standoff over the Obamacare aid was the key component to last year’s record-breaking, 43-day government shutdown.

"The shutdown sharpened the American people's view of the distinction between the parties on this crucial issue," Schumer said.

Democrats are also focused on a key issue from the midterms: the future of AI and data centers. They’re concerned that AI threatens American jobs.

"This revolt against data centers are just the initial tremors of a bigger AI earthquake that is upending American politics," said Rep. Greg Casar, D-Texas, chairman of the Progressive Caucus. "And we have to be on the right side of this issue."

There will undoubtedly be votes to end the war in Iran — perhaps by cutting off money for those operations. The House approved three "war powers" resolutions. The Senate green-lighted one. But those machinations did nothing to hem in the president on the war.

Democrats believe that ending the war will reduce gas prices. And that, in turn, will lower the cost of groceries.

Congressional Republicans balked at unwinding tariffs imposed by President Trump. So, there could be votes on that front, too. Democrats believe the election shifted the dynamic of Republicans aligning behind the President in near lockstep.

"This is the time for (Republicans) to show the courage to end this irrational and self-defeating tariff regime that Trump has put in place," said Rep. Greg Stanton, D-Ariz.

"Most of them realize that the tariffs from a fiscal and economic perspective are completely self-defeating."

However, some Republicans aren’t ready to abandon the old playbook, clinging to the GOP’s hallmark legislative achievement of this Congress.

"I think the tax cuts and the Big, Beautiful Bill were underappreciated by the electorate," Rep. Chris Smith, R-N.J., proffered.

President Trump signaled he’ll both work with Democrats and deploy his veto pen. But it’s about the math. When all House races are settled, Democrats could control more than 260 House seats.

They’re guaranteed 54 Senate seats with Mississippi and Alaska still undecided

It’s about the math. Overriding a veto requires two-thirds of both bodies. That’s 290 in the House and 67 in the Senate. If Democrats hover around 260 seats in the House — and Republicans are ready to ditch the president — hitting the 290 mark isn’t out of reach.

If Democrats clock in at 56 seats in the Senate, cobbling together 11 GOP votes to reach 67 might be tougher. Especially with GOP Trump antagonists like former Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell, R-Ky., and senators John Cornyn, R-Texas; Bill Cassidy, R-La.; and Thom Tillis, R-N.C., departing. But they’re in range.

It all hinges on what Democrats can pass. And what the president is willing to sign.

"This incident underscores a stark operational reality: cybersecurity in healthcare is fundamentally about patient safety," wrote Errol Weiss, the chief security officer at Health ISAC in an email to NPR. "When threat actors manipulate operational technology like cold-chain storage and refrigeration units holding donor blood, plasma, or critical therapies, they aren't merely stealing data or locking down administrative files, they are directly endangering the physical resources required to sustain human life."

On Thursday, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, the FBI, the Department of Health and Human Services, and the U.S. Department of Agriculture published their own fact sheet about threats to industrial refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems, tools they identify as critical to multiple industries from healthcare to chemical production, food and agriculture, and transportation.

The FBI Cyber Branch Operations director, Brett Leatherman, said the bureau is tracking bad actors targeting these systems "across critical infrastructure sectors globally, putting inventory and equipment at risk."

"Industrial refrigeration and cold-chain monitoring systems protect food, medicine, and other temperature-sensitive products, making them attractive targets for malicious cyber actors," Leatherman said.

The French blood bank confirmed in a press release Tuesday that one of its vendors used in cold storage rooms was targeted by malicious cyber activity, resulting in the defrost and temperature thresholds being raised dangerously high. The French Blood Establishment ultimately managed to detect the suspicious activity in time to rescue blood samples.

It remains unclear who is responsible for the global attacks on refrigeration controllers, and whether it's multiple actors or groups.

Iran has been linked to at least one incident in Israel earlier in the spring, though that breach involved a lot more sophistication and malicious activity. Investigators probing the disruptions at U.S. military bases are also considering Iran as the potential culprit.

Meanwhile, two pro-Russian hacking groups, NoName057(16) and Z-Pentest Alliance, belatedly released videos and posted on Telegram claiming responsibility for the attacks across Europe, publishing a sample of alleged hacked credentials that would've allowed them to access equipment including machines made by the Danish company Danfoss and the American company Copeland. Those machines can monitor and control temperature and other functions. The hacking groups have some history of targeting refrigeration controllers and other operational technology. However, it's unclear if the recent wave of attacks on refrigerators and freezers across Europe even required stolen credentials to execute, a factor that has led some cybersecurity experts to be skeptical of their claims.

Even so, it will require more evidence like access logs, configuration changes, and connections between maintenance accounts on the controllers to make any clear assessments, explained Tom Hegel, who leads threat intelligence research at cybersecurity firm SentinelOne.

"Russian-aligned actors are a credible hypothesis because there is a credible precedent, including the refrigeration-related attack DOJ attributed to CyberArmyofRussia_Reborn and NoName's history of politically motivated disruption. But that is only the context, not the missing evidentiary link to these European incidents," Hegel told NPR.

"It's important to note that such Russian threat actors often seek attention and claim to cause incidents even when false," he continued.

Regardless of who's responsible, the incident highlights how these nearly ubiquitous tools used to remotely manage critical systems are vulnerable to attack in several different ways.

For one, thousands of the refrigeration controllers in France alone were exposed to the open internet with almost no protection put in place before unidentified hackers went after them in October. Afterward, experts were still tracking hundreds of devices left unprotected. That's just a fraction of the tens of thousands of exposed operational devices online, many of them embedded in critical infrastructure.

Additionally, many of the major remote management systems used in refrigerators and freezers have security vulnerabilities. After alerting the companies to the problems, cybersecurity firm Claroty published research on vulnerabilities in multiple major refrigeration controllers, including those made by both the American manufacturer Copeland and by Danfoss.

While some of those vulnerabilities have been discovered and the vendors have designed patches to seal the hole, the problems don't stop there.

Experts warn it's difficult to patch operational technology because doing so often requires shutting systems down. In industries like healthcare, even minutes of downtime create extreme risk for patient safety. And for some aging models, it's impossible to patch, because the vendor is no longer offering updates — and companies can't afford to spring for a new model.

Sometimes, executives don't even know what machines are on their networks.

Additionally, for many companies, vendors that work with them demand remote access to systems like refrigeration controllers to regularly service them. It's often a requirement in the contract companies sign.

"Industrial chillers operate under vendor contracts that mandate persistent remote access," explained Nic Adams, CEO of 0rcus, a U.S. offensive security firm. "These commercial agreements leave national food and medical supplies defenseless against basic credential abuse."

Finally, hackers are now using AI to find these vulnerabilities faster than ever, and AI is also helping them to better understand the complex machines and the processes they control. Bad actors a world away no longer need to understand individual languages or industries, or even the physics involved to disrupt operations. In recent weeks, cyber incident responders tell NPR they're seeing a massive spike in attacks that are at least in part carried out by AI agents.

The good news is that a lot of these attacks, even if they incorporate AI, are not particularly sophisticated, novel, or stealthy.

Weiss of Health ISAC tells NPR there are basic steps the healthcare industry can take to prevent being a victim of these opportunistic attacks.

That process "begins" by "eliminating public internet exposure," wrote Weiss, then "enforcing strict network segmentation" in order to isolate refrigeration systems "from standard enterprise and guest networks" and finally adding on safeguards "that software overrides cannot bypass" like "hard-wired mechanical cutoffs" and "independent thermal alarms."

In the Health ISAC alert, the industry is urged to separate not just refrigeration controllers from hospital IT systems, but also medical gas monitors, for one, and train staff onsite on how to take physical manual control of machines in an emergency.

Finally, the alert urges hospitals to have offline backups of their systems.

Sharing intelligence about these kinds of cyberattacks can help other potential victims avoid disaster.

While Estonian grocery stores were targeted in the most recent wave of attacks, many of those store managers were on high alert thanks to warnings issued by Estonia's Information Systems Authority, RIA. "When one Selver store was attacked on 2 November, the attackers managed to change the refrigeration system's temperature setting to +50°C. However, the service provider reacted quickly and prevented significant damage," said Annika Müür, a spokesperson for RIA. "So while we can't say that Estonia managed to prevent the attacks entirely, I think this case is a good example of the importance of monitoring threats internationally, acting on early warnings and working closely with businesses to minimise the impact."

Weiss of Health ISAC also urged companies across sectors that are being targeted by this recent wave of attacks to share information to help others defend themselves.

"The attack highlights why timely, trusted information sharing across borders and sectors is our strongest collective defense," he wrote.

"Rapid intelligence sharing allows hospitals and health facilities globally to recognize emerging attack patterns, verify vendor exposure, and harden critical infrastructure before threat actors can cause physical harm."

#6033. No one knows how many ballots are outstanding in Mississippi's Senate race

Two Madison County election commissioners inspect an affidavit ballot envelope on Thursday in Canton, Miss. The state's razor-thin U.S. Senate race is shining a spotlight on Mississippi's election processes. Rogelio V. Solis/Getty Images hide caption

In Mississippi's too-close-to-call U.S. Senate race, every ballot matters.

But no one can say for sure how many ballots there are left to count.

The razor-thin margin between incumbent Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith and Democratic challenger Scott Colom is shining a light on an election process in Mississippi that is among the slowest and most decentralized in the country. It also doesn't have any provisions for recounts.

"Mississippi is not a model of election administration," said Michael Morse, a law professor at the University of Pennsylvania who studies voting.

As of mid-afternoon Friday, more than 2,000 votes separated the two Senate candidates. But information about exactly how many ballots were outstanding was hard to come by.

The Colom campaign said late Tuesday it was aware of more than 15,000 provisional ballots, which require verification before they're counted. And the chair of the state Republican Party said Thursday he thought there were 20,000 outstanding ballots, including potential absentee votes that can legally be received by officials until Nov. 10 if they were postmarked by Election Day.

Mississippi's top voting official, Republican Secretary of State Michael Watson, said counties run their own elections, and even his office didn't know how many provisionals were left to count. He said midday Friday over 9,000 of them had been processed.

"Until we have the certified results, the individual counties will be the best source of the latest and most up-to-date information," Watson said in a statement. There are 82 counties in Mississippi, and they are not required to transmit election results to the state until next week.

Morse said he was shocked by the lack of good information available for such a high-profile race.

"Right now I would love to know a lot of things about the process and I am struggling to figure out what is unfolding here," Morse said. "I cannot tell you basic things."

What is becoming clear is that the rate of provisional ballots, which are called affidavit ballots in Mississippi, may end up being among the highest in the country this election cycle. Those ballots are cast by people whose eligibility is questionable, according to local officials, so those votes are segregated and officials decide on a case-by-case basis whether they will be counted.

If the Colom campaign's estimate of 15,000 affidavit ballots holds true, that would represent roughly 1.5% of the total ballots cast in the Senate race, according to unofficial results.

In the last midterm year, 2022, just four states had a provisional ballot rate higher than that, according to federal data. And Mississippi looks to have a provisional rate close to double of that in North Carolina this year, based on the preliminary data in both states.

One potential reason is an in-the-weeds detail on how Mississippi does voter registration.

In most states, when voters don't cast ballots over a certain number of election cycles, or local officials come across data that indicates a voter has moved or is ineligible for some other reason, they move that voter to what's known as "inactive" status. But the voter is still able to vote normally for a period of a few years, mandated by federal law, before they either confirm their eligibility or are eventually removed.

Mississippi is one of the few states that makes those "inactive" voters have to vote provisionally. That means those voters need to fill out another form and their information needs to be reviewed after the fact by local officials before their votes are counted.

It's unclear at this point why so many voters had questionable eligibility when they showed up to vote, although the local outlet Mississippi Today has reported extensively on the state's experimental use of credit data to cull voter lists.

Morse said that list maintenance technique is garnering attention, but it's not the whole story.

"It's pairing the experiment with credit data, which could introduce some unreliability, with an undermining of the typical procedural process [in many other states], where a voter [marked "inactive"] could cast a regular ballot for at least the next two federal general elections," Morse said.

Watson, the Republican secretary of state, has already come out in defense of the state's registration process.

"The extra few minutes it may take to vote via affidavit ballot are well worth the election integrity added by having cleaner voter rolls!" Watson wrote Tuesday in a post on X.

But Kathy Boockvar, the former Democratic secretary of state of Pennsylvania, told NPR that while many people who have to vote with a provisional ballot are likely to be ultimately deemed eligible, the process doesn't breed trust from those who have to interact with it.

"It feels like you have to go talk to the teacher or something in the back room," Boockvar said. "It's a little bit of a suspicious-seeming thing because somebody who's going through it generally thinks that they're eligible to vote and feels like they're being asked to do something other than that."

Boockvar added that the fact that information has been so hard to come by is a sign Mississippi hasn't been in the limelight like traditional battleground states before.

"We all have to be prepared whether we expect it or not," Boockvar said. "Transparency and good data is one of the best solutions we have to combating all the suspicion and distrust."

#6001. The Future of Buildings in Illinois Is Electric

The Illinois Commerce Commission authorized a study of the most affordable way for the state to meet its building decarbonization goals. The report has fundamental flaws but critical insights.

Illinois’s landmark Climate and Equitable Jobs Act (CEJA) sets the goal of 100 percent clean energy across the state by 2050. For nonelectric sources of emissions and transportation—such as the 17 percent of emissions that occur when we burn gas to heat our homes and other buildings where we live, work, and play—CEJA provides little direction on how these sectors are expected to align with the state’s climate goals.

The Illinois Commerce Commission (ICC), which regulates the state’s electric and gas utilities, showed real leadership in 2023 when it found that “if the decarbonization goals of CEJA are to be met, the gas distribution system as currently operated will need to change.” To chart that change, the ICC launched a Future of Gas (FOG) open stakeholder process to identify the most efficient, cost-effective path to a gas system that fits the state’s climate goals.

As part of this FOG process, the ICC authorized a consultant to model five pathways to that CEJA-compliant system by 2050. In its recently released Gas Decarbonization Pathways Study, the consultant modeled a range of scenarios, from one where the state invests heavily in energy efficiency and helps customers replace outdated gas appliances with high-efficiency electric alternatives to others that rely on unproven biofuels with no clear evidence that they can be produced at anything close to the scale needed. For each scenario, the consultant was expected to assess technical feasibility, up-front and operating costs, impacts on the gas and electric systems, emissions reductions, and effects on environmental justice communities, all to help Illinois meet its gas decarbonization goals at the lowest cost and with the most benefits to residents.

Unfortunately, the consultant’s final report is biased by faulty (and at times, plainly unrealistic) assumptions that do not allow for accurate comparison across scenarios. Despite this, the study yields a clear finding: Gas use in Illinois’s buildings and industrial facilities must decline by at least 50 percent in order to reach the state’s climate target of net-zero emissions by 2050. Together with the state’s recently released Comprehensive Climate Action Plan, the study offers Illinois policymakers a clear agenda for initiating the transition to an affordable, equitable, and clean energy future.

The study offers some useful insights, but treating all of its findings as a road map for Illinois’s gas transition would be a mistake. Before getting into the no-regrets steps that Illinois can take now, we need to unpack the biggest problems with the report, including the flawed assumptions behind some of its conclusions.

Despite the study’s shortcomings detailed above, several key findings should influence the recommendations in the final FOG report:

The remaining technologies are incredibly early-stage, and to the extent that they are available at all over the next several decades, they are likely to be extremely expensive. The study’s assumptions around e-methane are particularly troubling. Modeling initially showed that the Continued Gas Use and Hybrid Gas cases could not meet the net-zero-by-2050 emissions requirement using only available technologies, like biomethane. As a result, in the final report, the model selected e-methane to backfill any resource gap—despite the fact that e-methane derived from green hydrogen and direct air carbon capture is still in “prototype and demonstration stage” today.

While the pathways study acknowledges that “[h]igher-gas scenarios present … greater challenges in alternative fuel procurement, technology readiness, long-term affordability, and air quality” compared to electrification-focused scenarios, this is a significant understatement. The state can and must act now to achieve the state’s climate goals in an affordable and equitable way. Illinois does not need to solve every aspect of the transition in the next few years. However, it can begin taking the following no-regrets steps now to prepare for a changing energy landscape.

Despite its numerous flaws, the study underscores the transformative role that clean, electric heat pumps will play in Illinois’s energy future and the need to plan for a smaller gas system. The ICC and the state legislature can and must begin advancing no-regrets building electrification policies today to ensure tomorrow’s energy system is healthy, clean, and affordable for all of the state’s residents.

#5998. Congress Is Cutting Off Transportation Investments that Help Families Save Money

The loss of guaranteed federal funding could stall projects that make it easier and cheaper for Americans to get where they need to go.

UPDATE: On October 27, 2026, an update to the Senate Commerce Committee’s Chopping Block report provided a state-by-state assessment of the harm to transportation systems if Congress fails to renew the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law’s $36.8 billion in advance appropriations funding. Led by Ranking Member Cantwell, the report demonstrates that these cuts will only drive increased costs for families and business across the country.

At a time when Americans are struggling to pay staggering prices at the fuel pump and the Trump administration sows uncertainty for states and communities by clawing back committed transportation infrastructure funds, Congress is moving to scale back more critical federal funding for affordable and clean transportation.

The need for consistent funding is clear: Transportation costs are the second-largest household expense in the United States, and our roads and transit infrastructure remain structurally inadequate. Nevertheless, state transportation and transit authorities are facing a significant funding cliff due to the expiration of the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) on September 30, 2026. Over the past five years, the infrastructure law has provided a guaranteed pot of funding worth $36.8 billion for transportation projects. These “advanced appropriations” cover a wide breadth of programs and include large amounts of funding for clean transportation, such as electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure projects, transit, and rail investments. This funding has helped state transit and transportation authorities plan for longer-term projects and has been critical to the infrastructure law’s support of almost 70,000 unique transportation projects across the country.

But transportation funding from the infrastructure law ran out on September 30, 2026, and while Congress passed a short-term extension until December 11, 2026, it does not include these guaranteed dollars. This means that, for the three-month duration of the extension, states will lack the continuity and certainty they need to plan for both large- and small-scale infrastructure projects.

To add insult to injury, Congress also seems to be contemplating leaving this guaranteed funding out of the next transportation bill altogether. The House BUILD America 250 Act proposal, released in May, proposes cutting all advanced appropriations funding. The proposal, in fact, slashes transportation funding from current infrastructure law levels by almost 50 percent, leaving Americans without access to travel options that help defray rising costs.

Historically, transportation bills have included authorizations of two types of funding: (1) contract authority, which is supported by revenues that flow into the Highway Trust Fund and (2) general funds, which Congress has to “appropriate” and assign annually. The current infrastructure law includes another category—advanced appropriations—which makes money available in the future for certain programs without the need for any congressional action in future years, guaranteeing states and communities secure funding over the five-year bill period. This certainty allows for states to plan in advance for future projects and grant opportunities.

Any cut to this pot of money takes a sledgehammer to clean transportation programs that reduce household costs and improve the health of our communities. Particular programs at risk include the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure program, which helps states build a national network of EV chargers; the Capital Investment Grants, a transit-related discretionary grant program; and the Reconnecting Communities Pilot, which aims to address harms caused by past transportation decisions. Also of concern are the BUILD and MEGA grants, which help support the build-out of transit, bicycle, and pedestrian infrastructure.

Congress is unlikely to agree on a new bill before the current extension comes to an end in December 2026, meaning that there is an opportunity to include advanced appropriations in any future extension of the current infrastructure law and the next transportation bill so that states and local governments can avoid having to grapple with temporary or potentially even permanent funding interruptions. This guaranteed funding is even more important now as transportation decision-makers contend with the Trump administration interfering with implementation and funding guidance. Additional funding clawbacks will only serve to stall progress. The sustained funding will also allow states and local governments to continue to build on the progress that the current infrastructure law made on clean transportation investments.

Even more important, American households are currently struggling to afford their basic daily necessities, and soaring gas prices have left many searching for ways to save on their transportation costs. In 2024, when gas prices averaged below $3.50 a gallon (a far cry from the current average price of $4.35 a gallon), transportation was the second-largest cost for American households, averaging 17 percent of household budgets. Cutting advanced appropriations funding for transit, EV charging, and rail will only serve to penalize the very households struggling the most, at a time when those investments would give Americans access to a wide range of affordable, reliable, and safe transportation options.

#5989. Trump Wants More Attention For His New Algae Biofuel Showcase

Situated on filled ground in former marshlands, the Reflecting Pool provides a golden opportunity to showcase the ability of the domestic biofuel industry to take advantage of the built infrastructure, rather than taking up valuable farmland with conventional energy crops like corn or soybeans.

Although algae cultivation was probably not the original intention of the Reflecting Pool, it has been a perennial host to algae ever since its construction in the 1920’s, with the humid environment and temperate climate of DC lending a hand.

In earlier years, the Pool was drained and refilled twice a year to control the growth. By the 1990’s, the structure was clearly sinking into the marshes, sparking calls for a major structural overhaul and the addition of a water circulating system.

Algae biofuel surfaced regularly on the pages of CleanTechnica in the early 2000’s, with the high oil content and rapid growth cycle of algae being the attractions along with the potential for developing next-generation farming and extraction systems leading to a competitive renewable energy resource (see more algae background here).

Not much progress has been made since then, and stakeholders in the field have weathered much skepticism if not downright mockery. A seal of approval from the President of the United States of America is just what the industry needs to get to the next level.

The Oman operation is set for operation at a demonstration scale before the end of 2027, which is just around the corner. Meanwhile, Japanese automaker Mazda is also beginning to crack the algae code.

Where conventional systems involve cultivating algae, drying it, and processing it to remove oils, the Saitama team has developed a strain of cyanobacteria (commonly known as blue-green algae) that can express free fatty acids outside of the cell and deposit them directly into a culture medium. Among other benefits, the Saitama system does not require destroying the algae with each harvesting cycle.

It’s too bad Exxon dropped the ball before Trump had a chance to pick up the algae biofuel torch. Oh, well. We’ll always have the Reflecting Pool…

#5984. Aluminum–Air Generators for Data Centres Face a Metal Logistics Test

California startup Voya Energy announced a $35 million financing round in August 2026 for generators that use prepared scrap aluminum, air and water to produce electricity without combustion. Its immediate opportunity includes replacing diesel backup equipment at data centres, where exhaust, noise and permitting can be genuine obstacles. The company also describes a larger market for firm power at grid-constrained sites, including systems operating around the clock. The electrochemistry is established, but delivering competitive electricity requires a materials supply chain that the generator specifications do not describe.

An aluminum–air cell consumes aluminum. It does not accept electricity through a charging cable and return to its original chemical state. Aluminum reacts with oxygen and water, producing electrical energy and aluminum hydroxide, sometimes called aluminum trihydrate. The generator needs more metal when the fuel is spent, and the hydroxide has to be collected, transported and either sold into another market or processed further. The aluminum brought to a data centre already embodies substantial electricity used earlier to turn aluminum compounds into metal. That upstream energy belongs in the assessment.

I encountered the same supply-chain issues when I examined Wright Electric’s aluminum–air aircraft proposal in 2021. Prepared aluminum would have needed delivery to airports, followed by collection and processing of spent material. Data centres avoid the aircraft weight constraint, while retaining the need for deliveries of prepared fuel and industrial handling of reaction products.

The operating schedule determines the size of that problem. Voya’s August announcement emphasizes diesel replacement while also describing larger, continuously available power installations. To test the larger proposition, I calculated the materials needed for a hypothetical 100 MW data centre supplied continuously by aluminum–air generation. Using the company’s reported 57% conversion efficiency and aluminum’s theoretical electrochemical energy, the system consumes roughly 520 metric tons of metal each day and produces about 1,500 metric tons of aluminum hydroxide. That becomes approximately 190,000 tons of incoming metal and 550,000 tons of outgoing material each year. The additional byproduct mass comes from oxygen and water incorporated in the reaction. These are scenario quantities, not measured operations at a Voya site.

An intermittent backup generator could consume orders of magnitude less aluminum annually than the continuous 100 MW case, changing the economics of deliveries and residue handling. Voya’s interest in low-grade contaminated scrap deserves examination, since some metal streams are difficult to sort into high-quality recycled products. Yet scrap is not automatically valueless because it is contaminated. Recycling aluminum into new metallic products uses approximately 95% less energy than making primary metal, and improving scrap recovery is itself an important industrial decarbonization opportunity. Buying that scrap as expendable fuel can compete with other recovery routes as their technology and economics improve.

Voya initially expects to sell aluminum hydroxide and proposes metal regeneration as a longer-term option. Those are different uses for the spent material. A 2026 lifecycle assessment modeled electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency of 29–35% for aluminum–air storage pathways involving regeneration. That finding is not a test result for Voya’s equipment, but it indicates how much additional electricity and processing could be needed to close the material loop. A company selling the hydroxide cannot also assume it will recover that same material for fuel without buying it back.

Voya’s published metal-price target leaves limited room for other costs. In a 2025 California Energy Commission submission, the company listed targets below $0.30/kWh for delivered electricity and $0.50 per pound for the metal fuel. At 57% conversion efficiency, the latter implies about $0.24/kWh for aluminum alone, before fuel preparation, generator capital, transport, residue handling or financing. Saleable hydroxide could provide an offset where purity, processing costs and buyers support it. The generation target remains a target, rather than a demonstrated commercial tariff.

There may be worthwhile applications for compact, noncombustion backup power where diesel is hard to permit and suitable scrap is readily available. Continuous power for large AI facilities calls for a much larger comparison with grid connections, generation and rechargeable batteries. The chemistry works, but the economics must account for every load of metal delivered, every load of residue removed and the electricity consumed upstream.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing assessment follows the aluminum through the generator, the possible recycling and regeneration routes, Voya’s cost targets and the very different material demands of backup and continuous generation.

#5981. PJM’s Emergency Order Should Be a Wake-Up Call for Michigan: Build More Power Before the Next Crisis

By Terence Parker, Michigan resident and Solar Mounts LLC team member

The U.S. Department of Energy’s October 12, 2026, emergency order directing PJM Interconnection to use additional generating resources should be a wake-up call for Michigan and the entire PJM region. Effective October 13–16, the order authorizes specified power plants to operate as needed for reliability and allows backup generators at large facilities to help reduce grid demand during emergencies.

This does not mean blackouts are inevitable. However, it highlights the serious challenges facing our electricity system and the need to add capacity before emergency measures become routine.

As a Michigan-based company, Solar Mounts LLC, a designer and manufacturer of commercial and utility-scale solar mounting systems, recognizes the potential implications for electricity prices, air quality, local businesses, and families across the state.

Rising Costs and the Consequences of Delayed Generation

PJM’s capacity auctions have exposed the difficulty of securing enough electricity for future demand. Prices reached $329.17 per megawatt-day for the 2026–2027 delivery year and $333.44 for 2027–2028, with the latter auction falling short of PJM’s stated reliability requirement.

Source: Vicinity Energy’s Analysis of PJM Capacity Auctions

Capacity costs are only one component of electricity bills, but rising prices signal growing pressure on consumers. We need more power before shortages force increasingly expensive emergency responses.

The October 2026 order permits designated generating units to exceed specified operating limits when necessary for reliability. It also authorizes PJM to direct backup generation at data centers and other large facilities, potentially including diesel generators as a last resort.

Running fossil-fuel plants more frequently can increase emissions, depending on the units involved. Operating diesel generators will worsen local noise pollution and expose operators to high diesel prices. If these measures become more common, we should ask why cleaner generation and storage are not coming online faster.

Weather Uncertainty Makes Planning Urgent

NOAA’s October 15, 2026, winter outlook anticipates warmer-than-average conditions across much of the northern United States, including the Midwest, influenced by a strong El Niño.

Source: NOAA’s 2026–2027 U.S. Winter Outlook

Seasonal forecasts cannot predict every period of extreme demand. Aging infrastructure, maintenance, changing weather, and growing electricity consumption reinforce the need for advance planning. Temporary authority to run additional fossil-fuel resources cannot replace long-term investment in generation.

Solar, Wind, and Batteries Can Help Close the Gap

Utility-scale solar, wind, and battery storage can help meet demand on timelines that many conventional generation projects cannot match. Suitable solar projects may be built within 18 months, depending on permitting, equipment, and interconnection. New nuclear facilities generally require substantially longer development periods.

Solar and wind will reduce emissions when they displace electricity from fossil-fuel generators. Batteries store surplus electricity for periods of higher demand. Note: actual emissions benefits depend on which generators are displaced and when electricity is delivered.

Faster interconnection reviews are also essential. Qualified solar projects shouldn’t remain in queues for years because of avoidable administrative delays. Better coordination among PJM, utilities, regulators, developers, and state governments, alongside necessary transmission upgrades, will bring capacity online sooner.

Solar Development and Agriculture Can Coexist

At Solar Mounts LLC, we witness concerns about installing solar on productive farmland here in rural Calhoun County (home to Solar Mounts LLC). However, well-designed projects can include restoration plans that allow agricultural use to resume when leases end. Solar leases can also provide farmers with supplemental income when crop prices fail to keep pace with production costs.

Agrivoltaic systems combine electricity generation with compatible crops or livestock. Thoughtful design and community engagement can balance renewable energy development with agricultural productivity, rural traditions, and farmland preservation.

Opportunities extend beyond prime farmland. Parking lots, commercial properties, brownfields, landfills, and other underutilized sites can accommodate solar generation. Solar carports provide electricity, shade, and weather protection, while community solar expands access for people unable or unwilling to install their own panels.

Solar Mounts LLC supports practical, site-appropriate solutions that expand generation while respecting farmers, landowners, and rural communities.

Hyperscale Data Centers Must Help Provide Their Own Power

Growing data center demand, particularly in Virginia and other parts of PJM’s territory, is adding pressure to an already constrained grid. Consequently, developers of new hyperscale facilities should demonstrate how they will secure adequate electricity and pay their fair share of supporting infrastructure. Dedicated generation, renewable energy contracts, batteries, flexible demand, and grid upgrades can all contribute.

Requiring every facility to build a gas-fired peaker plant is not necessarily the best answer. Gas turbines face equipment and supply-chain constraints, while additional fossil-fuel generation carries emissions and operating costs. A combination of solutions tailored to each location is more practical.

Existing facilities should also reduce peak demand where possible. Energy efficiency remains one of the most immediate ways businesses can limit electricity consumption.

Solar Mounts LLC sees the opportunity to expand commercial and utility-scale solar infrastructure across Michigan and the country. We encourage this expansion by supporting faster approvals, better grid planning, and a serious commitment to adding power generation and energy storage before shortages worsen.

Reliable electricity underpins our economy, jobs, and daily lives. Repeated emergency orders, rising capacity costs, and greater reliance on high-emission generation are not sound long-term strategies.

We shouldn’t wait for the next crisis to build the power system we already know we need.

#5977. If The US Department Of Defense Gets Solar Power, Why Can’t We?

In the topsy-turvy world of today’s federal energy policy, solar power is a bad thing. Various members of the President’s cabinet have advised that solar is  “unreliable,” and that solar power plants routinely experience “catastrophic failure” with every sunset. So why does the US Department of Defense continue to pursue solar-enabled microgrids at its facilities? Asking for a friend…

Though some solar projects at military facilities are relatively small, they are stamped with the seal of approval from the Defense Department, and that can have an outsized influence on public perceptions about the reliability, security, and durability of photovoltaic systems.

The history-making White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico is a case in point. In February of 2025 the facility celebrated the launch of a new, 700-kilowatt solar array designed and built by the joint venture HSGS-Ameresco.

At 700 kW, the new array is peanuts compared to the megawatt scale common among modern solar power plants. However, a little can go a long way in some cases, particularly when solar is integrated with other energy resources in a microgrid that can operate independently, without interruption, if the local distribution grid goes down.

“The innovative microgrid system, which integrates a 700kW solar photovoltaic array, a 500kW natural gas generator, and a 500kW lithium-ion battery system, will provide backup power to four groundwater wells on the range,” Ameresco explained in a press statement last year, in which it described the White Sands project.

The four wells are the primary source of potable water to the main post at White Sands. The new microgrid meets a US Army requirement for up to 14 days of independent water and power delivery to the post in case of a grid outage.

“This self-sufficient energy system, designed to serve a specific geographic area, helps ensure that critical missions at WSMR can proceed without interruption,” Ameresco elaborates, adding that the microgrid underscores White Sands’s commitment to building more resilient energy systems into its future construction projects.

Ameresco also indicated that the now-familiar combineation of solar power and BESS (battery energy storage systems) will do most of the heavy lifting, with the gas generator playing a back-up role if necessary.

“The solar array captures energy from the sun during the day, while the natural gas generator provides a dependable backup power source. The lithium-ion battery system efficiently stores excess energy generated by the solar array and releases it when needed, helping to ensure an uninterrupted power supply,” Ameresco emphasized.

The role of a lithium-ion battery in the new microgrid may come as quite a shock to US Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, who has somehow failed to get memo about energy storage systems for solar power. Energy storage is an actual thing that exists in the space-time continuum here on Earth, but apparently not in Burgum’s corner of the mulitverse.

“And of course, when the sun goes down, you have a catastrophic failure called sunset and there’s no solar energy produced, and yet we’re subsidizing these things that are intermittent, unreliable, and expensive,” Burgum told a Fox News audience in July of 2025.

Whether Burgum believes it or not, the HGSG-Ameresco project is living proof that solar power is just as reliable as any other energy resource, with a mature supply chain and knowledge base to back it up.

Take the Florida-based firm Cantsink, for example. A specialist in  piling specialist infrastructure, Cantskin installed the supports for  the solar racking system in the new microgrid.

“Cantsink delivered a foundation system engineered to withstand the demanding conditions of a military testing range,” the company reports in a recap of the project on its website.

“Given the site conditions, pull testing was performed to determine pile requirements, which confirmed that 17’6″ piles were needed to support the racking system,” Cantsink further explains.

Among other details, Cantsink notes that the racking system is supported by 193 8×10 piles measuring 17’6″ long. “Every bolt used had a torque requirement that was inspected by a vendor appointed third party,” Cantsink emphasizes.

Evidently the people in charge of energy systems at White Sands were satisfied with the results of their new microgrid, and the facility is on track to receive another solar-enabled microgrid. On November 3, the Kentucky-based global-facing engineering and infrastructure firm ABM described a new microgrid in the planning stages at White Sands, consisting of a carport-mounted solar power plant, BESS, and dispatchable on-site generators, presumably meaning gas generators for backup power.

The construction contract for the facility was assigned to ABM’s joint venture partner, AACON-RavenVolt. The AACON part of the venture is a general contractor based in Louisville, Kentucky. Ravenvolt is a subsidiary of ABM based in Georgia, having been acquired by the firm in 2022.

The relationship with RavenVolt is significant because ABM has positioned the partnership to zero in on microgrid opportunities throughout the federal government. “The partnership provides federal agencies with dependable, end-to-end energy resilience and infrastructure construction capabilities,” ABM notes.

Keep an eye on ABM and RavenVolt moving forward, as EV stakeholders in the US anticipate an uptick in sales following President Donald Trump’s decision to start a war against Iran last February. That will increase the demand for kilowatts, and RavenVolt is among those advocating for solar-enabled microgrids to avoid grid-related bottlenecks.

“It’s all about the easy integration of on-site renewables, whether that’s solar, battery or ensuring on-site resiliency and expansion capability,” explains RavenVolt co-founder and President of Sales Cody George. The company’s microgrid model is designed for ease of installation, combining whole-of-system standardization while tailoring the switchgear to customer-specific applications.

“We’re not beholden to one technology, so our customers aren’t locked into outdated infrastructure forcing them to completely retool,” George adds.

Circling back around to military applications, it’s not surprising to see solar power resurfacing as a resilient energy resource over at the Defense Department, regardless of last year’s sharp U-turn in federal energy policy. Resiliency was front and center in the early 2000’s, when the Defense Department began pushing the envelope on solar power while the civilian market was still spinning its wheels. Solar panels were still relatively expensive back then, and the civilian market was slow on the uptake. Energy storage systems were more expensive, too.

As one indication that the Defense Department is still carving out its own energy destiny, get a load of Defense Secretary Pete Hesgeth’s new “FORTRESS America” resiliency plan. The goal is to make major Defense Department facilities in the US energy independent for at least 90 days, alongside other emergency stockpiling measures.

Nuclear and fossil fuels are part of the plan, but keep an eye on the solar-plus-storage combo to be first out of the box on a speed-to-power basis.

#5974. Overshoot Demands a New Era of Adaptation, Loss & Damage, and Climate Justice

As the world is on track to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius of global warming, climate action must double down but also go beyond emissions cuts to prioritize adaptation, respond to loss and damage, and confront growing inequality.

For decades, climate policy has been dominated by a central question: How do we avoid exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius of global warming above preindustrial levels? An overshoot world—where warming exceeds 1.5 degrees Celsius before potentially declining—means more than higher temperatures; it means escalating risks to the communities, ecosystems, and species already bearing the brunt of climate change, with catastrophic consequences for many coastal regions and small island states.

The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) report on climate overshoot challenges us to confront a reality that many policymakers have been reluctant to fully acknowledge: The world will exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius. Decades of insufficient climate action have placed us in this predicament.

Aggressive emissions reductions can limit how far and for how long global warming exceeds that threshold. However, a future decline in global temperatures would not erase the losses experienced along the way. That reality should sharpen global climate ambition and broaden our understanding of what climate action requires. It is equally important to prepare for and respond to the escalating impacts that communities and ecosystems will experience in the decades between crossing 1.5 degrees Celsius and hopefully returning to a temperature below it.

One of the UNEP report’s central messages is that mitigation and adaptation are fundamentally intertwined, and both will be essential in navigating an overshoot world. The report also emphasizes the growing need for transformational adaptation: actions that go beyond incremental adjustments to fundamentally reshape systems, institutions, and development pathways in response to escalating climate risks.

Yet there is an alarming disconnect between the scale of adaptation that is needed and the reality on the ground. Many countries are still struggling to finance and implement basic adaptation measures. Adaptation finance remains grossly inadequate, national adaptation plans are underfunded, and frontline communities continue to lack the resources needed to prepare for worsening risks. The Adaptation Gap Report 2025 estimated adaptation finance needs in developing countries at up to US$365 billion per year by 2035, while international adaptation finance flows from developed countries reached just US$35 billion in 2024, only around 10 percent of what is needed.

There is reason for optimism. After decades of advocacy, adaptation is finally receiving greater recognition as a core pillar of climate action. Negotiations on the global goal on adaptation (GGA)—which aims to enhance adaptive capacity, strengthen resilience, and reduce vulnerability—have elevated adaptation within international climate discussions. Initiatives such as the Fostering Investable National Implementation (FINI) are also helping to mobilize public and private investments to scale adaptation efforts.

But overshoot forces us to think beyond closing today’s adaptation gaps.

What happens when livelihoods—particularly those connected to our food system—become untenable because of chronic drought, extreme heat, or repeated disasters? What happens when infrastructure designed for one climate must function in a dramatically different one; when transit and energy hubs become inoperable due to heat? What happens when fundamental changes in land use become unavoidable, and our forests and farmlands can’t keep up with those changes?

“The challenge is not deciding which type of climate action deserves support. We need all three: mitigation, adaptation, and responses to loss and damage.”

These are questions of transformation, requiring us to rethink how societies are organized, how development occurs, and how communities can thrive in a warmer world with greater climate impacts. The challenge is that we are asking societies to prepare for transformational adaptation before many governments have fully integrated adaptation into planning, investment, and governance.

Governments and funders—both public and private—must therefore treat adaptation as an investment in economic stability, public health, food security, water security, and human dignity, not as a secondary environmental issue. Support must be predictable, accessible, timely, and responsive to local priorities. Researchers, practitioners, governments, businesses, and communities must work together to envision, plan for, and implement systemic change, from rethinking infrastructure and land use to reshaping governance, livelihoods, and development pathways.

At COP31, progress on the GGA must be a priority. Countries must move beyond the stalled discussions to reach agreement on how adaptation progress will be measured and how implementation will be accelerated. Discussions on preparing for the next global stocktake must also grapple more explicitly with the implications of overshoot for adaptation and adaptation finance.

One of the most important implications of overshoot is that there are climate impacts that are unpreventable and irreversible. Some impacts exceed the capacity of households, institutions, and ecosystems to adjust, and others occur despite preparation. When communities lose lands to sea level rise, when repeated disasters force permanent displacement, when cultural heritage disappears, or when ecosystems collapse, we are no longer talking only about adaptation. We are talking about loss and damage.

Countries and communities need support for recovery and reconstruction, assistance with displacement and planned relocation, protection of livelihoods and cultural heritage, and solutions for slow-onset impacts such as sea level rise, desertification, and glacier loss. The scale of these needs is already staggering. Estimates find that loss and damage costs in developing countries could reach up to US$671 billion annually by 2030, and those figures only capture economic losses. They do not account for noneconomic losses, such as lives lost, cultural heritage erased, or permanent damage to ecosystems.

Yet climate policy discussions continue to focus primarily on the relationship between mitigation and adaptation. In an overshoot world, that is no longer enough. Responses to loss and damage must become a central component of climate action. The establishment of the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD) was an important step, but its success will ultimately depend on whether it mobilizes finance at the scale required and whether support reaches affected people quickly, directly, and equitably.

Loss and damage are also inseparable from questions of justice. The countries and communities contributing the least to climate change are those facing the greatest risks of irreversible harm. Small Island Developing States, Indigenous Peoples, women, low-income communities, and many countries across the Global South continue to bear disproportionate impacts despite contributing little to the problem.

Climate change has always raised questions about who bears the costs, who receives protection, and who gets left behind. Overshoot makes those questions even more urgent. If we are serious about navigating an overshoot world, loss and damage can no longer remain at the margins of climate policy. It must become a core pillar of climate action.

At COP31, loss and damage must be integrated across the full climate agenda rather than confined to a stand-alone negotiation track. Parties should move beyond procedural discussions and focus on delivering finance through the FRLD and other loss and damage funding arrangements at the scale required, with resources flowing quickly from pledges to actual disbursement. Access to support must become faster, more predictable, and responsive to the needs of affected communities. Discussions under the next global stocktake should also place loss and damage at the center of assessing collective progress, recognizing that overshoot will bring unavoidable and irreversible impacts that demand dedicated responses. Doing so will require rethinking current international approaches to climate finance and accountability, ensuring that support for loss and damage is treated as a core pillar of climate action alongside mitigation and adaptation.

One of the biggest barriers to addressing overshoot is the current approach to competing climate action. Too often, climate discussions become debates about mitigation versus adaptation and adaptation versus loss and damage. Overshoot makes it clear that this is the wrong conversation.

The challenge is not deciding which type of climate action deserves support. We need all three: mitigation, adaptation, and responses to loss and damage. These are mutually reinforcing responsibilities, not competitors. For climate finance, the problem is not only how we divide the pie; the problem is that the pie itself remains sorely inadequate for the challenges ahead of us. An overshoot world requires a dramatic expansion of overall climate finance that must be equitably distributed to mitigation, adaptation, and responses to loss and damage.

The findings from the UNEP report should serve as a blaring alarm—not only because overshoot is a reality, but because it forces us to confront whether our current systems, institutions, financing mechanisms, and political processes are capable of delivering the transformation required. An overshoot world is fundamentally about people: whether societies can adapt, how we respond to loss and damage, and whether the greatest burdens continue to fall on those who contributed the least to the problem.In an era of overshoot, our attention, ambition, and investment must finally match that reality.

Children run outside of Ojo Sarco, New Mexico’s one-room schoolhouse in a photo from January 1943. The structure still stands today as the Ojo Sarco Community Center. Credit: John Collier, Jr./Library of Congress

“We have no political voice, really,” said Carol Miller, a grant writer and board member for the Ojo Sarco Community Center. “We’re pretty neglected. We’ve lost resources in the past. We were the first wave of people to lose our post office many years ago. Everybody has to commute pretty much for everything.”

That commute is about 40 minutes round trip for basic amenities, she said.

The community center has tried to fill in those resource gaps with services like a twice-monthly food bank, summer meal programs for kids, safety classes and outings for its residents, such as a night at the opera. The OSCC also recently met all the qualifications to be a state library.

PPC Solar, also known as Paradise Power Company, from Taos, built a 7.5-kW solar project paired with 10.6-kWh of LFP energy storage at the OSCC. The contractor built the array at no-cost to the community center. Every year PPC donates a solar array to a regional nonprofit organization, and after three years of applying, the OSCC was awarded the project.

CEO Daniel Weinman’s father founded Paradise Power Company in the 1970s, and the company has installed solar in off-the-grid communities since the start. Its PV donation program is built out of that tradition.

“We’ve always wanted to find a way to give back to the community,” Weinman said. “The best thing that we found — and we’ve been doing it for over a decade now — is doing an annual nonprofit donation system … We’ve had farming organizations, land conservation organizations, and it goes on and on. Really, it’s about finding the right fit. We wanted to make a good impact for this community center, and we felt resiliency was the best way to do that.”

Solar Power World spoke to both PPC Solar and organizers with the OSCC about what effect this solar project has had in the village since it was built in fall 2025.

Larry Miller, project manager and OSCC board member: I think it’s a transformative project for our small community. We like to talk about the history of the community center, and now this is part of it. If someone doesn’t notice the solar panels on the roof, we point it out to them. It’s integrated into our lives here.

Carol Miller: It’s lowered our electric costs, which is really important for us, because we’re such a shoe-string operation. All the kids that come to our library programs, they’re solar oriented. The benefit of this company and its staff just has ripple effects throughout our greater community.

#5969. Stratos Data Center Will Emit Air Pollution That’s Linked to Premature Deaths and Health Economic Damages for Utah & States Downwind

A new report models emissions from different on-site methane generation power scenarios to show potential impacts on the people of Utah and residents of multiple downwind states.

Canadian businessman and television personality “Mr. Wonderful” (aka Kevin O’Leary) and his businesses have proposed the Stratos data center in Box Elder County, Utah, near Salt Lake City, that  will be one of the largest data centers in the world, requiring as much as 9 gigawatts (GW) of on-site power from burning methane gas. The Box Elder County Commission approved the project in May—under highly contentious circumstances and under a highly unusual arrangement within a project area created by Utah’s Military Installation Development Authority.

A new report prepared by EmPower Analytics Group for NRDC estimates that the air pollution, health harms, and economic damages from the proposed Stratos data center would be substantial: “[a]t the potential 9 GW full build-out, air pollution from Stratos’s methane-gas power generation is estimated to cause approximately 5–45 additional premature deaths and $75–$690 million in health-related economic damages every year.” The report also discusses additional health harms that extend beyond premature deaths and include new asthma cases, nonfatal heart attacks, hospital visits, and lost work and school days.

These ranges reflect scenarios that model the health harms and economic damage caused by air pollution from two possible methane-gas primary power generating technologies: modern combined-cycle combustion turbines and methane gas–fired reciprocating internal combustion engines (RICE). The primary harmful air pollution consists of tiny soot particles—particulate matter of 2.5 microns or less (PM2.5)—about 30 times smaller than the width of an average human hair.

The report further finds that over a 30-year period, at the potential 9 GW full build-out, the health and economic harms from this PM2.5 air pollution “could accumulate to approximately 856–1,350 premature deaths and $13.5–$20.7 billion under gas-fired RICE, compared with 145–253 premature deaths and $2.3–$3.8 billion under combined-cycle gas.”

Of the two methane-gas primary power generation technologies evaluated in the report, one uses a smaller number of large gas combustion turbines, while the RICE scenario reflected electricity generated by many individual engines operating in parallel. Indeed, the RICE scenario is patterned on a methane gas–powered data center with 69 RICE generators that the Utah Division of Air Quality permitted in Millard County in early 2026. RICE generators produce higher emissions than combined-cycle gas at the same generation capacity. Accordingly, the report finds that “[t]he technology used to power Stratos could make a five- to sixfold difference in its estimated health harms.”

Importantly, the modeled combined-cycle gas scenario does not estimate the emissions from the use of on-site diesel generators for backup power because those plans by Stratos remain unknown. (The report modeled diesel backup generators for the RICE scenario, scaled from the Millard County permit example.) Diesel generators are significantly dirtier than comparably sized methane gas generators, emitting 95 percent more smog-forming nitrogen oxides than an equivalent gas generator as well as 98 percent more non-methane hydrocarbons and 59 percent more carbon monoxide. Harmful PM2.5 emissions will be even higher from any data center employing diesel generators (the scale of that additional pollution would depend on whether they were truly used for “emergency conditions” versus being run more frequently; state permitting parameters for backup diesels is a critical issue for all data center developments).

The highest concentrations of harmful air pollution from the Stratos data center will occur near the facility in Box Elder County, neighboring areas of northern Utah (including Salt Lake City), and southern Idaho. But the health harms and economic damage from Stratos would extend well beyond these areas: The report finds that more than 80 percent of health harms and health-related economic damages occur outside the state, reaching downwind populations in Colorado, Wyoming, and as far away as Illinois. The report appendix identifies the full list of potentially impacted states.

The Stratos developers have maintained from the start that the massive data center will be powered by methane gas. “That’s never going to happen,” Utah Governor Spencer Cox told the Salt Lake Tribune in May. “The very first phase will be natural gas, but the other phases should not be. They should be nuclear, and they should be geothermal, and solar and other technology.” But the most recent filing by the Stratos developers with the state still show plans for the full build-out of the data center to be powered by methane gas. This filing shows that the developers now say that they plan a 7.5 GW data center using the dirtier and more harmful RICE generators that burn methane gas. None of these filings commit the developers to doing or not doing something different from what they are saying now, a reality that has characterized this data center project—and many other proposed data center projects nationally.

Data centers in Utah or anywhere else, of course, need not be powered by highly polluting, harmful fossil fuels like methane gas. Other data center developers have been doing better jobs showing how, in stark contrast to the Stratos proposal, it is possible to power these facilities with cleaner energy—such as a robust portfolio of wind, solar, geothermal, and batteries—and maxing out demand side resources.

NRDC commissioned this important EmPower Analytics report to raise awareness and concerns about the harmful health impacts and associated economic damages from dangerous air pollution caused by data centers like the proposed Stratos Project in Utah and by the proliferation of data centers across the United States. These harms arise especially from hyperscaler data centers powered by fossil fuels, like Stratos. While the dangers and costs of excessive water use by data centers are more well known, the deadly toll of air pollution—a silent killer—deserves greater attention and concern.

Harmful air pollution should be met with the best air pollution control technologies available. Data centers should be powered by new, additional, deliverable clean energy that does not release air pollution, along with batteries, storage, and other smart solutions. If someone nicknamed Mr. Wonderful wants to earn that name, he should live up to it, starting with the Stratos data center.

#5965. China Truck Sales Now Majority Electric. What’s Next?

EV heavy trucks hit 50.3% market share in China for September, with sales up 34% from a year ago. Long-haul big rig market share hit 64.5%. China is on track to have over 40% EV market share for the entire year, reaching their 2030 goal in a matter of months. This is not the first time China’s heavy truck market share topped 50%, but that previous blip in December last year was prior to subsidies ending, followed by a dip in January. Current sales rates are not bolstered by the subsidy and are now on a much more stable upward trajectory.

The speed of EV adoption for heavy trucks in China has recently grown much faster than for cars, having risen from just 13.6% in 2024. When I visited Guangzhou in November of last year and again in April of this year, I noticed a lot more green plates on trucks, indicating that they were electric. It seems that my perception is backed by sales data.

With the addition of LNG trucks, diesel’s share of heavy trucks has dropped to roughly 40%. While LNG sales have also declined with the rate of EV adoption, diesel trucks could soon make up less than 1/3 of sales and decline further from there. China could feasibly double its 2030 goal and hit 80% EV penetration, by which point many diesel models are likely to be discontinued.

With these levels of market penetration, in addition to retirements, China’s diesel demand is on track to fall by about 230,000 barrels per day this year, despite increased freight movement. Every additional electric truck that replaces a diesel truck then builds on that reduction. Due to their duty cycle, each electric truck sold has a much greater impact on fuel consumption than each car sold. And the price of fuel combined with the duty cycle has a much greater impact on overall truck economics.

To be clear, these improving results are not due to subsidies, as they appear in the year after subsidies were cut. They are also not due to mandates, as the 2030 goals were met before the ink was dry on their announcement. EV trucks are now more affordable to own and operate than diesel trucks in China. The increase in diesel prices has only increased the cost gap.

Last year, roughly 90% of all electric truck sales globally were in China. That percentage is set to expand this year, despite rising sales in some other countries. Those sales are simply not rising fast enough outside of China to keep pace with China. In comparison, the EU is looking at 2.5% EV market share this year. China is on pace to average roughly as many electric trucks sold per week as Europe sells per year. The US EV truck market looks like a rounding error in comparison at less than one percent.

In addition, China has rapidly been building out heavy truck charging infrastructure as part of the 28 million charging facilities planned to be in operation by the end of 2027, up from 21 million last March. This not only focuses on the major cities but also extends into more rural areas that have been slower to adopt electrification. In addition, several companies are building out battery swap stations. While we have yet to see if battery swap or MW+ charging wins in the truck market, expect the competition between the two approaches to be fierce.

Within Tier 1 cities, I could see a potential phase out of diesel trucks in the near future. Hainan province is already planning to end ICE passenger car sales by 2030, and I expect that to extend to diesel trucks as well. Although rural areas have been slower to adopt, they also tend to be less wealthy and thus more impacted by the changing economics. If diesel prices stay elevated while charging becomes convenient, many rural businesses will not be able to afford to buy new diesel trucks. With the end of diesel truck sales, strict emissions standards and a potential return of scrappage incentives could take remaining diesel vehicles off the road at an accelerated rate.

With fewer diesel trucks on the road in the largest market globally, diesel consumption is set to decline. In addition, the supply chain for diesel parts will also start to decline. As protectionism has targeted electric vehicles, rather than internal combustion  engine (ICE) vehicles, a decline in Chinese (ICE) parts has a potentially significant impact on global markets.

Of course, as EVs take over the majority of heavy truck sales in the fiercely competitive Chinese market, Chinese manufacturers will start looking outside of the country for growth.

The traditional thought would be to look at wealthy Western countries that have substantial buying power. However, many Chinese companies may be a bit reluctant to focus on those markets after the protectionist efforts against electric passenger vehicles. Many European markets, like Germany, also have very high electricity prices, which makes the financial proposition less attractive for commercial vehicles that are heavily motivated by costs. Chinese companies may start focusing elsewhere.

The Middle Corridor could become a focus. This essentially rebuilds the Silk Road land route from China, across the Caspian Sea, on to Turkey. Many of the countries along this route  once had prosperous trade, only to fall into decline when ocean trade routes were established. However, land transport is much easier to electrify than long ocean voyages. China is investing heavily with countries like Kazakhstan along the route with renewable electricity infrastructure. Electrified rail is expanding by 26-fold in Turkey. Electrified trucking is the next logical step, connecting the rail lines to more local production along the route.

Other Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) countries could also benefit. In countries like Ethiopia, which has expensive diesel but electricity prices to businesses of just $0.017/kWh, electric trucks could make perfect sense to transport between new factories and new ports. Trading partners like Vietnam and Thailand could also see an influx.

All this is not to say that local companies will not benefit. Unlike passenger cars, heavy equipment tends to be more modular. Due to the bulk and mass of the vehicles, final assembly is more likely to be localized, with greater use of local suppliers.

Overall, we could see much of the “Rest of the World” (ROW) leapfrog legacy economies in EV truck adoption. The purchase price is no longer prohibitive in China, and the operating costs are much lower. With infrastructure buildout and increasing renewable installed capacity, electric trucking is poised to take over rapidly.

That is not to say that the West will be completely left out. Countries with less prohibitive trade policies toward EVs will likely see increased adoption. While EVs have taken over passenger car sales in Norway, their trucking industry still has a way to go at just 14.4%. And the geographically most eastern country typically lumped in with the “West,” Australia, is likely to see EV truck sales follow a similar pattern to EV car sales. If Canada continues to open up, they could also benefit. Of course, as the majority of the economic benefit from electric trucks comes from their use, inflated trucking costs hurt every company that moves products by truck, and those inflated costs then lead to inflation throughout the economy. That impacts overall affordability. As such, there is a chance that protectionists do not put as much effort into undermining electric truck imports as they put into passenger EVs.

And the EV heavy truck global supply chain will become increasingly well established. This could mean more parts availability for brands outside of China to build competitive vehicles, if those companies have access to global supply chains. Competitive markets will also drive innovation, moving the technology forward at an accelerating rate. Meanwhile the supply chain for diesel trucks will shrink.

While the US still seems to be figuring out how to make a safe EV Semi, battery-powered heavy trucks are becoming increasingly mature products elsewhere. As electric trucks move from majority share to complete market dominance in the largest market, other markets will follow, and the transition will become inevitable. Hopefully, the largest oil producing country in the world will not be left too far behind.

#5960. How Does Oahu Get to an Economically Resilient, 100% Clean Energy Future?

Oahu is a unique place. It’s the home of one of the largest clean tech incubators in the world, Elemental Impact, for a reason. Exorbitantly high electricity rates combined with ample natural resources make it an incredible laboratory to bring clean tech solutions to market. Rapid investment for WWII created a grid that was dependent on foreign oil. Path dependency (the idea that “what you know” becomes what you continue to do) hampered innovation and resulted in the creation of infrastructure (multiple power plants across multiple islands) that still, today, burns diesel fuel as large generators, to produce electricity for the island. Throw in a dependence on imported fuels that must come at least 3,000 miles to arrive, being the strategic hub for the entire Pacific theater of the U.S. military, one of the wildest weather years and hurricane seasons the island chain has ever seen AND the state’s economic dependency on tourism dollars, high cost of living, and low wages, and and wow – you’ve got yourself a puzzle and a half to solve in order to get the place to a sustainable future.

Analyst Mike Barnard loves a challenge, so he set forth to put together a roadmap to help Oahu get to 100% clean energy, which helps recirculate money in the state, lower costs, create jobs and foster economic resiliency.

Recently I gave a talk at Hawaii Tech Week in which I summarized this report, added in my own experience running an energy efficiency service that has now greened more than 20,000 homes and is saving Hawaii residents over $10 million a year on their utility bills, and then compiled the new and coming technologies that can help the island get off fossil fuels.

Mike’s report did something very important. The state’s own reporting and strategy looks at the big picture, a very difficult mountain to climb. The result was that it was achievable, and there are technologies right now today that can be deployed in what he refers to as a “no regret stack” first and foremost.

I combined that with my approach, which was to showcase the economic resilience it creates. Case in point, an LED bulb saves so much money on Oahu that it pays for itself in 18 days. Every day after that, it is like change plinking in a piggy bank for the person who put it in. That builds economic resiliency for those who need it most. From LEDs to e-bikes to heat pump water heaters to balcony solar – it all adds up – fast payback periods, and financial independence.

Mike comes back to the things we simply can’t fix right now and argues that the tech will come – but for the duration of the 2020s and into the 2030s, we should be installing parking lot solar canopies, agrivoltaics, heat pump water heaters, and more.

#5922. “War” Secretary Pete Hegseth Endorses 15 Minute City Model

His newly announced “Fortress America” military resilience plan includes a new vision for military bases that embraces walkability and quality of life. If that sounds like the 15 Minute City plan in action, it is. Oh, lordy…

Walkable communities are as old as the hills. That’s what people did before automobiles hit the scene and cities were organized around cars. Credit for applying the walkability model to 21st century urban planning goes to the Franco-Colombian scientist Carlos Moreno, who formalized the 15 Minute City concept in 2016. The idea got a further boost of attention when the Mayor of Paris, Anne Hidalgo, pitched walkable, bike-able neighborhoods as part of her successful re-election campaign in 2020.

The COVID-19 lockdown of 2020 was also a pivotal moment, with a surge in work-from-home households drawing attention to the need for local resources as a matter of survival and resiliency in case of a widespread emergency.

The 15 Minute City model covers a lot of ground, but the basic idea is that people should be able to do their shopping, recreation, socializing, and other essential routines within walking or biking distance from their homes, making cars largely optional within the neighborhood. Walkable access to schools and health care is also part of the plan.

The argument for 15-minute cities (also called 15-minute neighborhoods or 15-minute communities) is also pretty straightforward. Walking is healthier than sitting in a car. Avoiding injury or death from car accidents is another plus. Walking also reduces carbon emissions, and it saves money on gas.

In addition, walkability levels the playing field, so to speak. Everyone in the 15-minute zone has walkable access to basic resources, regardless of whether or not they can afford a car. In other words, there is a significant equity and inclusion element to the concept…

…and, that’s where the trouble kicks in. In 2022 the World Economic Forum posted an article by Lisa Chamberlain, the founder of the urban communications and business firm Strategies for City Builders. Chamberlain describes a 15 Minute City as characterized by diversified, mixed-use neighborhoods built to withstand shocks like COVID-19 and climate change.

“As climate change and global conflict cause shocks and stresses at faster intervals and increasing severity, the 15-minute city will become even more critical,” Chamberlain emphasized, with a further emphasis on equity and inclusion:

“Specifically, communities that foster and maintain social and economic relationships don’t have to be wealthy, but they do need to be walkable and safe, with both residential and commercial buildings intact. And, I would add, for 15-Minute Cities to thrive, not just survive crises—and this cannot be stressed enough—they must also have plenty of mixed-income and equitable housing, as well as digital access.”

“To some, it’s an urban planning concept that promotes sustainable and healthy living.

“To a small group of others, it’s a plot by ‘tyrannical bureaucrats’ to take our cars and control our lives, which could lead to a real-life Hunger Games scenario.”

“Limiting cars and promoting pedestrian or bike access is framed as a slippery slope to government-run, open-air prisons,” ABC continued.

Yikes! ABC also cites the Canadian psychologist Jordan Peterson. who railed against “idiot tyrannical bureaucrats” who “decide by fiat where you’re ‘allowed’ to drive…and, make no mistake, it’s part of a well-documented plan” (emphasis added).

Ohfergawdsakes, of course there is a well documented plan, and it’s called urban planning. And, that’s where the US Department of Defense comes in.

Stripping away Secretary Hegseth’s testosterone-fueled purple prose in the September 30 announcement, FORTRESS AMERICA applies the 15 Minute City urban planning model to major Defense Department bases in the US, ensuring they can operate independently of civilian infrastructure for a sustained period of time. That includes everything needed to support community life in addition to the supplies needed to sustain military operations.

FORTRESS America even goes beyond the initial 15 Minute City concept. As envisioned under the “Next Great American Base” element of the program, a central feature of the plan consists of access to a reliable source of on-site energy.

Don’t just take my word for it. “The program aligns basing and infrastructure projects to ensure they are collectively advancing toward greater independence,” Hegseth stated in the official order, with independence referring to the capability to sustain operations within a facility that is cut off from civilian resources. After all, if speed-to-power is the name, solar and batteries are the game…

#5918. Nabrawind’s Crane-Free Wind Towers Face a Supplier Test

Spain’s Nabrawind has developed a way to install exceptionally tall wind turbine towers without bringing an enormous crawler crane to the site. Its Nabralift system uses a tubular upper tower supported by a three-legged steel structure. Hydraulic equipment raises the assembled turbine while modular sections are inserted below it near ground level. The lifting frame can then be removed and used on another installation. This addresses genuine logistical and construction problems, particularly at remote sites where specialist cranes are expensive to transport and where wind can interrupt conventional lifting operations.

The company has progressed beyond demonstration-scale hardware. In September 2026, UL Solutions issued type certification for Envision’s 7.8 MW turbine on Nabrawind’s 188-meter tower. Fortescue is using 17 of those tower configurations at the 133 MW Nullagine wind project in Western Australia’s Pilbara region, where construction is underway. Nabrawind previously erected a 160-meter prototype in Spain in under two weeks and has supplied smaller commercial towers in Morocco and France.

The engineering benefits are credible. Nabrawind reports using roughly 20% less tower steel than comparable conventional tubular designs and reducing foundation concrete by as much as 80%, depending on the design and site. Those are supplier claims rather than independently established savings across all wind farms. The potential to avoid large cranes, simplify transport and raise turbines in windier conditions could also matter substantially to total installed cost and construction schedules. High hub heights only earn their keep when those costs are justified by the electricity generated over the project’s life.

The current deployment has an unusual commercial structure. Fortescue acquired Nabrawind in September 2025 and is building Nullagine to serve its own mining operations. Fortescue has the capital, internal electricity demand and industrial incentive to support a technology it owns. Successful installation in that context would be important evidence of construction performance, but it would not establish that an unrelated wind developer can finance and maintain the same system under ordinary third-party commercial arrangements.

Nabralift’s proprietary construction approach creates a procurement issue. Conventional tubular towers can be sourced from several qualified manufacturers, and experienced contractors can compete for much of their construction work. A developer selecting Nabralift currently depends on one supplier for the core tower system, specialist lifting knowledge and associated support. Wind turbine original-equipment manufacturers also create supplier dependencies, but concentrating another critical part of the project in a proprietary installation system requires its own contract and risk assessment.

A type certificate provides valuable technical assurance about the certified turbine-and-tower configuration. #5904. Who Controls the Battery in Your Driveway?

Tesla brought home power backup to America. In Australia, an update is switching it off. North American owners just got Powershare with Powerwall. Australian owners of existing Teslas got a software update that appears to block the third-party gear they use to power their homes. It is a test case for who really controls the battery in your driveway.

Mark Purcell is an electrical engineer and energy advocate based in Noosa Heads, Queensland, Australia. He owns two Teslas and a bidirectional DC charger, and has been a member of the Australian Electric Vehicle Association since 2022. He has contributed content to CleanTechnica previously.

On 12 October, my Model Y ran my house. Through a Sigenergy bidirectional DC charger in my garage on Australia’s Sunshine Coast, it supplied about 25 kW to my home. The battery in my driveway holds several times the energy of a typical home battery, and I have already paid for it.

That may not last. Owners in several countries report that Tesla software from the 2026.38 branch, rolling out since October, cuts third-party discharge within minutes. On the Tesla Motors Club forum, owners describe power stopping after about three minutes with a battery management alert, or after 15 to 20 minutes whatever the load. The old Camp Mode and Service Mode workarounds no longer help. Drive Tesla Canada has covered the reports. EleJoy, Tlyard and Sigenergy users are all affected, and Tlyard now warns customers that Tesla’s 2026 software detects “simulated charging” and may cut the power link.

The release notes? “Security improvements” and minor fixes. Nothing about switching off a function owners use every day.

Here is the part that should interest readers everywhere. The same 2026.38 branch delivered Powershare with Powerwall home backup to owners in the US and Canada. That is a good thing. Tesla has finally joined Ford, GM, and Kia in letting the car back up the house.

Australia did not get it. Tesla Australia has said Powershare cannot be retrofitted to existing vehicles, and here Powershare is vehicle-to-load only. So, for every existing Tesla on Australian roads, third-party equipment is the only way to put the car to work for the home. If the software now blocks that equipment, Australian owners are left with nothing: not Tesla’s solution, and not anyone else’s.

The Tesla Australia owner’s manual says: “Do not use the Battery as a stationary power source. Doing so voids the warranty.” Tesla’s own Australian warranty page is narrower. It excludes damage caused by third-party adaptors or chargers, not the whole warranty.

Australian law is narrower again. Under the Australian Consumer Law, statutory consumer guarantees cannot be signed away by a line in a manual. Apple found this out when the Federal Court ordered A$9 million in penalties over the “Error 53” affair, after Apple told customers that third-party repairs had cost them their rights. American readers will recognise the shape of this argument from the right-to-repair fight.

Australia is a world leader in rooftop solar and is now subsidising home batteries at scale. The next step is obvious: the biggest battery most households will ever own is already parked outside. Bidirectional charging is how that capacity reaches the home and, in time, the grid.

That whole idea depends on interoperability. If one carmaker can switch off discharge overnight, through an over-the-air update the owner cannot roll back, and without saying so, then every bidirectional charger is a bet on that carmaker’s goodwill. Chargers bought in good faith become stranded. Competing suppliers lose access to the fleet. Grid operators lose flexibility they are counting on.

The ACCC, Australia’s competition and consumer regulator, has already named “denying interoperability” as conduct that can distort competition in digital platform markets. A car that decides which charger it will talk to is a platform in all but name.

I am deferring Tesla updates on both my cars, so I am also missing whatever genuine fixes they contain. I have asked Tesla in writing to hold updates until it confirms discharge will keep working. I have lodged a complaint with the ACCC under Australia’s competition and consumer laws. I have also asked the Australian Electric Vehicle Association, the national voice of EV users, to back it.

#5897. Latin America EV Sales Report: With Over 200,000 Units Sold in Q3, EVs Now Stand at 12% Market Share!

Through 2026, EVs have gained significant momentum all over the world (safe for the US), and Latin America has been no exception. Sales grew by almost 140% in the third quarter, and clock in at a very healthy 120% growth through 2026, bringing the yearly total to more than half a million units. If this trend continues, it’s all but certain we will surpass 700,000 units by the end of the year, and perhaps even get to 800,000.

Another interesting bit of trivia is that sales reached 220,000 units in the third quarter, not too far behind the USA’s 266,000. In theory, this means it should be possible for LatAm to surpass total US EV sales as early as Q1 2027 … though, I would be much happier if the USA’s market starts growing strongly again and thus Latin America cannot get ahead.

Now, to be fair, the US is much more BEV-heavy (242,000 units sold, vs 127,000 in LatAm), whereas Latin America prefers PHEVs to a higher degree (93,000 units sold vs 44,000 in the US), but, still, these are impressive results for a region overall much poorer than the US, and with a vehicle market around a third of the size. For over a decade, it was the richest countries in the world that led EV adoption, but — as many of us predicted on this very site — that has now changed.

As mentioned earlier, total EV sales reached 220,000 units, surpassing for the first time the 200,000 mark and growing a healthy 140% YoY, and a strong 17% from Q2. BEVs grew faster than PHEVs (146% vs 130%), and thus now represent 57.5% of all EV sales in the region, slightly below the 58% result for Q2.

Market share grew to 12%, up from 10.7% in Q2 and 5.5% in Q3 last year, a pretty strong result. This brought market share above 10% through 2026, 5.9% BEV and 4.3% PHEV.

Latin America’s overall vehicle market is growing at rates of around 10% annually, but thanks to strong EV growth, combustion-based powertrains (HEV, MHEV and ICEV) are stagnant: they fell by 3.7% last quarter, and barely grew by 3.1% this one, meaning it’s very likely that we’re reaching “Peak Combustion” in Latin America and the market will only go down from 2027 onwards.

Sales wise, we have a pretty stable list, with Brazil being the unsurprising winner, followed by Mexico and Colombia, just as in Q2. Uruguay follows, but then we have a new rising star: Ecuador, which nearly reached 5,000 BEVs sold last quarter! Costa Rica, Chile, and Argentina close the top 8 and make up all the countries with over 1.000 quarterly BEV sales.

PHEV sales are much more varied, though. Brazil, of course, leads again, but following come Mexico and Argentina, with Colombia in 4th place and Ecuador in 5th.

Market share: again, we have a pretty constant list, with the first 8 positions unchanged. Uruguay, unsurprisingly, leads the region with a very strong 50.7% EV market share (45.7% BEV), followed by Costa Rica, Colombia, Ecuador, and Brazil. Notable mention to Paraguay, the sixth country in our region to reach 10% EV market share through a quarter, but in this case thanks to PHEVs.

This has been an eventful year due to Donald’s dubious defense decisions, but even as high oil prices have been driving massive EV adoption and/or massive ICEV crashes all over the world, I feel the situation in Latin America isn’t as clear cut.

In the US and Europe, fuel prices have gone up by around 40–50%, at least in the case of gasoline. Now, prices have gone up similarly in some countries in Latin America, including Panama, Paraguay and Perú, but in many others, like Brazil, Colombia and Mexico, it has barely moved, either because of significant subsidies — direct or indirect — to maintain prices, or because it was already expensive before the war and the government did not make any adjustments to its price.

One would think that the countries most affected by high fuel prices would be at the forefront of the EV transition, but no: the largest increases in EV sales only partially track gasoline prices. For example: Panama and Perú are still lagging behind the region despite having the highest increase in the region (>50% in both cases), whereas Colombia and Brazil are sprinting ahead despite relatively stable fuel prices. What gives?

The answer, I believe, is linked to EV prices and how they compare with their ICEV competition. Yes, I know the TCO is a very important metric, but evidence in our markets point to the sticker price being the main driver of adoption. The cases of Brazil and Colombia on one hand, and Peru and Panama on the other, exemplify this very well:

Brazil and Colombia have had pretty stable fuel prices, up around 10% or less since the war started, yet both have booming markets, up 160% and 190%, respectively, with EV market share getting close to 20%. Meanwhile, Panama and Peru have seen gasoline prices spike over 50% from their pre-war costs, yet the former only showed a meager 57% growth in September, and the latter, though clocking in at a more impressive 270% YoY, remains at a disappointing 1.3% market share.

The main difference has to do with the nature of the market. In Brazil and Colombia, combustion vehicles are relatively expensive — in no small part due to tariffs to protect local industry — and EVs have effectively reached price parity with their combustion counterparts. Meanwhile, in Panama and Per,u EVs are just as cheap if not cheaper than in Brazil or Colombia, but combustion cars are significantly cheaper, with some entry-level models at or below USD$10.,00. This makes customers hesitant to try the new technologies by the simple logic that even if the EV will save a lot of money in the long run, $6,000 will buy a lot of gas.

And in Peru, this is particularly relevant insofar as gas-mobiles from Chinese brands are very popular. Changan, Jetour, Geely, and JAC together account for 20% of the Peruvian market largely thanks to their hyper-affordable combustion cars, and since the Chinese gas-mobile market is collapsing, it’s likely these brands would rather keep it like this for a few more months so they can move some of the inventory they won’t be able to sell at home before betting it all on EVs.

In general, this tracks for most markets, where EV prices compared to traditional combustion cars are probably the most relevant factor to keep track of. And of course, fuel prices do have an influence, but it seems it’s rather limited compared to, for example, what we’re seeing in Europe.

But the lessons are clear: Want to improve EV sales? Either make them cheaper, or make gas-mobiles more expensive.

At the beginning of this year, I was hopeful we would see 10% market share in the fourth quarter, normally the highest in the year. We went past that in Q2, and at 12%, we’re sitting far beyond what I considered feasible only 10 months ago.

Yet, there’s still a long path ahead. Aside from the 5 leading markets, we’re yet to see a country getting consistent BEV market share above 5%, and several large markets — Chile, Argentina, and Mexico — are still lagging. PHEVs have raised the bar in several countries, Paraguay most important of all, but we would rather see more BEVs.

This is not to be overtly pessimistic. The results so far are great, much better than we would’ve expected even a year ago. But as things improve, the bar rises, and I’m now waiting to see the moment when we will see 20% market share (Q3 2027?) or when e-truck sales will start to ramp up (2027? 2028?).

#5889. Data centers don’t need special treatment to get power faster

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service, writes Seyed Madaeni, CEO and co-founder of Verse.

Seyed Madaeni is CEO and co-founder of Verse, an energy software company specializing in battery dispatch optimization for data centers.

Even the best-funded project can’t operate without electricity, and the grid often can’t deliver hundreds of new megawatts as quickly as AI developers need them. Access to power has become a competitive advantage, and companies are searching for ways to get it faster.

Today, developers see two obvious options. They can wait years for traditional grid upgrades, or they can build private power plants onsite. The first can move too slowly. The second brings fuel-supply, equipment, permitting and community challenges of its own.

A third path to data center interconnection is taking shape. Across the country, utilities and grid operators are creating clearer ways for large loads that can reduce the risks they bring to the system. Depending on the market, a data center might guarantee a minimum payment, agree to reduce its grid demand when the system is strained, provide better operating data or contract for new generation and storage.

These policies differ, but they share one idea: If a data center wants faster or more certain access to power, it should give the grid greater certainty in return.

That is a better approach than advancing projects mainly because they promise jobs or investment. Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service.

Utilities often need to invest in substations, transmission equipment and other infrastructure years before a data center reaches full operation. They are building against forecasts that can change, and their queues may include projects that never get built.

Another utility might choose a different percentage or structure. But a company asking the grid to reserve an extraordinary amount of power should be willing to stand behind that request financially. This protects existing customers from paying for infrastructure built around demand that does not materialize. It also helps serious projects distinguish themselves from speculative ones.

Other markets are focused on how data centers behave once they connect.

A large facility can draw hundreds of megawatts, change its consumption quickly or disconnect during a grid disturbance. At that scale, it’s part of how the power system operates, whether it considers itself a grid participant or not.

Texas is responding with clearer technical and operating expectations. A facility that meets the Electric Reliability Council of Texas’ ride-through requirements clears one important gate on the path to energization, alongside required modeling, stability and interconnection reviews. Through controllable-load and bring-your-own-generation pathways in ERCOT's new Batch Zero process, developers can elect dispatchable curtailment under ERCOT’s real-time control in exchange for earlier energization.

Meaning, a data center must be able to reduce what it takes from the grid when the system is strained. It could curtail consumption, switch to onsite resources or coordinate several assets. What matters is that the response is available when promised.

Southwest Power Pool is making a similar trade through its High Impact Large Load framework. Developers that accept real-time forecast reporting, ramp-rate limits and remote-disconnect capability can have their interconnection study completed within 90 days, clearing the way for an agreement soon afterward.

In both markets, developers give the grid better visibility and more control during constrained periods. In return, they receive a faster and more predictable process.

PJM Interconnection is approaching the challenge through resource adequacy that prioritizes whether the region has enough capacity to meet demand when the system is under the most stress.

Under PJM’s proposed Interim Resource Adequacy Service framework, certain new large loads could connect even when the region doesn’t have enough capacity to serve all forecast demand at its traditional reliability standard. Loads that didn't bring qualifying new capacity or receive an applicable capacity allocation could have been required to reduce their grid consumption during limited emergency conditions. FERC rejected most of the framework in October on jurisdictional grounds, but it encouraged states to decide which retail customers are curtailed when the system is stressed, which keeps the same trade on the table.

A data center could reduce that exposure by bringing qualifying generation, storage or demand-side resources. Otherwise, it would accept service that is less firm than what existing customers receive.

Bringing new capacity does not make transmission and distribution constraints disappear. A project may still need local upgrades, engineering studies and regulatory approvals. But if the system is short on capacity, a new load should either help fill the gap or be prepared to reduce its demand when the shortage becomes critical.

Different rules, one underlying question.

Each market is responding to its own constraints, but the question at the heart of these efforts is the same: What can a data center guarantee in exchange for access to power?

Federal regulators are now pushing that question across the country. In June, FERC directed all six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify their existing large load tariffs or propose reforms. States are moving in parallel through large load tariffs and other requirements intended to ensure that data centers pay their share and manage their impact on the grid.

Today, developers learn the answer after spending time and money evaluating a site. Requirements can differ significantly from one utility or region to the next, making it difficult to compare locations, price the obligations attached to service and design the right energy infrastructure from the beginning.

Utilities and grid operators can make this process clearer. A developer should understand early on how specific commitments affect access to power.

The answer isn’t one national tariff or one required technology. Every grid has different needs, and data centers can provide flexibility through batteries, onsite generation, workload management or a combination of resources.

Utilities and grid operators should define a clear menu of performance-based pathways. Developers should know what they can commit, what the grid will require and what faster or more certain service they can receive in return.

Clearer options will help utilities separate real projects from speculative requests, allow developers to understand the cost of speed before committing to a site and give regulators a stronger basis for determining whether an arrangement protects reliability and existing customers.

The fastest data centers will not necessarily be the ones that find a way around the grid. They will be the ones that give the grid enough confidence to move forward with them.

#5884. Electric Buses Need a Plan for the Next Operating Contract

Greater Manchester is entering a new stage of bus franchising. In September 2026, Go-Ahead announced that Go North West had won two Bee Network operating contracts covering 38 routes and approximately 60 buses, with service beginning in March 2027. Eighteen of those routes will operate from a new depot in Leigh. The award is a reminder that operating companies can change while the depots, charging infrastructure and grid connections supporting the passenger network remain in place for decades.

Electric-bus procurement adds a long-lived physical commitment to contracts that are often much shorter. An operating agreement may run for five to seven years. A depot building, substation or multi-megawatt electricity connection can remain useful through several such contracts. Buses can sometimes be transferred between operators, but land, cable routes, electrical capacity and installed charging equipment are tied to particular sites. Those assets need to support the passenger service even when the company responsible for driving the buses changes.

Consider an authority retendering electric-bus routes. The incumbent has used a convenient depot and financed chargers and a substantial grid connection. A competing operator submits an attractive price and a credible operating plan, but has no assured right to use the depot and electricity infrastructure. It might need to negotiate with the incumbent, find alternative premises or secure another grid connection, potentially adding years of work and substantial costs. A nominally competitive tender can become much less contestable once those obligations are included.

South Yorkshire has taken a deliberate approach to this problem. In March 2026, the South Yorkshire Mayoral Combined Authority announced the purchase of five major depots, adding them to the publicly owned Leger Way depot in Doncaster. First Bus and Stagecoach will lease the acquired premises back during the transition. The authority intends to use the estate for franchised operations and electric-bus infrastructure, with its first franchised services scheduled for September 2027. Six operators reached the next procurement stage for the initial packages at Olive Grove and Leger Way.

This does not establish that every city should own every depot. London has long maintained a publicly specified, competitively operated bus network while many garages remain outside direct Transport for London ownership. Secure leases, access rights, assignable contracts and specialist infrastructure providers may preserve the same continuity, depending on the local market. The practical test is whether a qualified successor operator can obtain the depot, buses, charging capacity and operational information required to take over on the contracted date.

Remaining asset value is central to that test. If an operator finances twenty-year charging infrastructure under a five-year contract, it needs to know what happens to the investment if another operator wins the routes. The authority must also avoid paying for a useful electrical connection and then finding it cannot make that capacity available to the next contractor. Depreciation, compensation, asset transfer, warranties, software access and grid-connection permissions need to be settled before the first contract ends, ideally before it starts.

England’s January 2026 bus-franchising guidance recognizes the importance of depots, charging infrastructure, transfer arrangements and competition in subsequent procurements. The engineering of electric buses has advanced considerably. Public authorities now need procurement structures that keep the fleet and its supporting infrastructure useful as routes, contractors and technologies evolve.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing article examines the second-contract test through Greater Manchester and South Yorkshire, including asset access, residual values and operating continuity. It draws on my open report, The Bus Is Not the System, comparing metropolitan bus governance and electrification across Britain and Ireland.

#5883. Faropoint building 1st of 16 New Jersey community solar projects

Faropoint, an industrial real estate manager, has started construction on a 1.6-MW community solar installation on the roof of its building in Fair Lawn, New Jersey. The installation is the first of 16 planned Faropoint community solar projects in the state, which will have a total capacity of more than 10 MW.

Solar contractors install the first solar modules on the first solar project in Faropoint’s 16-project portfolio in New Jersey. Credit: Faropoint

The other 15 projects, totaling approximately 8.5 MW, have been accepted into New Jersey’s Community Solar Energy Program. Nine of them have received a notice to proceed, allowing construction to begin.

“We aim to create positive community impact, increase access to clean energy and become a responsible long-term owner in the markets where we operate,” said Brian Nave, VP of asset management and renewable energy at Faropoint. “As one of the top-five small-bay industrial owners in New Jersey, we believe our scale comes with a responsibility to drive meaningful sustainability initiatives. Utilizing previously unused portions of our buildings allows us to support the communities where we invest.”

The 16 projects are expected to generate approximately 12.7 million kWh of electricity a year, which is about the annual electricity use of more than 2,000 homes.

“Managing solar across a large real estate portfolio takes persistence, coordination, and a lot of work behind the scenes,” said Yaniv Kalish, CEO of SolarKal. “Seeing the first of Faropoint’s New Jersey community solar projects move into construction is a major milestone. And with nine more projects cleared to proceed and a half dozen more behind them, that’s real progress for Faropoint and the communities these projects will serve.”

SolarKal, Faropoint’s independent solar advisor, has advised the firm on dozens of projects across its portfolio, providing financial analysis, competitive procurement, developer evaluation and selection and ongoing project management. SolarKal also helped Faropoint select developers able to use available federal clean energy incentives before they are phased out.

Faropoint and SolarKal are also working on community solar projects in Illinois, New York and Maryland.
